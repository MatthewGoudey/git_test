# Prompt: A Field Guide to Humanity, Written for an Alien

> Copy everything below the line into an LLM (or hand it to a writer). It is written to be used as one prompt, but it also works in stages; see **Working in stages** at the end.

---

## Role and premise

You are a careful, neutral scholar writing a briefing about humans for an intelligent alien. The alien:

- is intelligent, literate, and can follow abstract reasoning;
- knows nothing about Earth, human biology, human history, or human culture;
- shares no assumptions with us. It does not know what "money," "a nation," "a god," or "a family" is;
- wants to understand **how humans actually live, what they have actually learned, and what actually shapes their societies**. It does not want to be persuaded of anything.

Your job has two parts:

1. **Build a map of human knowledge**: a structured, expansive outline of everything humans know and do.
2. **Write a basic explainer** for the major topics on that map.

## Core principle: concrete reality first

Focus on **concrete things and well-established truths** that measurably shape human life and society: economics, politics, law, technology, health, demographics, energy, food, war, and so on. Weight your coverage by real-world impact. A topic that affects billions of lives (agriculture, money, states, disease) gets more depth than one that affects few.

### Epistemic rules (follow these strictly)

1. **Separate the kinds of claims.** Label or phrase every significant claim so the reader knows which kind it is:
   - **Established fact**: strong evidence and broad expert consensus (e.g., "Earth orbits the Sun"; "smallpox was eradicated in 1980").
   - **Strong evidence, still refined**: well supported but with details under study (e.g., the specific mechanisms of many diseases).
   - **Contested or uncertain**: experts disagree, or the evidence is weak (e.g., the effects of particular economic policies, the origins of language).
   - **Belief, value, or ideology**: claims about what *ought* to be, or claims about the supernatural that cannot be tested. Describe these as things **humans believe**, never as things that **are true**.
2. **Beliefs are described, not endorsed.** Religion, spirituality, mythology, astrology, and similar topics are real and hugely important as **human phenomena**. Cover their history, institutions, texts, practices, number of followers, and social, political, economic, and psychological effects. Explain what people believe and why the belief matters to them. Do **not** state supernatural or spiritual claims as facts. Write "Christians believe Jesus rose from the dead," not "Jesus rose from the dead." Write "Many humans report experiences they describe as spiritual," not "Humans have souls."
3. **Ideologies get the same treatment.** For capitalism, socialism, liberalism, conservatism, nationalism, fascism, communism, and others, explain the core ideas, where they came from, where they have been tried, and the measurable outcomes. Report the evidence where there is evidence. Where outcomes are disputed, show the strongest version of each major view without picking a side.
4. **Quantify when you can.** Prefer numbers, dates, and scale ("about 8.2 billion humans," "roughly 4 billion people live in cities") over vague words. Give approximate dates and say what time period a figure describes.
5. **Say what humans don't know.** Each domain should name its biggest open questions.
6. **No hidden values.** Don't call cultures, eras, or peoples "primitive," "advanced," "good," or "evil." Describe what happened and its effects. Facts about atrocities, inequality, and harm should be stated plainly. Neutral does not mean softened.
7. **Explain from zero.** Define every human concept the first time you use it, as if to a reader with no shared context. Use analogies only when they help, and say when an analogy is imperfect.

## Part 1: The Map of Human Knowledge

Produce a hierarchical map with **three levels**: **Domain → Field → Key topics**. Aim for roughly 12–16 domains, each with 4–10 fields. It should be expansive. Use the skeleton below as a starting point and expand, merge, or add domains where it improves coverage:

1. **The Human Organism**: biology, anatomy, evolution and origins, genetics, the brain and cognition, emotions and behavior, sex and reproduction, the lifespan, health and disease.
2. **The Physical World as Humans Understand It**: physics, chemistry, astronomy and cosmology, Earth science, climate, ecology and other life on Earth.
3. **Methods of Knowing**: mathematics, logic, the scientific method, statistics, measurement, how humans tell truth from error (and how they fail to).
4. **Human History**: prehistory, the agricultural revolution, early civilizations, classical empires, the medieval and early modern periods, colonialism, the industrial revolution, world wars, decolonization, the Cold War, globalization, the present day.
5. **Economics and Material Life**: scarcity and trade, money and banking, markets, labor, capital, firms, inequality, growth and development, trade between nations, financial crises, poverty, measures like GDP.
6. **Politics and Power**: the state, forms of government, democracy and authoritarianism, elections, political parties, bureaucracy, international relations, empires, war and peace, revolutions, terrorism, diplomacy, international institutions.
7. **Law and Justice**: legal systems, constitutions, rights, crime and punishment, courts, property, contracts, international law, human rights.
8. **Society and Social Structure**: family and kinship, gender, race and ethnicity, class, cities and migration, demography, social movements, institutions, social norms.
9. **Ideologies and Political Thought**: capitalism, socialism, communism, liberalism, conservatism, libertarianism, nationalism, fascism, anarchism, environmentalism, feminism, religious political movements. Cover origins, core claims, key thinkers, where each was applied, and documented results.
10. **Religion, Spirituality, and Belief**: what religion is, its probable origins (as studied by anthropology, psychology, and history), the major world religions (history, texts, practices, demographics, institutions, schisms), folk and indigenous religions, secularism and atheism, spirituality and ritual, religion's documented effects on politics, war, law, charity, art, and social cohesion. *All supernatural claims presented strictly as beliefs.*
11. **Philosophy and Ethics**: the major questions (what exists, what can be known, how to live), the main ethical frameworks, major traditions across the world, and how they shaped law and politics.
12. **Technology and Infrastructure**: tools, energy (fire to fossil fuels to nuclear and renewables), agriculture and food systems, transportation, communication, computing and the internet, artificial intelligence, manufacturing, weapons, medicine and public health.
13. **Language, Communication, and Information**: what language is, writing systems, major language families, mass media, propaganda, education, literacy, the internet and social media.
14. **Culture and the Arts**: literature, music, visual art, architecture, film, games, sport, food cultures, fashion. Cover what they are, why humans make them, and their economic and social role.
15. **Mind, Psychology, and Behavior**: cognition, cognitive biases, motivation, personality, mental illness, cooperation and conflict, how individual behavior adds up to social outcomes.
16. **Global Challenges and the Future**: climate change, biodiversity loss, pandemics, nuclear risk, inequality, aging populations, AI, resource limits, and the open questions humans are actively working on.

For each domain in the map, also list:
- **Why it matters to understanding humans** (1–2 sentences);
- **Key concepts** (5–15 terms);
- **Landmark works**: the most influential books, documents, or texts (e.g., *The Wealth of Nations*, *On the Origin of Species*, the U.S. Constitution, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the Quran, the Bible, *The Communist Manifesto*), with author and year where it applies;
- **Major figures** (only when they really changed the field);
- **Connections** to other domains (e.g., "Religion ↔ Politics: divine right of kings, theocracies, religious parties").

## Part 2: The Explainers

For every **Field** on the map (or, if space is limited, the most important fields in each domain, ranked by real-world impact), write an explainer using this template:

```
### [Field name]

**In one sentence:** What this is, in plain terms.

**What an alien needs to know first:** Background concepts, defined from zero.

**How it works:** The core mechanics, the concrete, established understanding.
   Include numbers, scale, and examples.

**How it developed:** A short history. Key turning points, with dates.

**Effects on human life and society:** Measurable, documented impacts.
   Who it affects, how many, and how much.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** The main competing views, each stated
   fairly, with the evidence for and against where evidence exists.

**Key works and figures:** 3–7 items, each with one line on why it matters.

**What is established vs. debated:**
   - Established: ...
   - Debated: ...
   - Matters of belief or value: ...

**Open questions:** What humans don't yet know.

**See also:** Links to related fields on the map.
```

Explainers should be about 300–800 words each. Use more for high-impact fields (economics, government, health, energy, major religions as social phenomena) and less for narrow ones.

## Output format

Produce a single readable document in Markdown:

1. **Title and a short preface to the alien**: who humans are in one paragraph, plus a note on how to read the claim labels.
2. **"Humans at a Glance"**: a one-page fact sheet: population, lifespan, where they live, how they get food and energy, how they organize, what they believe, major languages, the state of the planet. Put a date on the figures.
3. **The Map**: the full hierarchical outline (Part 1). Add a simple text diagram of how the domains connect, if useful.
4. **The Explainers**: grouped by domain, in map order (Part 2).
5. **Glossary**: every human-specific term used, defined briefly.
6. **Recommended Reading Path**: an ordered list of 20–40 works an alien should read to understand humanity, from foundational to advanced, covering science, history, economics, politics, and the major religious and philosophical texts (read as cultural artifacts).
7. **A Note on Bias**: a short, honest section on the limits of this document: whose perspective dominates the historical record, which regions or peoples are under-documented, and where the author's own framing may shape the account.

## Style

- Clear, plain, and direct. Write for a smart reader with no context, not for specialists.
- Short paragraphs, informative headings, and tables where comparison helps (e.g., religions by followers, origin, and core practice; political systems by how power is gained and checked).
- Calm and neutral, with no moralizing and no cheerleading. Curiosity is fine.
- Prefer showing over asserting: give the example, the number, the date.

## Working in stages

If the whole document won't fit in one response, work in this order and stop after each stage so it can be continued:

1. Preface, "Humans at a Glance," and the complete Map (Part 1).
2. Explainers for domains 1–4.
3. Explainers for domains 5–9 (economics, politics, law, society, ideologies; give these the most depth).
4. Explainers for domains 10–16.
5. Glossary, Reading Path, and Note on Bias.

Begin with Stage 1.
