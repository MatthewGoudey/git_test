# Domain 14: Culture and the Arts

Humans do not only survive. They also tell stories, sing, decorate, dance, play, cook in elaborate ways, and follow thousands of shared habits that have no obvious survival purpose. This domain describes what those activities are, how they are passed on, how they differ across the roughly 8.2 billion humans and their thousands of distinct groups, and how much economic and social weight they carry. Judgments that a work is "great," "beautiful," or "important art" are human value judgments [Belief/Value]. This guide reports that humans make them, and which works many humans rank highly. It does not endorse those rankings.

---

### 14.1 What Culture Is

**In one sentence:** Culture is the body of learned behavior, knowledge, beliefs, and habits that a human group shares and passes on by teaching and imitation rather than through genes.

**What an alien needs to know first:** A **norm** is an unwritten rule about how to behave (for example, which hand to eat with). A **custom** is a habitual group practice. A **value** is a shared idea about what is good or important. Human infants are born able to learn any culture. A child born in Japan but raised in Brazil grows up culturally Brazilian.

**How it works:** Culture is transmitted by imitation, instruction, and language, from parents, peers, and institutions such as schools and religions (see 13.1, 8.7). Because learning is faster than genetic evolution, cultural traits can change within one generation. Humans copy selectively, favoring successful or high-status people and whatever most of their group does. There are several thousand distinct cultural groups, roughly tracking the ~7,000 languages. Large surveys such as the World Values Survey (since 1981, ~100 countries) find consistent differences in attitudes to religion, authority, gender roles, and individual choice. Some traits are near-universal: every known society has language, kinship rules, music, ritual, and rules about sex and food.

**How it developed:** Simple traditions (tool-making styles) exist in other primates and in human ancestors over a million years ago. Rapid cultural accumulation in *Homo sapiens* is clear by ~100,000–50,000 years ago (beads, pigments, varied tools) [Refining]. The scientific study of culture began in the 1800s.

**Effects on human life and society:** Culture shapes what people eat, whom they marry, how they raise children, and how they treat outsiders. Since ~1950, trade, migration, film, and the internet have spread some cultural products worldwide, such as American films, Japanese animation, Korean pop music, and Indian cinema. Governments treat cultural appeal as influence, which Joseph Nye called **soft power** (1990).

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Cultural relativism** (Franz Boas, early 1900s): describe each culture on its own terms. As a research method it is widely used. As a moral claim that no culture can be judged, it is [Belief/Value] and disputed.
- **Gene–culture coevolution:** culture and genes shape each other (e.g., adult milk digestion spread among dairy-herding peoples) [Refining].
- **Globalization:** whether global media makes cultures alike or produces local hybrids [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- Edward Tylor, *Primitive Culture* (1871): early scientific definition ("that complex whole...").
- Ruth Benedict, *Patterns of Culture* (1934): culture as a coherent pattern.
- Clifford Geertz, *The Interpretation of Cultures* (1973): culture as shared meanings.
- Joseph Henrich, *The Secret of Our Success* (2015): culture as the key to human success.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: culture is learned, not inherited genetically; it varies widely and changes over time.
- Debated: how far culture explains economic and political differences between countries; whether globalization flattens diversity.
- Matters of belief or value: whether any culture is better than another; cultural "appropriation" and who may use another group's traditions.

**Open questions:** Why did cumulative culture take off in humans and not in other species? How do individual choices aggregate into lasting cultural change?

**See also:** 1.1 Evolution and Human Origins; 13.1 Language; 8.1 Family and Kinship; 10.1 What Religion Is and Why Humans Have It; 15.6 Cooperation, Conflict, and Group Behavior.

### 14.2 Storytelling and Literature

**In one sentence:** Storytelling is the human practice of relating real or imagined sequences of events, and literature is storytelling and other artful language preserved in fixed (usually written) form.

**What an alien needs to know first:** A **myth** is a traditional story, often involving gods or ancestors, that a group uses to explain origins or customs. An **oral tradition** is knowledge passed by speech and memory. **Poetry** is language arranged by rhythm, sound, or line. **Prose** is ordinary sentence-based writing. A **novel** is a long fictional prose narrative. **Fiction** describes invented events.

**How it works:** Every known human society tells stories. Common functions include passing on practical and moral knowledge, recording genealogy and history, explaining the world, and entertainment [Refining as to which functions came first]. Recurring plot patterns (journey, conflict, return) appear across unrelated cultures. Writing (from ~3200 BCE; see 13.2) let long texts be fixed and copied, and printing (China from ~800 CE; Gutenberg's press in Europe ~1450) made them cheap. Global book publishing is worth roughly US$100–150 billion a year (estimates vary by method). Many cultures, including West African griots and Pacific Islander navigators' chants, kept long oral traditions without writing.

**How it developed:**
- ~2100–1200 BCE: the *Epic of Gilgamesh* (Mesopotamia), among the oldest surviving long literary works.
- ~1000 BCE–400 CE: the Hebrew Bible, the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, the Sanskrit *Mahabharata* and *Ramayana*, and the Chinese *Classic of Poetry* take shape.
- ~1010: *The Tale of Genji* (Murasaki Shikibu, Japan), often called an early novel.
- 1300s–1700s: *One Thousand and One Nights* compiled in Arabic; Dante, Shakespeare, Cervantes in Europe; classical Chinese novels such as *Journey to the West* (1592) and *Dream of the Red Chamber* (18th c.).
- 1800s–today: the novel spreads worldwide, with major 20th-century traditions in Africa, Latin America, and South Asia.

**Effects on human life and society:** Stories carry religious and national identity (see 10.2, 9.6). Some books changed politics, such as *Uncle Tom's Cabin* (1852) in the US slavery debate. Governments have long banned books (see 13.5). Fiction reading may modestly improve understanding of others' minds [Contested; replication is mixed].

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** The **canon** is the set of works a group treats as essential. Which works belong is [Belief/Value], and Western canons have been criticized for over-representing European men. Criticism schools include formalism (study the text itself), historicism (study its context), and reader-response theory (meaning lies in the reader).

**Key works and figures:** *Epic of Gilgamesh*; Homer's epics; the *Mahabharata*; *The Tale of Genji*; *Don Quixote* (Cervantes, 1605/1615); *Things Fall Apart* (Chinua Achebe, 1958); *One Hundred Years of Solitude* (Gabriel García Márquez, 1967).

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: storytelling is universal; writing and printing transformed its reach.
- Debated: the evolutionary purpose of fiction; measurable effects of reading on empathy.
- Matters of belief or value: which works are "great."

**Open questions:** Why do humans invest so much in events they know are invented? How will AI-generated text change authorship?

**See also:** 13.1 Language; 13.2 Writing and Literacy; 10.2 The Abrahamic Religions; 4.2 Farming and the First Civilizations; 14.5 Performance, Film, and Games.

### 14.3 Music

**In one sentence:** Music is organized sound, usually built from pitch, rhythm, and repetition, that humans make and listen to for emotional, social, and ritual purposes.

**What an alien needs to know first:** Humans hear air-pressure vibrations between about 20 and 20,000 cycles per second. **Pitch** is how high or low a sound is. A **scale** is a set of pitches a tradition uses (many use 5 or 7 per octave, the doubling of a pitch's frequency). **Rhythm** is the pattern of sounds in time, often around a steady **beat**. **Harmony** is pitches sounded together.

**How it works:** Music appears in every studied human society. A 2019 study of 315 cultures found song in all of them, and that lullabies, dance songs, healing songs, and love songs share recognizable features across cultures [Refining]. Music triggers the brain's reward system (see 15.2) and helps groups move and feel together. Traditions differ in structure. Indian classical music uses *raga* (melodic frameworks) and *tala* (rhythmic cycles). Indonesian gamelan uses tuned percussion and different scale tunings. Much West and Central African music layers several rhythms at once. European classical and today's global pop use a 12-pitch system with harmony based on chords.

**How it developed:** Bone flutes from Germany date to ~40,000 years ago. Singing and drumming are almost certainly older but leave no trace [Refining]. Systems of musical notation appeared in Mesopotamia (~1400 BCE), China, India, Greece, and medieval Europe. Recording began with Edison's phonograph (1877). Radio (1920s), vinyl records, cassettes, and CDs (1982) followed, then digital files and, from ~2008, streaming services.

**Effects on human life and society:** Music marks religious ritual, weddings, funerals, and national events (every country has an anthem). Recorded-music revenue was roughly US$28–30 billion in 2024, about two-thirds from streaming. Live concerts add a comparable or larger amount. Hundreds of millions of people pay for streaming subscriptions. Music has served protest movements (South African anti-apartheid songs, US civil-rights songs) and has also been censored or banned by states and religious authorities.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** Theories of why music evolved include: bonding groups, courtship display, and parent–infant communication. A minority view holds that music is a by-product of language and hearing, with no evolved purpose of its own [all Contested]. Ideas of musical "quality" are [Belief/Value].

**Key works and figures:** the *Natyashastra* (India, ~200 BCE–200 CE), early theory of music and performance; Bach and Beethoven, central to the European classical repertoire; Umm Kulthum (Egypt, 20th c.), among the most-heard Arab singers; Fela Kuti (Nigeria), creator of Afrobeat; The Beatles (1960s), who spread global pop; Mehr et al. (2019), the cross-cultural song study.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: music is universal and engages emotion and reward circuits.
- Debated: its evolutionary origin; how much musical perception is innate versus learned.
- Matters of belief or value: which music is "good."

**Open questions:** Why does organized sound produce strong emotion? Did music come before language, after it, or alongside it?

**See also:** 15.2 Emotion and Motivation; 1.4 The Brain and Nervous System; 10.5 Spirituality, Ritual, and Religious Experience; 14.5 Performance, Film, and Games.

### 14.4 Visual Arts and Architecture

**In one sentence:** Visual art is the making of images and objects mainly to be looked at, and architecture is the design of buildings and spaces, which serves both practical and symbolic purposes.

**What an alien needs to know first:** Human vision is the dominant human sense and detects a narrow band of light (see 1.3). Visual art includes **painting**, **sculpture** (three-dimensional forms), **drawing**, **calligraphy** (artful writing), **textiles**, **pottery**, **photography**, and **digital art**. A **style** is a recognizable set of visual conventions tied to a place and time.

**How it works:** Art objects carry meaning by depicting things, by symbolism, by decoration, or by abstraction (form and color without depicting anything). Many traditions did not separate "art" from craft, religion, or status display. Islamic religious art, for example, often favors calligraphy and geometric pattern. Architecture combines engineering (materials, load, climate) with signals of power, faith, or identity, and monumental buildings take vast labor. The Great Pyramid at Giza (~2560 BCE) used roughly 2.3 million stone blocks. The global art market (sales of fine art and antiques) is roughly US$55–65 billion a year, concentrated in the US, China, and the UK.

**How it developed:**
- ≥100,000–75,000 years ago: engraved ochre and shell beads in southern Africa.
- ~45,000–50,000+ years ago: cave paintings in Sulawesi (Indonesia), among the oldest known figurative art [Refining]. Chauvet (~36,000 years) and Lascaux (~17,000 years) in France.
- ~9500 BCE: Göbekli Tepe (Turkey), carved stone enclosures built before farming was fully established.
- Ancient to early modern: traditions developed in Egypt, China, India, Mesoamerica, West Africa (Ife and Benin bronzes), the Islamic world, and Europe.
- 1839: photography. Late 1800s–1900s: abstraction and modernism. 2000s: digital art and AI image tools (from ~2022).

**Effects on human life and society:** Art and architecture record history and religion, display power, and draw tourism. Some heritage sites receive millions of visitors yearly. UNESCO lists over 1,200 World Heritage sites. Art has been looted in war and colonial conquest. Return of such objects (e.g., the Benin Bronzes) is an active political issue.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** Theories of why humans make art include: costly signaling of skill, social bonding, ritual use, and by-product of pattern-seeking [Contested]. Aesthetics (the philosophy of beauty, see 11.1) asks whether beauty is objective or in the viewer [Belief/Value].

**Key works and figures:** the Sulawesi and Chauvet cave paintings; the Pyramids of Giza; the Ajanta caves (India, ~200 BCE–500 CE); the Forbidden City (Beijing, 1420); Leonardo da Vinci's *Mona Lisa* (~1503–1519), probably the world's most-viewed painting; Katsushika Hokusai's *Great Wave* (~1831), reproduced worldwide.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: image-making is at least ~45,000 years old and found worldwide.
- Debated: whether Neanderthals made art; the origin and function of art.
- Matters of belief or value: what counts as "art" and what is "beautiful."

**Open questions:** What were the earliest images for? How will AI image generation affect artists' livelihoods and the meaning of authorship?

**See also:** 4.1 Prehistory; 10.7 Religion's Effects on Society; 8.6 Cities and Settlement; 11.1 What Philosophy Is; 16.5 Artificial Intelligence.

### 14.5 Performance, Film, and Games

**In one sentence:** Performance arts (theater, dance) present stories or movement to an audience live, while film, television, and video games deliver recorded or interactive entertainment through screens.

**What an alien needs to know first:** **Theater** is acted storytelling on a stage. **Dance** is patterned body movement, usually to music. **Film** (cinema) is a rapid sequence of still images shown fast enough (~24 per second) that human eyes perceive motion. **Television** brings moving images into homes. A **video game** is an interactive program in which players' choices change what happens on screen.

**How it works:** Live performance relies on shared presence. Recorded media rely on cheap copying, so one work can reach hundreds of millions. The US (Hollywood), India (with the largest number of films per year, roughly 1,500–2,000), Nigeria ("Nollywood," very high volume), China, and South Korea are major centers. Global cinema box office was roughly US$30–35 billion in 2024, below its 2019 peak of ~$42 billion. Video games are the largest of these industries, with roughly US$180–190 billion a year in revenue and roughly 3 billion players, mostly on mobile phones.

**How it developed:** Ritual dance is likely as old as music. Formal theater emerged in Athens (5th c. BCE tragedy and comedy) and in India (Sanskrit drama, codified in the *Natyashastra*). Later traditions include Japanese Noh (14th c.) and kabuki (17th c.), Chinese opera (Beijing opera ~late 1700s), and Indonesian shadow puppetry (*wayang*). Film began in the 1890s (the Lumière brothers' public screening, 1895), sound film arrived in 1927, and television spread from the 1950s. Video games began in the 1960s–70s (*Pong*, 1972). Streaming services grew from ~2007.

**Effects on human life and society:** Screen media take up several hours of a typical person's day in many countries. Film and TV spread language and habits across borders and are used for soft power and propaganda (see 13.5). Most research finds small or no effects of violent games on real-world violence [Contested]. "Gaming disorder" is a diagnosis in the WHO's ICD-11 (2019) [Contested among researchers].

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** Debates concern whether mass entertainment homogenizes culture or diversifies it, whether games are an art form [Belief/Value], and how much screen time harms children [Contested].

**Key works and figures:** Aeschylus and Sophocles (Athenian tragedians, 5th c. BCE); Zeami Motokiyo (shaped Noh theater, ~1400); Shakespeare (plays still widely performed worldwide); the Lumière brothers (first public film shows); Satyajit Ray (Indian filmmaker, 1950s–80s); Shigeru Miyamoto (Japanese game designer, *Super Mario*, 1985).

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: these are very large industries; performance traditions exist worldwide.
- Debated: media effects on aggression, attention, and well-being.
- Matters of belief or value: artistic merit; whether games are art.

**Open questions:** How will AI-generated video and interactive media change these industries? What are the long-term effects of very heavy screen use?

**See also:** 13.3 Media and Mass Communication; 13.4 The Internet and Social Media; 14.3 Music; 15.5 Mental Health and Illness; 12.6 Computing, the Internet, and Artificial Intelligence.

### 14.6 Sport and Play

**In one sentence:** Play is activity done for its own sake, and sport is rule-governed physical competition, often organized into leagues and watched by large audiences.

**What an alien needs to know first:** Many young mammals play, which seems to build physical and social skills. Human **games** have explicit rules and a way to win. A **sport** is a game centered on physical skill. **Professional** athletes are paid. A **team** is a group of players competing together, usually representing a city, club, or country.

**How it works:** Organized sport runs on governing bodies that set rules (e.g., FIFA for association football, called "soccer" in some countries), competitions, broadcasting rights, sponsorship, and fans. Estimates of the global sports industry range widely, roughly US$400–600 billion a year. Association football is the most-followed sport worldwide, with billions of fans. Cricket is followed by hundreds of millions, mainly in South Asia. Other major sports differ by region: basketball, American football, baseball, rugby, Gaelic games, sumo, and kabaddi. Large events attract enormous audiences. FIFA reports that the 2022 World Cup final was watched by around 1.5 billion people (FIFA's own estimate).

**How it developed:** Ball games are recorded in ancient China (*cuju*, ~3rd c. BCE), Mesoamerica (the rubber-ball game, from ~1600 BCE), and elsewhere. Wrestling is depicted in Egyptian and Mesopotamian art. The ancient Olympic Games in Greece traditionally began in 776 BCE. Many modern sports were codified with written rules in 1800s Britain and the US and spread through empire and trade. The modern Olympics began in 1896. The Paris 2024 Games had ~10,500 athletes from over 200 national teams. Women's professional sport grew sharply from the 2010s.

**Effects on human life and society:** Sport is tied to national identity and politics. Boycotts (e.g., 1980 and 1984 Olympics), bans (apartheid South Africa, roughly 1964–1992), and hosting events for prestige are common. Governments spend heavily on stadiums, though most studies find small or negative economic returns to host cities [Contested in detail]. Concerns include doping, match-fixing, betting addiction, and brain injury (e.g., chronic traumatic encephalopathy in contact sports).

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** Johan Huizinga argued that play is older than culture and underlies it (*Homo Ludens*, 1938) [Contested]. Debates include amateurism versus professionalism, and whether sport reduces or channels conflict between groups [Contested].

**Key works and figures:** Huizinga, *Homo Ludens* (1938); Pierre de Coubertin (founded the modern Olympics); the FIFA World Cup (since 1930); Jesse Owens (1936 Berlin Olympics); Pelé (global football star, 1950s–70s).

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: play is widespread among mammals; sport is a large global industry.
- Debated: the economic value of hosting mega-events; whether sport builds social cohesion.
- Matters of belief or value: fairness rules (e.g., eligibility categories in sex-segregated sport).

**Open questions:** Why do humans care intensely about the results of games they do not play? How should sport handle genetic enhancement and doping?

**See also:** 1.3 The Body and Its Needs; 9.6 Nationalism; 6.5 International Relations and Diplomacy; 15.6 Cooperation, Conflict, and Group Behavior.

### 14.7 Food and Everyday Customs

**In one sentence:** Cuisines, dress, greetings, holidays, and calendars are the everyday shared practices that structure daily life and mark group identity.

**What an alien needs to know first:** A **cuisine** is a group's characteristic way of choosing, preparing, and eating food. A **taboo** is a strong prohibition, often religious or moral. A **holiday** is a day set aside for celebration or remembrance. A **calendar** is a system for dividing time into days, months, and years, based on the Sun, the Moon, or both.

**How it works:** Cuisines are built from local crops and animals, trade, religion, and technology. Rice is central in much of Asia, wheat in Europe, the Middle East, and North Africa, maize in Mesoamerica, and cassava and sorghum in parts of Africa (see 12.2). Cooking is universal among humans. Religious food rules affect billions: Judaism and Islam forbid pork, many Hindus avoid beef, and Jains avoid harming even small creatures. Dress signals gender, status, religion, and occupation. The global clothing industry is roughly US$1.5–2 trillion a year. Greetings vary (bows, handshakes, cheek kisses). Most of the world uses the solar **Gregorian calendar** (introduced 1582) for business, while also keeping others. The Islamic calendar is lunar (~354 days a year). The Chinese and Hebrew calendars are lunisolar (lunar months adjusted to the solar year).

**How it developed:** Cooking with fire dates back at least several hundred thousand years [earliest dates Contested]. Farming (from ~10,000 BCE) narrowed diets onto a few staple crops. The "Columbian Exchange" after 1492 moved potatoes, maize, and chili peppers from the Americas to Afro-Eurasia, and wheat, cattle, and sugar the other way. Refrigeration, industrial processing, and global supply chains (1900s) spread processed foods and fast-food chains worldwide.

**Effects on human life and society:** Poor diet is among the top risk factors for death worldwide (see 1.6). Food customs mark ethnic and religious belonging, and migrants often keep cuisines longest. Holidays drive economies: around China's Spring Festival (Lunar New Year), people make billions of trips, the largest annual human migration. Calendars set work weeks and religious observance.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** Explanations of food taboos include health, ecology (Marvin Harris argued India's sacred cattle were more valuable alive), and symbolic group-marking (Mary Douglas) [all Contested]. Ethical diets (vegetarianism, veganism) rest on [Belief/Value] claims about animals, plus testable claims about health and environment.

**Key works and figures:** Mary Douglas, *Purity and Danger* (1966), on taboo; Marvin Harris, *Good to Eat* (1985), on materialist explanations of food rules; Alfred Crosby, *The Columbian Exchange* (1972); Richard Wrangham, *Catching Fire* (2009), on cooking in human evolution [Contested]; UNESCO's intangible heritage list, which includes cuisines such as Japanese *washoku* and Mexican cuisine.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: diet varies widely by culture; religious food rules affect billions; the Gregorian calendar is the global standard for commerce.
- Debated: the origins of specific taboos; when cooking began.
- Matters of belief or value: what is proper, polite, or ethical to eat and wear.

**Open questions:** How will rising meat consumption, climate change, and lab-grown foods reshape cuisines? Will global brands keep displacing local customs, or will local foods hold?

**See also:** 12.2 Agriculture and Food Systems; 10.5 Spirituality, Ritual, and Religious Experience; 1.6 Health, Disease, and Medicine; 8.5 Population and Migration; 16.7 Resources: Food, Water, and Materials.
