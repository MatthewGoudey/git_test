# Domain 13: Language, Communication, and Information

Humans coordinate almost everything they do by exchanging symbols: spoken words, written marks, images, and now digital signals. This domain explains what human language is, how writing turned speech into a durable record, how a few senders came to reach millions through mass media, how the internet let billions broadcast to one another, and how all of these channels are used to persuade and mislead. Control over these channels is a major source of political and economic power.

---

### 13.1 Language

**In one sentence:** Human language is a system for combining a limited set of sounds (or hand signs) into an unlimited number of meaningful messages, and every human group has one.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- Humans make sounds by pushing air from the lungs past vibrating folds in the throat and shaping it with the tongue and lips. A **phoneme** is one of the distinct sounds a language uses to tell words apart (English has about 44; languages range from roughly a dozen to over 100).
- **Grammar** is the set of rules for combining sounds into words and words into sentences. **Syntax** is the part governing word order and sentence structure. **Semantics** is meaning.
- A **language** is a system shared by a community. A **dialect** is a regional or social variety of one. The line between "language" and "dialect" is often political, not linguistic.
- **Sign languages** use hands, face, and body instead of sound. They are full languages with their own grammars, not gestures for spoken words. There are roughly 200–300 of them.
- A **language family** is a group of languages descended from one ancestor, the way Spanish, French, and Italian descend from Latin.

**How it works:** Human language differs from other animal communication in several ways. It is **compositional** (small units combine into larger meanings), **recursive** (sentences can be nested inside sentences without limit), and **displaced** (it can describe things that are absent, past, future, or imaginary). Children learn their first language without formal teaching. Most master the core grammar by **age 4–5**, if they hear enough language early in life.

There are about **7,000 living languages**, but they are very unevenly used. About **half of humanity** speaks one of roughly 20–25 languages as a first language. Most languages have under 10,000 speakers.

| Language | First-language speakers (approx.) | Total speakers incl. second language (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| English | ~380 million | ~1.5 billion |
| Mandarin Chinese | ~950 million | ~1.1–1.2 billion |
| Hindi | ~350 million | ~600 million |
| Spanish | ~480 million | ~550–600 million |
| Arabic (all varieties) | ~350–400 million | ~400 million+ |
| French | ~80 million | ~300 million |

The largest families by speakers are **Indo-European** (about 45% of humans, including English, Hindi, Spanish, Russian, Persian), **Sino-Tibetan** (~1.4 billion), **Niger–Congo** (~1,500 languages across Africa), **Afroasiatic** (Arabic, Hebrew, Amharic, Hausa), **Austronesian** (~1,200 languages from Madagascar to Hawaii), and **Dravidian** (South India). Some languages, such as Basque and Korean, have no proven relatives [Refining for some groupings].

About **40%** of languages are considered **endangered**: children are no longer learning them. Linguists estimate a language falls silent every few months on average [Refining].

**How it developed:** When language began is [Contested]. Estimates range from about 50,000 to over 200,000 years ago, or earlier if older human species had language. Speech leaves no fossils, so evidence is indirect: throat anatomy, genes (such as *FOXP2*, involved in speech), and the appearance of symbolic objects. Languages change continuously. Latin split into the Romance languages over about 1,500 years. Linguists have reconstructed parts of ancestral languages, such as **Proto-Indo-European** (spoken roughly 6,000 years ago), by comparing their descendants.

**Effects on human life and society:** Language allows teaching, planning, trade, law, religion, and storytelling. It is a strong marker of group identity. Many states have imposed a national language, sometimes suppressing minority languages through schools. Language differences shape trade, migration, and conflict. English became the main global language of science, business, aviation, and the internet in the 20th century, largely because of British and then American power.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Universal grammar** (Noam Chomsky, from the 1950s): humans have an inborn, language-specific mental capacity that constrains all languages. **Usage-based** and **construction** approaches argue that language is learned by general learning abilities from heard patterns. This debate is [Contested]; recent AI language models, which learn grammar from text alone, have added fuel to it.
- **Linguistic relativity** (the "Sapir–Whorf hypothesis"): the language you speak shapes how you think. The strong version (language *determines* thought) is generally rejected. Weaker effects, for example on color naming or describing directions, have some experimental support [Refining].
- **Prescriptivism vs. descriptivism:** whether linguists should state how people *should* speak or describe how they *do*. Modern linguistics is descriptive. Ideas of "correct" language are social norms [Belief/Value].

**Key works and figures:**
- Pāṇini, *Aṣṭādhyāyī* (~4th century BCE): a precise grammar of Sanskrit, among the earliest scientific descriptions of a language.
- William Jones (1786): proposed that Sanskrit, Greek, and Latin share an ancestor, founding comparative linguistics.
- Ferdinand de Saussure, *Course in General Linguistics* (1916): language as a system of signs.
- Noam Chomsky, *Syntactic Structures* (1957): made grammar a formal, mental science.
- Steven Pinker, *The Language Instinct* (1994): popular case that language is an evolved human ability.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: all human groups have full, complex languages; children acquire language rapidly and without instruction; languages descend from ancestors and change over time; sign languages are full languages.
- Debated: when and how language evolved; how much grammar is inborn; how much language shapes thought.
- Matters of belief or value: whether endangered languages should be revived; which language(s) a state should use officially.

**Open questions:** How did language first arise? What exactly in human brains and genes makes it possible? Can endangered languages be saved at scale? What do AI language models reveal about how language works?

### 13.2 Writing and Literacy

**In one sentence:** Writing is a system of visible marks that records language, letting knowledge outlast its speaker; for most of history few people could read it, but today about 87% of adults can.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- A **script** (writing system) maps marks onto language. There are three main types:
  - **Logographic**: each sign stands for a word or meaningful part of a word (Chinese characters).
  - **Syllabic**: each sign stands for a syllable (Japanese kana).
  - **Alphabetic**: each sign stands roughly for one sound (Latin letters used for English; Arabic and Hebrew scripts mainly write consonants).
  Most real scripts mix these principles.
- **Literacy** is the ability to read and write. **Printing** is the mechanical copying of text.

**How it works:** Writing turns fleeting speech into a lasting object. That allows records (taxes, debts, laws), communication across distance and time, and the accumulation of knowledge beyond what any memory can hold. Learning to read rewires part of the brain's visual system to recognize letters and words, and usually takes years of schooling.

The most widely used scripts today are **Latin** (used by roughly 70% of the world's population in some form), **Chinese characters** (~1.4 billion people), **Arabic**, **Devanagari** (Hindi and others), and **Cyrillic** (Russian and others). The **Unicode** standard, which lets computers represent text, encodes **about 170 scripts** and over 150,000 characters.

| Era | Approximate world adult literacy |
|---|---|
| 1500 | Under 10% (higher in some cities and religious groups) |
| 1820 | ~12% |
| 1950 | ~45–55% |
| Mid-2020s | ~87% (~750 million adults cannot read; about two-thirds are women) |

**How it developed:**
- **~3200 BCE:** writing appears in **Mesopotamia** (cuneiform, wedge-shaped marks pressed into clay), first for accounting. Egyptian hieroglyphs appear around the same time.
- **~1200 BCE:** Chinese writing on "oracle bones" (inscribed bones used in divination). **~900–300 BCE:** writing in Mesoamerica. Writing was invented independently at least 3–4 times [Refining on the exact count].
- **~1800 BCE:** an early alphabet emerges among Semitic-speaking workers in Egypt and the Sinai, adapted from hieroglyphs. Its **Phoenician** descendant (~1050 BCE) spread around the Mediterranean; the **Greeks** added vowels (~800 BCE). Most alphabets today descend from this line.
- **~100 CE:** paper made in China (traditionally credited to Cai Lun, 105 CE). Woodblock printing in China by the 600s–700s; movable type in China (~1040) and metal movable type in Korea (by 1377).
- **~1450:** Johannes **Gutenberg** develops a printing press with metal movable type in Germany. Within 50 years, Europe printed an estimated 10–20 million books. Printing helped spread the Protestant Reformation (1517 onward) and the Scientific Revolution (see 4.4).
- **1800s–1900s:** states introduce compulsory schooling; mass literacy spreads in Europe, North America, and Japan, then worldwide after 1950.

**Effects on human life and society:** Writing enabled bureaucratic states, written law (the Code of Hammurabi, ~1754 BCE), scriptures, history, and science. For millennia, literacy was a skill of small elites (scribes, priests, officials), which concentrated power. Literacy is strongly linked to income, health, lower child mortality, and political participation; part of that link is causal [Refining on size]. Printing made texts cheap, standardized languages, and is widely thought to have helped break the control of religious and political authorities over information.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Orality vs. literacy:** scholars such as Walter Ong and Jack Goody argued that writing changes how societies think, favoring abstraction, lists, and fixed texts. Critics say these claims are overstated [Contested].
- **The "printing revolution":** Elizabeth Eisenstein argued printing was a major cause of the Reformation and Scientific Revolution. Others give it a supporting role among many causes [Contested on degree].
- **Script reform:** some states have changed scripts for political reasons, such as Turkey moving from Arabic to Latin letters (1928) and China simplifying characters (1950s).

**Key works and figures:**
- Cuneiform tablets of Uruk (~3200 BCE): among the oldest known writing, mostly accounts.
- Johannes Gutenberg and the Gutenberg Bible (~1455): start of mass printing in Europe.
- King Sejong of Korea: commissioned the Hangul alphabet (1443–46), designed to be easy to learn.
- Walter Ong, *Orality and Literacy* (1982): how writing changes thought and culture.
- Elizabeth Eisenstein, *The Printing Press as an Agent of Change* (1979).

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: writing was invented several times independently, first for record-keeping; mass literacy is less than 200 years old; literacy has risen sharply since 1950.
- Debated: how strongly writing and printing change thinking and cause social change.
- Matters of belief or value: the sacred status some cultures give to particular scripts and texts (see 10.2, 10.3).

**Open questions:** Will digital media and voice interfaces change how much humans read? How can the remaining ~750 million non-readers be reached? Several ancient scripts (e.g., the Indus script) are still undeciphered.

### 13.3 Media and Mass Communication

**In one sentence:** Mass media are technologies and organizations that let a small number of senders reach very large audiences at once, through newspapers, radio, and television.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Media** (singular: medium) are channels that carry messages. **Mass media** send one message to many receivers.
- **Journalism** is the gathering, checking, and publishing of information about current events. Its professional norms include accuracy, verification, and separating news from opinion. Practice varies.
- **Press freedom** is the ability of media to publish without government control or punishment. It is protected in law in many countries (e.g., Article 19 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948) and restricted in many others.
- Most commercial media earn money by **selling the attention of their audiences to advertisers**, not only by selling content.

**How it works:** Mass media follow three main ownership models: **commercial** (private companies funded by advertising and subscriptions), **public service** (publicly funded but formally independent, such as the UK's BBC, founded 1922), and **state-controlled** (run by the government, common in authoritarian states). Media decide which events become news (**gatekeeping**) and how they are presented (**framing**).

Television remains the most-used news source for many older people worldwide, but online sources now dominate among younger people. Traditional news businesses have shrunk sharply. In the United States, newspaper advertising revenue fell from about **US$50 billion in the mid-2000s to under US$10 billion** by the 2020s, and newsroom jobs roughly halved [Refining]. Surveys in many countries find that only about **40%** of people say they trust most news most of the time.

Press freedom varies widely. Press-freedom groups count roughly **300–500 journalists jailed** worldwide at any given time, with China, Israel, Myanmar, Belarus, and Russia among the largest jailers in recent counts [Refining; counts vary by organization and year].

**How it developed:**
- **~59 BCE:** Rome posts official daily notices (*Acta Diurna*). China circulated official court bulletins for centuries.
- **1605:** first printed weekly newspaper in Strasbourg (then in the Holy Roman Empire).
- **1830s–1890s:** cheap mass-circulation newspapers; the **telegraph** (1840s) sends news across continents in minutes; news agencies (e.g., Reuters, 1851) form.
- **1920s:** commercial and public **radio** broadcasting begins.
- **1930s–1950s:** **television** broadcasting begins and spreads into most homes in rich countries by the 1960s.
- **1980:** CNN launches 24-hour TV news. Satellite TV spreads globally in the 1990s (e.g., Al Jazeera, 1996).
- **2000s–2020s:** audiences and advertising shift online (13.4).

**Effects on human life and society:** Mass media created shared national conversations, national identities, and celebrity. They became central to elections, war, and propaganda (13.5). Radio and TV let leaders speak directly to millions. Independent journalism has exposed abuses of power, e.g., the **Watergate** reporting that led to a US president's resignation (1974). The decline of local news in some countries has been linked to lower civic participation and less oversight of local government [Refining].

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Media effects:** early theorists feared media could inject ideas directly into passive audiences. Research since the 1940s found more limited direct effects on opinions, but strong effects on **what people think about** (**agenda-setting**, McCombs & Shaw, 1972) and on how issues are understood (**framing**) [Established in general; size varies].
- **The public sphere** (Jürgen Habermas, 1962): democracy needs spaces where citizens debate as equals, and commercial media can erode them [Contested].
- **Propaganda model** (Herman & Chomsky, 1988): in market systems, ownership, advertising, and reliance on official sources filter news in favor of elites. Critics argue it underrates journalists' independence and audience choice [Contested].
- **Objectivity vs. advocacy:** whether journalists should aim for neutrality or openly take positions [Belief/Value]. Norms differ: Hallin and Mancini (2004) describe distinct media systems across Western countries.

**Key works and figures:**
- Walter Lippmann, *Public Opinion* (1922): people react to media-made "pictures in their heads," not the world directly.
- Marshall McLuhan, *Understanding Media* (1964): "the medium is the message," i.e., each technology reshapes society regardless of content.
- Jürgen Habermas, *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere* (1962).
- Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky, *Manufacturing Consent* (1988).
- Reporters Without Borders' World Press Freedom Index (annual since 2002): ranks countries by press freedom.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: media shape which issues people consider important; advertising-funded newspapers have declined sharply; press freedom varies greatly by country.
- Debated: how much media change opinions and votes; whether concentrated ownership biases coverage; how to fund journalism.
- Matters of belief or value: what counts as fair or balanced coverage; how much governments should fund or regulate media.

**Open questions:** What business model, if any, will sustain independent journalism? How will AI-generated content affect trust in news? Does the loss of shared national media increase political division?
