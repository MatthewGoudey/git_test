# Domain 13: Language, Communication, and Information

Humans coordinate almost everything they do by exchanging symbols: spoken words, written marks, images, and now digital signals. This domain explains what human language is, how writing turned speech into a durable record, how a few senders came to reach millions through mass media, how the internet let billions broadcast to one another, and how all of these channels are used to persuade and mislead. Control over these channels is a major source of political and economic power.

---

### 13.1 Language

**In one sentence:** Human language is a system for combining a limited set of sounds (or hand signs) into an unlimited number of meaningful messages, and every human group has one.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- Humans speak by pushing air past vibrating folds in the throat and shaping it with tongue and lips. A **phoneme** is one of the distinct sounds a language uses to tell words apart (English has about 44).
- **Grammar** is the set of rules for building words and sentences. **Syntax** governs sentence structure; **semantics** is meaning.
- A **dialect** is a regional or social variety of a language. Whether two varieties count as separate "languages" is often a political decision, not a linguistic one.
- **Sign languages** (roughly 200–300 of them) use hands, face, and body. They are full languages with their own grammars.
- A **language family** is a group of languages descended from one ancestor, as Spanish, French, and Italian descend from Latin.

**How it works:** Human language is **compositional** (small units combine into larger meanings), **recursive** (sentences can nest inside sentences without limit), and **displaced** (it can describe the absent, past, future, or imaginary). No other animal's communication is known to have all three. Children learn their first language without formal teaching and master its core grammar by **age 4–5**, provided they hear enough language early.

There are about **7,000 living languages**, but they are very unevenly used. About **half of humanity** speaks one of roughly 20–25 languages as a first language. Most languages have under 10,000 speakers.

| Language | First-language speakers (approx.) | Total speakers incl. second language (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| English | ~380 million | ~1.5 billion |
| Mandarin Chinese | ~950 million | ~1.1–1.2 billion |
| Hindi | ~350 million | ~600 million |
| Spanish | ~480 million | ~550–600 million |
| Arabic (all varieties) | ~350–400 million | ~400 million+ |
| French | ~80 million | ~300 million |

The largest families by speakers are **Indo-European** (about 45% of humans, including English, Hindi, Spanish, Russian, Persian), **Sino-Tibetan** (~1.4 billion), **Niger–Congo** (~1,500 languages across Africa), **Afroasiatic** (Arabic, Hebrew, Amharic, Hausa), **Austronesian** (~1,200 languages from Madagascar to Hawaii), and **Dravidian** (South India). Some languages, such as Basque and Korean, have no proven relatives [Refining for some groupings].

About **40%** of languages are considered **endangered**: children are no longer learning them. Linguists estimate a language falls silent every few months on average [Refining].

**How it developed:** When language began is [Contested]: estimates run from about 50,000 to over 200,000 years ago, or earlier if older human species spoke. Speech leaves no fossils, so evidence is indirect (throat anatomy, genes such as *FOXP2*, symbolic objects). Languages change continuously; Latin split into the Romance languages over about 1,500 years. By comparing descendants, linguists have partly reconstructed ancestors such as **Proto-Indo-European** (spoken roughly 6,000 years ago).

**Effects on human life and society:** Language makes teaching, planning, trade, law, and religion possible, and it is a strong marker of group identity. Many states imposed a national language, often suppressing minority languages through schools. English became the main global language of science, business, aviation, and the internet in the 20th century, largely because of British and then American power.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Universal grammar** (Noam Chomsky, from the 1950s): humans have an inborn, language-specific capacity that constrains all languages. **Usage-based** approaches argue that general learning abilities extract language from heard patterns. The debate is [Contested]; AI language models, which learn grammar from text alone, have added fuel to it.
- **Linguistic relativity** ("Sapir–Whorf hypothesis"): language shapes thought. The strong version (language *determines* thought) is generally rejected; weaker effects, e.g., on color naming or describing directions, have some support [Refining].
- **Prescriptivism vs. descriptivism:** modern linguistics describes how people *do* speak; ideas of "correct" speech are social norms [Belief/Value].

**Key works and figures:**
- Pāṇini, *Aṣṭādhyāyī* (~4th century BCE): a precise grammar of Sanskrit, among the earliest scientific descriptions of a language.
- William Jones (1786): proposed that Sanskrit, Greek, and Latin share an ancestor, founding comparative linguistics.
- Ferdinand de Saussure, *Course in General Linguistics* (1916): language as a system of signs.
- Noam Chomsky, *Syntactic Structures* (1957): made grammar a formal, mental science.
- Steven Pinker, *The Language Instinct* (1994): popular case that language is an evolved human ability.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: all human groups have full, complex languages; children acquire language rapidly and without instruction; languages descend from ancestors and change over time; sign languages are full languages.
- Debated: when and how language evolved; how much grammar is inborn; how much language shapes thought.
- Matters of belief or value: whether endangered languages should be revived; which language(s) a state should use officially.

**Open questions:** How did language first arise? What exactly in human brains and genes makes it possible? Can endangered languages be saved at scale? What do AI language models reveal about how language works?

### 13.2 Writing and Literacy

**In one sentence:** Writing is a system of visible marks that records language, letting knowledge outlast its speaker; for most of history few people could read it, but today about 87% of adults can.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- A **script** (writing system) maps marks onto language. There are three main types:
  - **Logographic**: each sign stands for a word or meaningful part of a word (Chinese characters).
  - **Syllabic**: each sign stands for a syllable (Japanese kana).
  - **Alphabetic**: each sign stands roughly for one sound (Latin letters used for English; Arabic and Hebrew scripts mainly write consonants).
  Most real scripts mix these principles.
- **Literacy** is the ability to read and write. **Printing** is the mechanical copying of text.

**How it works:** Writing turns fleeting speech into a lasting object. That allows records (taxes, debts, laws), communication across distance and time, and the accumulation of knowledge beyond what any memory can hold. Learning to read rewires part of the brain's visual system to recognize letters and words, and usually takes years of schooling.

The most widely used scripts today are **Latin** (used by roughly 70% of the world's population in some form), **Chinese characters** (~1.4 billion people), **Arabic**, **Devanagari** (Hindi and others), and **Cyrillic** (Russian and others). The **Unicode** standard, which lets computers represent text, encodes **about 170 scripts** and over 150,000 characters.

| Era | Approximate world adult literacy |
|---|---|
| 1500 | Under 10% (higher in some cities and religious groups) |
| 1820 | ~12% |
| 1950 | ~45–55% |
| Mid-2020s | ~87% (~750 million adults cannot read; about two-thirds are women) |

**How it developed:**
- **~3200 BCE:** writing appears in **Mesopotamia** (cuneiform, wedge-shaped marks pressed into clay), first for accounting. Egyptian hieroglyphs appear around the same time.
- **~1200 BCE:** Chinese writing on "oracle bones" (inscribed bones used in divination). **~900–300 BCE:** writing in Mesoamerica. Writing was invented independently at least 3–4 times [Refining on the exact count].
- **~1800 BCE:** an early alphabet emerges among Semitic-speaking workers in Egypt and the Sinai, adapted from hieroglyphs. Its **Phoenician** descendant (~1050 BCE) spread around the Mediterranean; the **Greeks** added vowels (~800 BCE). Most alphabets today descend from this line.
- **~100 CE:** paper made in China (traditionally credited to Cai Lun, 105 CE). Woodblock printing in China by the 600s–700s; movable type in China (~1040) and metal movable type in Korea (by 1377).
- **~1450:** Johannes **Gutenberg** develops a printing press with metal movable type in Germany. Within 50 years, Europe printed an estimated 10–20 million books. Printing helped spread the Protestant Reformation (1517 onward) and the Scientific Revolution (see 4.4).
- **1800s–1900s:** states introduce compulsory schooling; mass literacy spreads in Europe, North America, and Japan, then worldwide after 1950.

**Effects on human life and society:** Writing enabled bureaucratic states, written law (the Code of Hammurabi, ~1754 BCE), scriptures, history, and science. For millennia, literacy was a skill of small elites (scribes, priests, officials), which concentrated power. Literacy is strongly linked to income, health, lower child mortality, and political participation; part of that link is causal [Refining on size]. Printing made texts cheap, standardized languages, and is widely thought to have helped break the control of religious and political authorities over information.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Orality vs. literacy:** scholars such as Walter Ong and Jack Goody argued that writing changes how societies think, favoring abstraction, lists, and fixed texts. Critics say these claims are overstated [Contested].
- **The "printing revolution":** Elizabeth Eisenstein argued printing was a major cause of the Reformation and Scientific Revolution. Others give it a supporting role among many causes [Contested on degree].
- **Script reform:** some states have changed scripts for political reasons, such as Turkey moving from Arabic to Latin letters (1928) and China simplifying characters (1950s).

**Key works and figures:**
- Cuneiform tablets of Uruk (~3200 BCE): among the oldest known writing, mostly accounts.
- Johannes Gutenberg and the Gutenberg Bible (~1455): start of mass printing in Europe.
- King Sejong of Korea: commissioned the Hangul alphabet (1443–46), designed to be easy to learn.
- Walter Ong, *Orality and Literacy* (1982): how writing changes thought and culture.
- Elizabeth Eisenstein, *The Printing Press as an Agent of Change* (1979).

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: writing was invented several times independently, first for record-keeping; mass literacy is less than 200 years old; literacy has risen sharply since 1950.
- Debated: how strongly writing and printing change thinking and cause social change.
- Matters of belief or value: the sacred status some cultures give to particular scripts and texts (see 10.2, 10.3).

**Open questions:** Will digital media and voice interfaces change how much humans read? How can the remaining ~750 million non-readers be reached? Several ancient scripts (e.g., the Indus script) are still undeciphered.

**See also:** 4.2 Farming and the First Civilizations; 4.4 The Medieval and Early Modern World; 8.7 Education; 13.3 Media and Mass Communication.

### 13.3 Media and Mass Communication

**In one sentence:** Mass media are technologies and organizations that let a small number of senders reach very large audiences at once, through newspapers, radio, and television.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Media** (singular: medium) are channels that carry messages. **Mass media** send one message to many receivers.
- **Journalism** is the gathering, checking, and publishing of information about current events. Its professional norms include accuracy, verification, and separating news from opinion. Practice varies.
- **Press freedom** is the ability of media to publish without government control or punishment. It is protected in law in many countries (e.g., Article 19 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948) and restricted in many others.
- Most commercial media earn money by **selling the attention of their audiences to advertisers**, not only by selling content.

**How it works:** Mass media follow three main ownership models: **commercial** (private companies funded by advertising and subscriptions), **public service** (publicly funded but formally independent, such as the UK's BBC, founded 1922), and **state-controlled** (run by the government, common in authoritarian states). Media decide which events become news (**gatekeeping**) and how they are presented (**framing**).

Television remains the most-used news source for many older people worldwide, but online sources now dominate among younger people. Traditional news businesses have shrunk sharply. In the United States, newspaper advertising revenue fell from about **US$50 billion in the mid-2000s to under US$10 billion** by the 2020s, and newsroom jobs roughly halved [Refining]. Surveys in many countries find that only about **40%** of people say they trust most news most of the time.

Press freedom varies widely. Press-freedom groups count roughly **300–500 journalists jailed** worldwide at any given time, with China, Israel, Myanmar, Belarus, and Russia among the largest jailers in recent counts [Refining; counts vary by organization and year].

**How it developed:**
- **~59 BCE:** Rome posts official daily notices (*Acta Diurna*). China circulated official court bulletins for centuries.
- **1605:** first printed weekly newspaper in Strasbourg (then in the Holy Roman Empire).
- **1830s–1890s:** cheap mass-circulation newspapers; the **telegraph** (1840s) sends news across continents in minutes; news agencies (e.g., Reuters, 1851) form.
- **1920s:** commercial and public **radio** broadcasting begins.
- **1930s–1950s:** **television** broadcasting begins and spreads into most homes in rich countries by the 1960s.
- **1980:** CNN launches 24-hour TV news. Satellite TV spreads globally in the 1990s (e.g., Al Jazeera, 1996).
- **2000s–2020s:** audiences and advertising shift online (13.4).

**Effects on human life and society:** Mass media created shared national conversations, national identities, and celebrity. They became central to elections, war, and propaganda (13.5). Radio and TV let leaders speak directly to millions. Independent journalism has exposed abuses of power, e.g., the **Watergate** reporting that led to a US president's resignation (1974). The decline of local news in some countries has been linked to lower civic participation and less oversight of local government [Refining].

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Media effects:** early theorists feared media could inject ideas directly into passive audiences. Research since the 1940s found more limited direct effects on opinions, but strong effects on **what people think about** (**agenda-setting**, McCombs & Shaw, 1972) and on how issues are understood (**framing**) [Established in general; size varies].
- **The public sphere** (Jürgen Habermas, 1962): democracy needs spaces where citizens debate as equals, and commercial media can erode them [Contested].
- **Propaganda model** (Herman & Chomsky, 1988): in market systems, ownership, advertising, and reliance on official sources filter news in favor of elites. Critics argue it underrates journalists' independence and audience choice [Contested].
- **Objectivity vs. advocacy:** whether journalists should aim for neutrality or openly take positions [Belief/Value]. Norms differ: Hallin and Mancini (2004) describe distinct media systems across Western countries.

**Key works and figures:**
- Walter Lippmann, *Public Opinion* (1922): people react to media-made "pictures in their heads," not the world directly.
- Marshall McLuhan, *Understanding Media* (1964): "the medium is the message," i.e., each technology reshapes society regardless of content.
- Jürgen Habermas, *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere* (1962).
- Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky, *Manufacturing Consent* (1988).
- Reporters Without Borders' World Press Freedom Index (annual since 2002): ranks countries by press freedom.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: media shape which issues people consider important; advertising-funded newspapers have declined sharply; press freedom varies greatly by country.
- Debated: how much media change opinions and votes; whether concentrated ownership biases coverage; how to fund journalism.
- Matters of belief or value: what counts as fair or balanced coverage; how much governments should fund or regulate media.

**Open questions:** What business model, if any, will sustain independent journalism? How will AI-generated content affect trust in news? Does the loss of shared national media increase political division?

**See also:** 6.3 Democracy and Elections; 6.4 Authoritarianism; 13.4 The Internet and Social Media; 13.5 Propaganda, Persuasion, and Misinformation; 14.5 Performance, Film, and Games.

### 13.4 The Internet and Social Media

**In one sentence:** Since the 1990s, the internet has let most humans publish to, search, and talk with one another directly, and a few companies now run the platforms through which billions receive news, entertainment, and social contact.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- The **internet** is the global computer network described in 12.6. About **6 billion people (~70%)** use it, mostly on **smartphones** (pocket computers with touchscreens and cameras).
- A **search engine** indexes billions of web pages and returns the most relevant to a typed query. Google handles roughly **90%** of searches outside China.
- **Social media** are online services where users create and share content and connect with others. The company running one is a **platform**.
- A **feed** is the stream of posts a user sees, ordered by a **recommendation algorithm** that predicts what that user will engage with (click, watch, like, share).
- Most platforms are free to use and earn money from **advertising** targeted with data on users' behavior: the **attention economy**, in which user attention is the product sold.

**How it works:** Social media operate at enormous scale.

| Platform (owner) | Approx. monthly users, mid-2020s | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Facebook (Meta, USA) | ~3 billion | Largest social network |
| YouTube (Google, USA) | ~2.5 billion | Video |
| WhatsApp (Meta) | ~2–3 billion | Private messaging |
| Instagram (Meta) | ~2 billion | Photos, short video |
| TikTok / Douyin (ByteDance, China) | ~1.5–2 billion | Short video, strongly algorithm-driven |
| WeChat (Tencent, China) | ~1.4 billion | Messaging, payments, services |

About **5 billion people** use social media, on average for over **2 hours a day** [Refining; survey-based]. Online advertising is roughly **US$700–800 billion a year**, about **70%** of all advertising spending, and most of it goes to a handful of companies.

Recommendation algorithms rank content mainly by predicted engagement. Content that provokes strong emotion, including outrage at rival groups, tends to be shared more [Refining]. A 2018 study of Twitter found that false news stories spread farther and faster than true ones, driven mainly by human users rather than automated accounts.

Platforms remove some content (**content moderation**) using automated systems and many thousands of human reviewers. Laws differ sharply. In the United States, a 1996 law (Section 230) largely shields platforms from liability for what users post. The European Union's **Digital Services Act** (in full force from 2024) requires large platforms to assess and reduce risks. China blocks most foreign platforms and requires its own to censor content (13.5). Australia barred under-16s from major social media from late 2025.

**How it developed:**
- **1971:** first network email. **1990s:** the World Wide Web opens to the public; **Google** (1998).
- **2001:** **Wikipedia**, a free volunteer-written encyclopedia, later among the most visited websites.
- **2004–2011:** Facebook (2004), YouTube (2005), Twitter (2006; renamed X, 2023), iPhone (2007), WhatsApp (2009), Instagram (2010), WeChat (2011).
- **2010–2012:** "Arab Spring" protests across the Middle East and North Africa, organized partly online.
- **2016–2018:** alarm over election disinformation and data misuse (the **Cambridge Analytica** scandal, 2018, involved data on up to ~87 million Facebook users). TikTok's algorithm-driven short video spreads worldwide.
- **2022 onward:** AI chatbots become a new route to information; AI-generated text, images, and video become common.

**Effects on human life and society:** Benefits are broad and well documented: near-free communication with distant family, access to vast information and free education, new ways to find work and sell goods, and tools to organize protests (e.g., the Arab Spring, #MeToo in 2017). Harms are also documented in specific cases. UN investigators concluded that Facebook was a significant channel for spreading hatred against the Rohingya minority before the mass violence in Myanmar in 2017. Broader effects are harder to measure and are **[Contested]**:

**Mental health, especially of adolescents:**
- *Evidence suggesting harm:* rates of depression, anxiety, and self-harm among teenagers, especially girls, rose sharply from around **2012** in the US, UK, Canada, and some other countries, as smartphones and social media spread. Some experiments found that quitting or limiting social media for weeks modestly improved users' reported well-being. A study of Facebook's staggered arrival at US colleges (2004–06) found worse student mental health where it arrived. Leaked internal company research (2021) suggested Instagram worsened body image for some teenage girls.
- *Evidence suggesting smaller or unclear effects:* large analyses find that the average statistical link between social media use and adolescent well-being is **small**, explaining well under 1% of the variation between individuals in some studies. The rise in distress is not uniform across countries, and other causes have been proposed (academic pressure, less independent play, economic worry, changing diagnosis and reporting). Correlation studies cannot show which way causation runs: distressed teenagers may use social media more.
- *Status:* researchers broadly agree that heavy use is associated with worse outcomes for some young people, especially girls. They disagree on whether social media is a major *cause* of the population-wide rise.

**Politics and polarization:**
- *Evidence suggesting harm:* engagement-driven feeds can amplify divisive and false content. Disinformation campaigns use platforms to target voters (13.5). One experiment found that deactivating Facebook for four weeks before the 2018 US elections slightly reduced users' political polarization.
- *Evidence suggesting smaller effects:* in the US, polarization began rising before social media and rose fastest among older people, who use it least. Polarization has not risen in several other rich countries with heavy social media use. Large experiments run with Meta during the 2020 US election (published 2023) changed users' feeds, for example to chronological order, and found little measurable effect on polarization or political attitudes over three months. Most people's news diets are less one-sided than the **"echo chamber"** or **"filter bubble"** ideas suggest; exposure to fake news is concentrated in a small, highly engaged minority.
- *Status:* [Contested]. Effects may be real but smaller and more uneven than popular accounts claim, or may build over longer periods than experiments can test.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Techno-optimism** (dominant in the 1990s–2000s): connecting people spreads knowledge, empowers the weak, and undermines censorship.
- **Techno-critique** (prominent since the mid-2010s): platforms profit from capturing attention and harvesting personal data, harming users and democracy ("surveillance capitalism").
- **Regulation** [Contested; partly Belief/Value]: free expression versus removing harmful content; who decides what is false; age limits; breaking up large platforms.

**Key works and figures:**
- Eli Pariser, *The Filter Bubble* (2011): argued that personalization isolates users in their own views (later evidence has been mixed).
- Tim Wu, *The Attention Merchants* (2016): history of businesses that sell human attention.
- Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral, "The spread of true and false news online" (*Science*, 2018).
- Shoshana Zuboff, *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019).
- Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski (2019): found small average associations between technology use and teen well-being.
- Jonathan Haidt, *The Anxious Generation* (2024): argues smartphones and social media caused a youth mental health crisis; widely read and widely disputed.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: about 5 billion people use social media; a few firms dominate; feeds are ranked by engagement; platforms have been used to spread hate and disinformation in specific documented cases.
- Debated: how much social media causes adolescent mental illness; how much it drives political polarization; whether echo chambers are common.
- Matters of belief or value: where to draw lines on speech; whether children should be kept off social media; how much power private companies should have over public discussion.

**Open questions:** What are the long-run effects of growing up online? How will AI-generated content change trust in what people see? Can platforms be governed across very different political systems? Will AI assistants replace search engines and feeds as the main gateway to information?

### 13.5 Propaganda, Persuasion, and Misinformation

**In one sentence:** Humans constantly try to change one another's beliefs and behavior, through advertising, political campaigns, state propaganda, and deliberate lies, and they have developed tools such as censorship and fact-checking to control or counter these efforts.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Persuasion** is any attempt to change what someone believes or does.
- **Propaganda** is organized, systematic communication designed to shape public opinion in the sender's interest, often selective or misleading. It is usually produced by states, parties, or movements.
- **Advertising** is paid communication to promote a product, service, or cause. Spending on it is roughly **US$1 trillion a year** worldwide.
- **Misinformation** is false or misleading information spread without necessarily meaning to deceive. **Disinformation** is falsehood spread deliberately.
- **Censorship** is the suppression of speech or information, usually by governments, sometimes by companies or religious authorities.

**How it works:** Persuasion exploits regular features of human thinking (see 15.3). People tend to believe claims they hear repeatedly (the **illusory truth effect**, [Established] in experiments). They trust sources from their own group and accept information that fits what they already believe (**motivated reasoning**). They respond to emotion, social proof (what others seem to do), and authority.

Propaganda works best when the propagandist controls most channels. Authoritarian states combine propaganda with **censorship**. China, for example, blocks most foreign websites and employs large numbers of people to filter content. One study estimated that government-linked accounts post roughly **450 million** fabricated social media comments a year, mostly to distract rather than argue [Refining]. Freedom House reports that global internet freedom has declined every year since the early 2010s.

Measured effects vary greatly with context:
- **Under monopoly control or in crises, effects can be large.** Studies found that Nazi radio raised Nazi support and antisemitic acts in areas already prone to antisemitism (1930s Germany). In Rwanda in 1994, areas with reception of the extremist station RTLM had more participation in genocidal violence; one study attributed roughly a tenth of the violence to the broadcasts [Refining].
- **In competitive democracies, effects on votes are often small.** A 2018 review of field experiments found that campaign contact and advertising had, on average, close to zero effect on vote choice in US general elections, although effects were larger in primaries and on less-known issues [Refining].
- **Corrections work, modestly.** Fact-checks generally reduce belief in false claims. A feared "backfire effect," where corrections strengthen false beliefs, rarely appears in large studies. Effects of corrections fade over time. "**Prebunking**" (warning people about manipulation techniques in advance) shows modest benefits in experiments.

**How it developed:**
- **~520 BCE:** Persian king Darius I justifies his rule in the Behistun Inscription, carved on a cliff in three languages. Rulers have used monuments, coins, and rituals this way since the first states.
- **1622:** the Catholic Church founds the *Congregatio de Propaganda Fide* ("for propagating the faith"), source of the word.
- **1914–1918:** World War I governments run mass propaganda campaigns (e.g., the US Committee on Public Information, 1917).
- **1920s:** Edward Bernays builds modern **public relations**, applying psychology to persuasion.
- **1933–1945:** Nazi Germany's Propaganda Ministry (Joseph Goebbels) controls press, radio, and film; the Soviet Union runs a comparable system until 1991.
- **2010s–2020s:** state-backed online disinformation, including Russian operations targeting the 2016 US election (documented by US investigations). Since 2022, AI makes realistic fake text, audio, and video (**deepfakes**) cheap.

**Effects on human life and society:** Propaganda has helped mobilize populations for war and genocide, sustain dictatorships, and win elections; it has also served public-health campaigns, such as anti-smoking efforts. Advertising funds most commercial media and the internet (13.3, 13.4). Misinformation contributed to vaccine refusal during COVID-19 and to violence in several countries [Refining on size]. Censorship shapes what billions can learn about their own governments.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **The "marketplace of ideas"** (rooted in John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty*, 1859): truth tends to win in open debate, so the answer to bad speech is more speech [Belief/Value; its empirical premise is Contested].
- **Regulating harmful speech:** many democracies ban some speech (e.g., incitement; Holocaust denial in Germany), arguing unchecked disinformation damages democracy [Belief/Value].
- **Strong vs. limited effects:** early theorists saw propaganda as nearly all-powerful; later research found limited effects in open societies. Current views stress context, repetition, and control of channels [Refining].
- **Who decides what is false?** Fact-checking and moderation are attacked as biased by some and defended as essential by others [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- Walter Lippmann, *Public Opinion* (1922): coined the phrase "the manufacture of consent."
- Edward Bernays, *Propaganda* (1928): a frank guide to modern persuasion by its practitioner.
- Jacques Ellul, *Propaganda* (1962): propaganda as a feature of all modern technological societies.
- Robert Cialdini, *Influence* (1984): principles of persuasion from experimental psychology.
- George Orwell, *Nineteen Eighty-Four* (1949, fiction): a portrait of total propaganda and surveillance, widely used as a reference point.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: repetition and group loyalty increase belief; propaganda has had large effects where states controlled the media; corrections modestly reduce false beliefs.
- Debated: how much online disinformation changes votes; how effective fact-checking and prebunking are at scale; the effects of AI-generated content.
- Matters of belief or value: the limits of free speech; whether and when governments or platforms should remove false content.

**Open questions:** Will cheap AI-generated content overwhelm humans' ability to tell real from fake? Can any institution be widely trusted to judge truth? Which defenses against manipulation work across cultures?
