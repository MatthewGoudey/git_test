# Domain 7: Law and Justice

Law is the set of rules a society formally enforces, backed by the power of the state. Unlike customs or manners, which are enforced by disapproval, law is enforced by courts, police, fines, prisons, and sometimes execution. Humans also hold strong, often conflicting, beliefs about **justice**, meaning what treatment people deserve. Law is their practical, imperfect attempt to turn some of those beliefs into enforceable rules.

---

### 7.1 Legal Systems

**In one sentence:** A legal system is a society's whole machinery of rules, the institutions that make and apply them (legislatures, courts, police), and the professionals who run it (judges, lawyers).

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- A **law** (or **statute**) is a rule made by an authority recognized as entitled to make it, usually a legislature.
- A **court** is a public body that settles disputes and decides guilt by applying law to facts. A **judge** presides; **lawyers** argue for each side.
- **Precedent** is a past court decision that later courts must follow in similar cases.
- **Criminal law** concerns wrongs against society, prosecuted by the state and punished. **Civil law** (in one of its two meanings) concerns disputes between private parties, such as over debts or injuries, usually resolved by payment.
- The **rule of law** is the principle that everyone, including rulers, is bound by known, stable laws applied equally by independent courts.

**How it works:**
Most of the world's legal systems belong to a few broad families:

| Family | Main source of law | How courts work | Where used |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Civil law** | Comprehensive written **codes** | Judges apply the code and often lead the investigation ("inquisitorial") | Most of Europe and Latin America; China, Japan, South Korea; much of Africa. The most widespread family. |
| **Common law** | Statutes plus judges' precedents | Two sides argue before a neutral judge, sometimes with a **jury** of ordinary citizens ("adversarial") | UK, US, India, Canada, Australia, Nigeria, and other former British colonies; roughly a third of humanity |
| **Religious law** | Sacred texts and scholarly interpretation | Religious or state courts | Islamic law (**sharia**) applied broadly in Saudi Arabia, Iran, Afghanistan; for family and inheritance matters in many Muslim-majority states. Jewish law (**halakha**) governs marriage and divorce for Jews in Israel. |
| **Customary law** | Long-standing community practice, often unwritten | Elders, chiefs, community councils | Widely used in sub-Saharan Africa and the Pacific, often recognized alongside state law |

Many countries mix families. India uses common law but has separate religion-based "personal laws" on marriage and inheritance. South Africa, Scotland, the Philippines, Israel, and the Canadian province of Quebec are formal hybrids.

In China, courts operate under the supervision of the Communist Party, which reviews politically sensitive cases; the government calls this "socialist rule of law," while critics say it means courts are not independent [Contested].

Access to justice is limited for many. A 2019 international estimate found about 5 billion people had legal problems they could not resolve, often because courts were too costly, slow, distant, or corrupt [Refining].

**How it developed:**
The oldest surviving law code, from the city of Ur (Mesopotamia), dates to about 2100 BCE. The **Code of Hammurabi** (~1754 BCE) set out 282 laws, including "eye for an eye" punishments that varied by social class. Rome's **Twelve Tables** (~450 BCE) began a legal tradition later compiled by the emperor Justinian (529–534 CE), which became the base of civil law. China's imperial codes, notably the Tang Code (653 CE), shaped East Asian law for over a thousand years. Islamic jurisprudence (**fiqh**) developed between the 8th and 10th centuries into several schools that still operate. English common law grew from royal courts after 1066. The French **Napoleonic Code** (1804) spread across Europe and, through conquest and colonization, to Latin America, Africa, and the Middle East.

**Effects on human life and society:**
Legal systems decide how disputes are settled, who owns what, and how predictable life and business are. Economists find that reliable courts and enforcement of contracts are associated with higher incomes [Refining on causation]. Corruption and slow courts impose large costs; in some countries, civil cases routinely take a decade or more.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Natural law:** valid law must conform to moral principles that exist independently of humans (sometimes attributed to God or reason) [Belief/Value].
- **Legal positivism:** law is whatever has been validly enacted, whether or not it is moral (H.L.A. Hart). The two views clashed over whether Nazi laws were "law."
- **Legal realism:** law in practice is what judges actually do, shaped by their views and interests.
- **Law and economics:** legal rules can be judged by whether they produce efficient outcomes (Richard Posner) [Contested as a normative guide].

**Key works and figures:**
- Code of Hammurabi (~1754 BCE): an early public law code carved in stone.
- *Corpus Juris Civilis* (Justinian, 529–534): the foundation of civil law.
- *Al-Risala* (al-Shafi'i, ~820): founding text on the method of Islamic jurisprudence.
- *Commentaries on the Laws of England* (William Blackstone, 1765–69): spread common law to the American colonies.
- Napoleonic Code (1804): the model for modern codes worldwide.
- *The Concept of Law* (H.L.A. Hart, 1961): the leading modern statement of legal positivism.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Civil and common law are the two largest families; both spread largely through empire and colonization.
- Debated: Whether common-law systems promote financial development more than civil-law ones [Contested]; how much legal origin shapes economic outcomes.
- Matters of belief or value: Whether law must be moral to be valid; whether religious law should govern a state.

**Open questions:** How should states blend customary or religious law with state law and international rights standards? Can technology, including AI, make justice cheaper and more widely available without making it less fair?

### 7.2 Constitutions and Rights

**In one sentence:** A constitution is a state's highest law, setting up its institutions and their powers, and usually listing **rights**: protections individuals hold that even the government may not override.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- A **codified constitution** is a single written document. An **uncodified** one is spread across ordinary laws, court rulings, and customs (as in the UK and New Zealand).
- A **right** is a claim a person can enforce against others, including the state. **Civil liberties** are rights that limit government, such as freedom of speech and religion. **Due process** is the right to fair legal procedure before punishment.
- **Judicial review** is the power of courts to strike down laws that conflict with the constitution.
- An **amendment** is a formal change to a constitution, usually harder to pass than an ordinary law.

**How it works:**
Nearly every state has a constitution, including dictatorships. What matters is whether it is followed. The USSR's 1936 constitution promised freedom of speech during Stalin's mass purges. Researchers call such documents **sham constitutions** [Refining].

Constitutions vary widely. The US Constitution (1787) is the oldest codified national constitution still in use: about 7,500 words including its 27 amendments. India's (1950) is the longest, at roughly 145,000 words in English, amended over 100 times. National constitutions last about 17–19 years on average before being replaced [Refining].

Most states now give courts some power of judicial review. Some (the US) let ordinary courts do this; others (Germany, South Africa, South Korea) have special **constitutional courts**. In the UK, Parliament remains supreme and courts can only declare a law incompatible with rights. Constitutions often divide rights into categories:

| Type | Examples | Typically enforceable in court? |
|---|---|---|
| Civil and political | Speech, religion, fair trial, voting | Usually yes |
| Economic and social | Education, health, housing, work | Sometimes (South Africa, India, Colombia) |
| Collective / newer | Indigenous self-government, a healthy environment | Increasingly, but unevenly |

**How it developed:**
**Magna Carta** (England, 1215), forced on a king by his barons, established that the ruler was subject to law, though it protected mainly the elite. England's Bill of Rights (1689) limited the monarch further. The US Constitution (1787) and Bill of Rights (1791) and France's Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789) set models for written rights, while both countries kept slavery or colonies. Haiti's 1805 constitution abolished slavery outright. Mexico's 1917 constitution was the first to guarantee social rights such as land and labor protections. After World War II, Germany's Basic Law (1949) began with "Human dignity shall be inviolable." India's constitution (1950), drafted under B.R. Ambedkar, banned "untouchability." South Africa's (1996), written after apartheid, is often cited for its broad, court-enforced social rights.

**Effects on human life and society:**
Constitutions shape how power transfers and how disputes between branches are resolved. Court rulings under them have ended legal racial segregation (US, 1954), expanded or limited abortion access, and recognized same-sex marriage (in about 35–40 countries by the mid-2020s, often through courts). Research suggests written rights protect people best when backed by independent courts and organized citizens [Refining].

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Judicial review vs. majority rule:** critics ask why unelected judges should overrule elected legislatures (the "counter-majoritarian difficulty"). Defenders reply that rights must be protected from majorities [Contested; Belief/Value].
- **Originalism vs. living constitutionalism** (prominent in the US): should a constitution be read as its authors understood it, or adapted to present conditions [Belief/Value]?
- **Universal vs. culturally specific rights:** some governments (e.g., in the 1990s "Asian values" debate) argued that Western rights ideas do not fit every society; critics replied that rulers use this argument to excuse repression [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- Magna Carta (1215): the symbol of rulers being under the law.
- *Two Treatises of Government* (John Locke, 1689): natural rights to life, liberty, and property.
- US Constitution (1787) and Bill of Rights (1791): the longest-surviving codified constitution.
- Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (France, 1789).
- *Marbury v. Madison* (US Supreme Court, 1803): established judicial review in the US.
- Constitution of India (1950) and Constitution of South Africa (1996): influential post-colonial and post-apartheid models.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Written constitutions are now nearly universal; many are not followed in practice; judicial review has spread worldwide since 1945.
- Debated: How much constitutional design affects stability [Contested]; how far courts should go in enforcing social rights.
- Matters of belief or value: Which rights humans *should* have; how to balance rights that conflict (speech vs. protection from hate or harm).

**Open questions:** Why do some constitutions endure while others are discarded? Chile's voters rejected two proposed new constitutions, in 2022 and 2023, showing how hard wholesale redesign can be. How should constitutions handle new questions, such as privacy in an age of mass data collection?

**See also:** 6.2 Forms of Government; 6.3 Democracy and Elections; 7.5 International Law and Human Rights; 9.2 Liberalism; 11.4 Political Philosophy.

### 7.3 Crime, Policing, and Punishment

**In one sentence:** Crime is behavior a state has forbidden and will punish, and the criminal justice system is the chain of police, prosecutors, courts, and prisons that enforces those prohibitions.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- A **crime** is an act the law defines as an offense against society, such as murder, theft, or fraud. What counts as a crime varies by place and time. Same-sex relations are criminal in roughly 60–65 countries and legal in the rest; some states criminalize blasphemy, adultery, or criticism of the ruler.
- **Police** are state employees authorized to investigate crime, keep order, and arrest people, using force if needed.
- A **trial** is a court proceeding that decides guilt. In many systems, the accused is presumed innocent until proven guilty.
- A **sentence** is the punishment imposed: a fine, supervision in the community, imprisonment, or, in some states, death.
- **Homicide** is the killing of one person by another; it is the most reliably counted crime, so it is often used to compare countries.

**How it works:**
Roughly **450,000 people** are murdered each year, about 6 per 100,000 people. Rates vary more than tenfold between regions: the Americas have about a third of all homicides with about 13% of the population, while most of East Asia and Western Europe have rates near or below 1 per 100,000. About 80% of homicide victims and about 90% of convicted killers are men. Women are more likely to be killed by a partner or relative; roughly 50,000 women and girls a year are killed this way.

About **11–12 million people** are in prison worldwide, about a third of them not yet convicted (awaiting trial). The United States holds the most, about 1.8–1.9 million. El Salvador has had the highest rate since 2022, when it suspended some constitutional protections and imprisoned over 80,000 suspected gang members. Its government reports that homicides fell from among the world's highest rates to among the lowest in the Americas; human-rights groups report wrongful arrests and deaths in custody [Contested assessments of costs and benefits].

About 55 countries still use the **death penalty**; around 145 have abolished it in law or practice. Over 1,500 executions were recorded in 2024, mostly in Iran, Saudi Arabia, and Iraq. China is believed to execute thousands a year but keeps the number secret.

**How it developed:**
For most of history, punishment was public and physical (whipping, mutilation, execution) or took the form of compensation paid to victims' families. Private revenge and feuds were common. Italian jurist Cesare Beccaria (1764) argued that punishment should be certain, proportionate, and aimed at prevention, and opposed torture and the death penalty. Prisons as the main punishment spread in the 1800s. London's Metropolitan Police (1829) became a model for professional, uniformed police. Many rich countries saw large drops in crime from the early 1990s; US violent crime roughly halved between 1991 and 2019, rose sharply in 2020, and fell again after 2022.

**Effects on human life and society:**
Crime causes death, injury, fear, and economic losses; organized crime (drug cartels, gangs, mafias) controls territory in parts of Latin America, Africa, and Asia. Mexico has recorded over 400,000 homicides since its government launched a military campaign against drug cartels in 2006. Punishment also has costs. Imprisonment can lower people's later earnings and harm their families [Refining]. Justice systems often fall unequally: in the US, Black Americans are imprisoned at about five times the rate of white Americans, and Indigenous Australians make up about a third of Australia's prisoners while being about 4% of its population [causes Contested]. Wrongful convictions occur everywhere; the US alone has recorded over 3,500 **exonerations** (convictions later overturned on evidence of innocence) since 1989.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
Humans give five main justifications for punishment, which often conflict [Belief/Value]:

| Aim | Idea |
|---|---|
| Retribution | Wrongdoers deserve to suffer in proportion to their wrong |
| Deterrence | Punishment discourages the offender and others |
| Incapacitation | Locking people up prevents them from offending while held |
| Rehabilitation | Punishment should reform the offender |
| Restorative justice | Repairing harm through meetings between offender, victim, and community |

Evidence suggests the **certainty** of being caught deters crime more than the **severity** of punishment [Refining]. Whether the death penalty deters murder more than long imprisonment remains unproven [Contested]. Debates over why crime fell (more police, more prisons, less lead in the environment, changing demographics, technology) are unresolved [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- *On Crimes and Punishments* (Cesare Beccaria, 1764): founding text of modern penal reform.
- Robert Peel: founder of London's Metropolitan Police (1829) and its principles of policing by consent.
- *Discipline and Punish* (Michel Foucault, 1975): an influential critique of prisons as tools of social control [Contested].
- UNODC *Global Study on Homicide*: the main source of comparative homicide data.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Homicide rates vary enormously across countries; men commit most violent crime; crime fell in many rich countries after the 1990s.
- Debated: Causes of crime trends; the effects of mass incarceration [Contested]; how much policing reduces crime versus causing harm.
- Matters of belief or value: What should be criminal (drugs, sex work, speech); whether the death penalty is ever just.

**Open questions:** Why did crime fall so widely after the 1990s? How should societies handle crimes committed online and across borders?

### 7.4 Property, Contracts, and Commercial Law

**In one sentence:** Property and contract law set out who owns what and which promises the state will enforce, and together they make trade, lending, and companies possible.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Property** is not a physical thing but a bundle of socially enforced rights over a thing: to use it, to keep others out, and to sell or give it away. It can be held privately, by the state, or in common by a community.
- A **contract** is a promise, or exchange of promises, that the law will enforce. If one side breaks it, the other can go to court for compensation.
- A **corporation** is an organization the law treats as a **legal person**: it can own property, sign contracts, sue, and be sued, separately from its owners.
- **Limited liability** means a company's owners can lose only what they invested, not their personal wealth, if the company fails.
- **Intellectual property** (IP) is ownership of creations of the mind: **patents** (inventions, usually for 20 years), **copyright** (creative works, commonly the creator's life plus 50–70 years), and **trademarks** (brand names and logos).
- **Bankruptcy** is a legal process for dealing with people or companies that cannot pay their debts.

**How it works:**
A buyer can pay a stranger for land, a bank can lend to a company, and a firm can hire thousands of workers because courts will enforce titles and agreements. Land **registries** record who owns which piece of land. In many poorer countries, much land, especially rural land held under customary systems, is undocumented, which can make it hard to use as **collateral** (security for a loan) and easy to seize [Refining on scale].

Corporations are the main way large-scale economic activity is organized. Commercial disputes are increasingly settled outside courts by **arbitration**: private judges chosen by the parties, whose rulings are enforceable in about 170 countries under a 1958 treaty. About 3.5 million patent applications are filed worldwide each year, nearly half of them in China.

**How it developed:**
Ancient law codes already regulated sales, loans, and inheritance. Roman law developed detailed concepts of ownership and contract still used today. Islamic law developed sophisticated contract and partnership rules; its ban on interest (**riba**) underpins modern Islamic finance, a sector holding several trillion US dollars in assets. Venice issued an early patent law (1474), and Britain's Statute of Anne (1710) created copyright. The Dutch East India Company (1602) was an early company with tradable shares. General limited liability spread in the 1800s (New York, 1811; Britain, 1855), fueling industrial growth. Debtors' prisons gave way to modern bankruptcy, which lets debtors start over.

**Effects on human life and society:**
Secure property and enforceable contracts are strongly associated with investment and growth. Economists Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, and James Robinson (Nobel Prize in economics, 2024) argued that institutions protecting property rights explain much of the gap between rich and poor countries [Refining; causal strength Contested]. Property law also decides distribution: land disputes are a major cause of local violence, and the forced removal of Indigenous peoples and colonized populations from land was carried out largely through law.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Locke's labor theory:** people gain property by mixing their labor with unowned resources [Belief/Value].
- **Socialist critiques:** private ownership of productive property (factories, land) enables exploitation (see 9.4) [Belief/Value].
- **Land titling:** Hernando de Soto argued that giving the poor formal titles would unlock capital. Results of titling programs have been mixed [Contested].
- **IP debates:** patents reward innovation but raise prices, a conflict seen in fights over access to HIV and COVID-19 medicines. Lawsuits over whether training AI systems on copyrighted works is legal are ongoing in several countries [Contested].
- **Corporate personhood:** critics argue companies have gained too many rights (e.g., to political spending in the US after 2010) [Belief/Value].

**Key works and figures:**
- *Two Treatises of Government* (John Locke, 1689): property as a natural right.
- *The Wealth of Nations* (Adam Smith, 1776): on markets and the legal order they need.
- *The Mystery of Capital* (Hernando de Soto, 2000): property titles and poverty.
- *Why Nations Fail* (Acemoglu and Robinson, 2012): institutions and prosperity.
- New York Convention (1958): makes international arbitration enforceable.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Markets and corporations depend on enforceable property and contract law; limited liability enabled large firms.
- Debated: How much property institutions cause growth; the right length and scope of IP protection [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: Whether property is a natural right or a social convention; how much private ownership is just.

**Open questions:** How should law treat data, digital goods, and AI-generated works? Who owns resources beyond national borders, such as the deep seabed and outer space?

### 7.5 International Law and Human Rights

**In one sentence:** International law is the body of rules that states accept as binding in their dealings with each other, including rules on war and on how governments must treat people, enforced without any world police.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- International law comes mainly from **treaties** (written agreements states sign and **ratify**, that is, formally approve) and **customary international law** (consistent state practice accepted as legally required).
- **Jurisdiction** is a court's authority to hear a case.
- **Human rights** are rights humans are said to hold simply by being human, regardless of nationality. Their existence as moral facts is [Belief/Value]; their existence in treaties is [Established].
- **International humanitarian law** (the laws of war) limits how wars are fought. Its core principles are **distinction** (do not target civilians), **proportionality** (civilian harm must not be excessive relative to military gain), and **precaution**.
- **War crimes** are serious violations of these laws. **Crimes against humanity** are widespread or systematic attacks on civilians. **Genocide** is the attempt to destroy a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group.

**How it works:**
There is no world legislature, compulsory court, or police. Compliance rests on reciprocity, reputation, domestic courts, sanctions, and the interests states have in predictable rules. Most international law, on trade, aviation, postal services, and borders, is obeyed routinely. It is most often broken where stakes are highest: war and the treatment of a government's own opponents.

| Institution | Founded | What it does | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| International Court of Justice (The Hague) | 1945 | Settles disputes between states; gives advisory opinions | States must consent to its jurisdiction; enforcement depends on the UN Security Council, where vetoes apply |
| International Criminal Court (The Hague) | 1998 treaty, active 2002 | Tries individuals for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, aggression | About 125 member states; the US, China, Russia, and India are not members; it has no police and relies on states to arrest suspects |
| UN Human Rights Council and treaty bodies | 2006 / various | Review states' human rights records | Mostly reports and recommendations |
| Regional courts (European, Inter-American, African) | 1959 onward | Hear cases brought by individuals against states | Compliance varies; the European court is the strongest |

Recent high-profile cases show both reach and limits. The ICC issued arrest warrants for Russian President Vladimir Putin (2023) over the deportation of Ukrainian children, and for Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, his former defense minister, and Hamas leaders (2024). Russia and Israel reject the court's jurisdiction, and the US has sanctioned ICC officials. In 2025, former Philippine president Rodrigo Duterte was arrested and sent to the ICC over killings in his anti-drug campaign. At the ICJ, South Africa brought a genocide case against Israel (2023), which Israel rejects as baseless; the case continues.

**How it developed:**
Hugo Grotius's *On the Law of War and Peace* (1625) is often called the founding text. The Hague Conventions (1899, 1907) and the first Geneva Convention (1864) began codifying the laws of war. After World War II, the **Nuremberg trials** (1945–46) tried Nazi leaders and established that individuals, not just states, can be punished for international crimes. The **UN Charter** (1945) banned the use of force between states except in self-defense or with Security Council authorization. The **Universal Declaration of Human Rights** (1948) was adopted with 48 votes in favor, none against, and 8 abstentions. It was not itself binding, but was followed by binding treaties: the Genocide Convention (1948, a word coined by lawyer Raphael Lemkin in 1944), the four **Geneva Conventions** (1949, ratified by every state), the Refugee Convention (1951), and the two International Covenants on rights (1966). UN tribunals for the former Yugoslavia (1993) and Rwanda (1994) led to the permanent ICC.

**Effects on human life and society:**
Treaties have eliminated or limited entire categories of harm: the chemical weapons ban (1993) has over 190 parties, and the ozone treaty (1987) phased out most ozone-destroying chemicals. The Refugee Convention protects tens of millions from being forced back to persecution. Yet violations are widespread: civilians are routinely targeted in war, and ICC prosecutions remain few (dozens of cases in over 20 years).

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Realist skepticism:** international law reflects power and is obeyed only when convenient.
- **Institutionalist view:** law shapes behavior by setting expectations and raising the cost of violations. Lawyer Louis Henkin (1968) observed that "almost all nations observe almost all principles of international law… almost all of the time."
- **Do human rights treaties work?** Some studies found ratification alone changes little; others (Beth Simmons, 2009) found treaties help domestic activists push for change [Contested].
- **Selectivity critique:** the ICC's early cases focused almost entirely on Africa, leading some African governments to accuse it of bias; supporters note several cases were referred by African governments themselves [Contested].
- **Intervention:** whether outside force is justified to stop atrocities (the 2005 "Responsibility to Protect" doctrine) versus respect for sovereignty [Belief/Value].

**Key works and figures:**
- *On the Law of War and Peace* (Hugo Grotius, 1625).
- UN Charter (1945): the basic rules on the use of force.
- Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), drafted under the leadership of Eleanor Roosevelt, with key roles for René Cassin, P.C. Chang, and Charles Malik.
- Geneva Conventions (1949): the core of the laws of war.
- Rome Statute (1998): founded the International Criminal Court.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: International law is mostly obeyed in routine matters; enforcement against powerful states is weak; the Security Council veto blocks action against permanent members and their allies.
- Debated: Whether human rights treaties change behavior; the legality of particular wars and interventions (Kosovo 1999, Iraq 2003, Ukraine 2022, and Gaza are all argued over) [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: Whether human rights are universal; whether sovereignty should yield to stopping atrocities.

**Open questions:** Can international law constrain great powers? How will it handle cyberwar, autonomous weapons, and climate harm? In 2025 the ICJ advised that states have legal obligations to prevent climate damage; how that will be applied is unknown.
