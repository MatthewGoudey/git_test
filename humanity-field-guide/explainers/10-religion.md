# Domain 10: Religion, Spirituality, and Belief

Religion is one of the oldest and most widespread human behaviors. About three-quarters of living humans identify with a religion, and religious ideas have shaped laws, wars, art, calendars, diets, and family life for thousands of years. This domain describes what humans believe, how religions are organized, where they came from, and what effects they have. **It never asserts that any supernatural claim is true or false.** Where a sentence describes a belief, it says who holds it; historical facts about religions (dates, numbers, events) are stated as history.

---

### 10.1 What Religion Is and Why Humans Have It

**In one sentence:** A religion is a shared system of beliefs and practices, usually centered on beings, forces, or realities that its followers treat as sacred and beyond ordinary experience, which binds a community together and tells its members how to live.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- *Sacred*: set apart and treated with special respect, awe, or fear, and protected by rules against ordinary use. A forbidden act of this kind is called a *taboo*.
- *Supernatural*: said to exist or act outside the natural order that science studies. Supernatural claims cannot be tested with current methods.
- *God* or *deity*: a powerful being, usually invisible and believed to be immortal, that humans *worship* (address with praise, requests, and offerings). *Monotheism* is belief in one god; *polytheism* is belief in many.
- *Ritual*: a formal, repeated sequence of actions with symbolic meaning, such as a set prayer at set times.
- *Soul* and *afterlife*: many religions teach that some non-physical part of a person (a soul) continues to exist after the body dies.
- Humans have **no agreed definition** of religion. Some traditions have no creator god (much of Buddhism); some have no fixed scripture. Many languages had no word matching "religion" before contact with Europeans, so the category itself is partly a modern Western one [Refining].

**How it works:** Most religions combine several of these features, though none is found in all: beliefs about invisible agents (gods, spirits, ancestors) or impersonal cosmic laws; rituals, including rites of passage at birth, adulthood, marriage, and death; sacred places, objects, and times; specialists (priests, shamans, monks, rabbis, imams); moral rules believed to carry divine or cosmic authority; sacred origin stories (*myths*, in the scholarly sense, not "false stories"); in literate societies, sacred texts (*scripture*); and a community joined through initiation.

Religious practice has been found in nearly every society anthropologists have documented. Most humans keep the religion they were raised in, so religion passes mainly through families.

**Scientific theories of why humans are religious.** Researchers study religion "from outside" using archaeology, psychology, and anthropology, without ruling on whether its claims are true.
- *Archaeology:* deliberate burials appear by roughly 100,000 years ago, some with objects placed in the grave. Whether they show belief in an afterlife is [Contested]. Göbekli Tepe in modern Turkey, a complex of carved stone enclosures from about 9500 BCE (before farming was established there), is widely read as a ritual site [Refining].
- *Cognitive science of religion* (since the 1990s) treats belief as a by-product of ordinary mental tools. Humans readily detect agents behind events (a rustle in the grass might be a predator; false alarms are cheap), habitually imagine other minds, and see purpose in nature. Ideas that break just one or two intuitive expectations (a person who walks through walls) are memorable and spread well. Each mechanism is [Refining]; how much they explain is [Contested].
- *Evolutionary theories* split into **by-product** views (religion is a side effect of traits that evolved for other reasons) and **adaptation** views (religion helped groups survive). The "Big Gods" hypothesis says belief in all-seeing, moralizing gods let large groups of strangers trust each other [Contested; a 2019 study argued such gods appeared only after societies grew large, and that study was itself sharply criticized]. "Costly signaling" theory says demanding rituals prove commitment and build trust [Refining].
- *Social theories:* Émile Durkheim (1912) argued that shared ritual creates intense group feeling and that religion is partly a society worshipping itself [influential, hard to test]. Karl Marx (1844) called religion "the opium of the people," a comfort for the oppressed that also props up rulers [Contested]. Sigmund Freud (1927) saw it as wish fulfillment [largely unsupported]. Others stress that it eases fear of death and uncertainty [Refining].

Most specialists think several mechanisms combine [Refining].

**How it developed:** In small foraging bands, religion typically centered on spirits of animals, places, and ancestors. After farming and cities appeared (~10,000–5,000 years ago), temples, full-time priests, and gods of cities and kings emerged; rulers often claimed divine approval. Between roughly 800 and 200 BCE, thinkers in Greece, Israel, Persia, India, and China put new weight on ethics and inner transformation (the "Axial Age" idea, itself [Contested]). From about 500 BCE to 700 CE, missionary religions (Buddhism, Christianity, Islam) began seeking converts across ethnic lines. The academic study of religion began in 19th-century Europe.

**Effects on human life and society:** See 10.7. Religion organizes identity, morality, and community for billions of humans and has been a major source of both cooperation and conflict.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- *Religious accounts* explain religion as a response to real divine or spiritual realities [Belief/Value].
- *Naturalistic accounts* explain it fully through psychology, evolution, and society [Belief/Value as a total explanation; the specific mechanisms are testable].
- *Methodological agnosticism*, the working rule of most scholars, studies religion's causes and effects while setting aside whether its claims are true.

**Key works and figures:**
- *Primitive Culture* (Edward Tylor, 1871): defined religion as "belief in spiritual beings."
- *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (William James, 1902): founded the psychology of religion.
- *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (Émile Durkheim, 1912): religion as social glue.
- *Religion Explained* (Pascal Boyer, 2001): key text of the cognitive approach.
- *Big Gods* (Ara Norenzayan, 2013): religion and large-scale cooperation.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: religion is near-universal across societies; it is mostly transmitted through families; ritual burial is very old.
- Debated: whether religion is an adaptation or a by-product; when it began; whether moralizing gods enabled large societies.
- Matters of belief or value: whether any god, spirit, or afterlife exists.

**Open questions:** Did religion begin once or many times? Why do some individuals and societies become much less religious while others do not? How much of religious belief is innate tendency versus learned culture?

### 10.2 The Abrahamic Religions

**In one sentence:** Judaism, Christianity, and Islam are three related monotheistic religions, together followed by roughly 4.3 billion people (more than half of humanity), which all trace their spiritual ancestry to a figure named Abraham and teach that one God created the universe and revealed his will to humans.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- *Revelation*: a message that believers hold was communicated directly by God to humans, often through a *prophet* (a person believers say God chose to speak through).
- *Covenant*: a binding agreement; in these religions, an agreement believers say God made with humans.
- *Scripture* here means texts believers regard as divinely inspired or, in Islam's case, as God's literal words.
- *Abraham* is described in the Hebrew Bible as a herder whom God called to found a people. Most historians regard the stories of Abraham as legend or cannot confirm them [Contested]; his importance is as a shared symbol.
- *BCE/CE* ("before/of the common era") count years from the traditional estimate of Jesus's birth, a convention originally Christian and now used worldwide.

**How it works:**

| Religion | Approx. followers (2020) | Origin place & date | Core beliefs (as held by followers) | Key texts | Main branches |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Judaism** | ~15 million | The Levant (eastern Mediterranean); Israelite religion from ~1000 BCE, consolidated ~500 BCE; rabbinic form after 70 CE | One God; a covenant with the Jewish people; living by God's commandments (traditionally 613) | Hebrew Bible (*Tanakh*), especially the *Torah* (first five books); the *Talmud* (rabbinic law and debate, ~200–500 CE) | Orthodox (including Haredi), Conservative, Reform; many secular Jews |
| **Christianity** | ~2.3 billion | Roman-ruled Judea and Galilee, ~30 CE | One God in three persons (the *Trinity*: Father, Son, Holy Spirit); Jesus as the Son of God and *Messiah* (Christ); salvation through Jesus's death and resurrection | The Bible: Old Testament (largely the Hebrew Bible) and New Testament (27 books, written ~50–120 CE) | Catholic (~1.3–1.4 billion), Protestant including Pentecostal and evangelical (~0.8–0.9 billion), Eastern and Oriental Orthodox (~0.25–0.3 billion) |
| **Islam** | ~2.0 billion | Mecca and Medina, Arabia, 610–632 CE | One God (*Allah*, the Arabic word for God); Muhammad as the final prophet; a Day of Judgment; submission to God's will (*islam* means "submission") | The *Quran*; the *Hadith* (reports of Muhammad's words and actions) | Sunni (~85–90%), Shia (~10–15%); Sufism is a mystical strand within both |

**Judaism.** Jews believe God made a covenant with Abraham and later gave the Law (*Torah*) to the prophet Moses. Religious practice centers on observing commandments: a weekly day of rest (the *Sabbath*, Friday sunset to Saturday sunset), dietary rules (*kosher*), festivals such as Passover and Yom Kippur, and study of texts. Jews are both a religious group and a people; many identify as Jewish without religious belief. About 45% of Jews live in Israel and roughly 40% in the United States [Refining].

**Christianity.** Historians broadly agree that Jesus of Nazareth existed as a historical person: a Jewish preacher in Galilee who was executed by crucifixion under the Roman governor Pontius Pilate around 30–33 CE [Established among historians]. Christians believe he rose from the dead three days later, performed miracles, and is divine [Belief/Value]. The earliest surviving Christian texts are letters by the missionary Paul (~50–60 CE). Practices include *baptism* (a water rite of entry), the *Eucharist* or communion (a ritual meal of bread and wine), Sunday worship, and the festivals of Christmas and Easter.

**Islam.** Muhammad (~570–632 CE) was a merchant in Mecca. Muslims believe the angel Gabriel delivered God's revelations to him from 610 until his death, and that these were recorded as the Quran, which Muslims regard as God's literal, unaltered word [Belief/Value]. The standard written text was compiled by about 650 CE. Muslim practice rests on the *Five Pillars*: declaring faith, praying five times daily facing Mecca, giving alms (*zakat*), fasting from dawn to sunset during the month of *Ramadan*, and making the pilgrimage (*hajj*) to Mecca at least once if able. *Sharia* ("the path") is the body of religious law derived from the Quran and Hadith by scholars. Only about 20% of Muslims are Arabs; the largest Muslim population is in Indonesia (~240 million), followed by Pakistan, India, and Bangladesh.

**How it developed:**
- ~1000–500 BCE: Israelite kingdoms; worship of the god Yahweh becomes exclusive monotheism, especially after the Babylonian conquest of Jerusalem in 586 BCE [Refining].
- 70 CE: Rome destroys the Jerusalem Temple; Judaism reorganizes around rabbis (teachers), synagogues, and texts, and Jews disperse (the *diaspora*).
- ~30–100 CE: Christianity emerges as a Jewish movement and spreads to non-Jews. 313: Emperor Constantine legalizes it; 380: it becomes the Roman Empire's official religion.
- 622: Muhammad and followers move to Medina (the *Hijra*, year 1 of the Islamic calendar). By 750, Arab-led conquests created a state from Spain to Central Asia; Islam later spread by trade to West Africa and Southeast Asia.
- 632–680: disputes over who should lead Muslims after Muhammad produce the Sunni–Shia split; Shia Muslims hold that leadership belonged to Muhammad's family through his cousin and son-in-law Ali.
- 1054: Christianity splits into Catholic (West) and Orthodox (East). 1517 onward: the Protestant Reformation splits Western Christianity, followed by over a century of religious wars.
- 1095–1291: the Crusades, Christian military campaigns to control Jerusalem and the surrounding region.
- 1500s–1900s: European colonization spreads Christianity to the Americas, Africa, and Oceania.
- 1941–45: the Holocaust: Nazi Germany murders about 6 million Jews, about two-thirds of Europe's Jews. 1948: the State of Israel is founded.
- Today: Christianity's center of gravity has shifted south; sub-Saharan Africa now has more Christians than Europe. Islam is the fastest-growing major religion, mainly because Muslims are younger and have more children on average [Refining].

**Effects on human life and society:** These religions shaped law (Jewish *halakha*, Christian canon law, Islamic sharia), the seven-day week, the world's dominant calendar, and the moral vocabulary of much of the planet. They have built hospitals, universities, and charities, and have also driven crusades, inquisitions, and sectarian wars. See 10.7.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** Within each religion, *traditionalists* hold scriptures and inherited law as binding, while *liberal* or *reformist* believers read them as historically shaped. *Fundamentalism* (a term from early-20th-century American Protestantism, now applied more widely) emphasizes literal readings and resistance to secular change.

**Key works and figures:**
- The Hebrew Bible/Tanakh: foundational for all three.
- The New Testament: the four Gospels (accounts of Jesus's life) and Paul's letters.
- The Quran: Islam's central text, recited in Arabic.
- Moses, Jesus, Muhammad: central figures (Muslims also honor Moses and Jesus as prophets).
- Paul of Tarsus: spread Christianity to non-Jews.
- Martin Luther (1483–1546): launched the Protestant Reformation.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: founding dates, spread, schisms, demographics; Jesus's existence and crucifixion; Muhammad's life in outline.
- Debated: the dating and authorship of many biblical books; how Islam's earliest decades unfolded [Refining]; future demographic projections.
- Matters of belief or value: God's existence, revelation, miracles, the resurrection, and which tradition (if any) is true.

**Open questions:** How will Christianity change as its majority becomes African, Latin American, and Asian? Will secularization reach Muslim-majority societies as it did Western Europe?

**See also:** 4.3 Classical Empires and Universal Religions; 4.4 The Medieval and Early Modern World; 7.1 Legal Systems; 9.8 Other Movements; 10.7 Religion's Effects on Society.

### 10.3 Religions of South and East Asia

**In one sentence:** The religions that arose in India and China, including Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism, Confucianism, Taoism, Shinto, and Chinese folk religion, are followed or practiced by roughly 2 billion people and differ from the Abrahamic model in that many have no single founder, no single creed, and no rule that a person may follow only one.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- *Karma*: the belief that a person's actions produce future consequences for that person, in this life or later ones.
- *Reincarnation* (*samsara*): the belief that beings are reborn repeatedly after death, in forms shaped by karma.
- *Liberation* (*moksha*, or *nirvana* in Buddhism): the goal, in Indian religions, of escaping the cycle of rebirth.
- *Dharma*: a word with several meanings: cosmic order, moral duty, or (in Buddhism) the Buddha's teaching.
- *Syncretism*: combining practices from several traditions. In China, Japan, and Korea, many people visit Buddhist temples, honor ancestors in Confucian style, and consult local gods, without seeing this as conflict. This makes counting followers difficult.

**How it works:**

| Religion | Approx. followers | Origin place & date | Core beliefs (as held by followers) | Key texts | Main branches |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hinduism** | ~1.2 billion (~94% in India) | Indian subcontinent; roots in Vedic religion ~1500 BCE; no single founder | One ultimate reality (*Brahman*), worshipped through many deities; karma, rebirth, liberation; duty according to one's station | Vedas (~1500–500 BCE), Upanishads, *Bhagavad Gita*, epics (*Mahabharata*, *Ramayana*) | Vaishnavism (devotion to Vishnu), Shaivism (Shiva), Shaktism (the Goddess), Smarta |
| **Buddhism** | ~320 million (higher, up to ~500 million, if East Asian part-time practice is counted) | Northeastern India, ~5th century BCE | Life involves suffering caused by craving; suffering can end (*nirvana*) through an ethical and meditative path; no permanent self | Pali Canon; Mahayana sutras; Tibetan canon | Theravada, Mahayana (incl. Zen, Pure Land), Vajrayana (Tibetan) |
| **Jainism** | ~4–6 million | India, ~6th–5th century BCE (Mahavira) | Strict non-violence (*ahimsa*) to all living things; the soul's liberation through self-discipline | Agamas (scriptures) | Digambara, Svetambara |
| **Sikhism** | ~25–30 million | Punjab (India/Pakistan), ~1500 CE | One God; equality of all people; honest work, sharing, remembrance of God | *Guru Granth Sahib* | Largely unified; Khalsa (initiated) and non-initiated Sikhs |
| **Confucianism** | Not counted as a separate membership; influences East Asia (~1.6 billion people) | China, ~500 BCE | Social harmony through moral cultivation, respect for parents (*filial piety*), ritual propriety | *Analects*; the Five Classics and Four Books | Classical; Neo-Confucianism (from ~1000 CE) |
| **Taoism (Daoism)** | Few formal members; practices widespread in Chinese societies | China, texts ~4th century BCE; organized religion ~2nd century CE | Harmony with the *Dao* ("the Way"), the natural order; simplicity; in religious Taoism, gods and practices for long life | *Tao Te Ching*; *Zhuangzi* | Philosophical and religious strands; Zhengyi and Quanzhen orders |
| **Shinto** | Most Japanese take part in rites; few identify as "religious" | Japan, prehistoric roots; first texts 712 CE | Worship of *kami* (spirits or gods in nature, places, ancestors); ritual purity | *Kojiki* (712), *Nihon Shoki* (720) | Shrine Shinto; folk and sect Shinto |
| **Chinese folk religion** | Several hundred million [highly uncertain] | China, prehistoric roots | Ancestor veneration; local gods; divination; balance of forces (yin–yang) | No central text | Highly local |

**Hinduism.** "Hindu" was originally a Persian geographic term for people beyond the Indus River; the idea of Hinduism as one religion was consolidated mainly in the 19th century [Refining]. It is internally diverse: Hindus may worship one god, many gods, or an impersonal absolute. Common practices include *puja* (offerings to images of deities at home or in temples), festivals such as Diwali, and pilgrimage to sacred rivers such as the Ganges. The *caste* system, a hereditary ranking of social groups, was justified in some Hindu texts. Caste discrimination was outlawed by India's 1950 constitution but persists [Established]. Nepal is also Hindu-majority.

**Buddhism.** Siddhartha Gautama, called the Buddha ("awakened one"), was a teacher in northeastern India; most historians accept he existed, though his dates are uncertain (~480–400 BCE is a common estimate) [Refining]. Buddhists believe he attained enlightenment and taught the *Four Noble Truths* and the *Eightfold Path*. The Indian emperor Ashoka (~3rd century BCE) promoted it; it then spread to Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, China, Korea, Japan, and Tibet, while nearly disappearing from India by ~1200 CE. Many Buddhists also worship deities, honor bodhisattvas (beings believed to delay their own liberation to help others), and make merit by giving to monks.

**Jainism.** Jains believe Mahavira was the last of 24 teachers who achieved liberation. Their non-violence is unusually strict: monks may sweep the ground to avoid stepping on insects, and Jains are vegetarian. Jains are a small but economically prominent community in India.

**Sikhism.** Founded by Guru Nanak (1469–1539) and developed by nine successor gurus. In 1699 the tenth, Gobind Singh, created the *Khalsa*, an order of initiated Sikhs who keep five symbols, including uncut hair. Sikhs regard their scripture as their eternal guru. Every gurdwara (Sikh temple) runs a *langar*, a free kitchen open to all.

**Confucianism and Taoism.** Confucius (551–479 BCE) taught ethics and government. Whether Confucianism is a religion is debated; it includes rituals for ancestors and Heaven but says little about gods. From the 2nd century BCE until 1905 it was China's state orthodoxy, enforced through the civil-service examinations. Taoism grew from texts attributed to Laozi (whose historical existence is [Contested]) and became an organized religion with priests and temples from about 142 CE.

**Shinto.** Japan has roughly 80,000 shrines. From 1868 to 1945 the state promoted "State Shinto," teaching that the emperor descended from the sun goddess; the emperor publicly renounced divine status in 1946. Many Japanese mark births at Shinto shrines and funerals with Buddhist rites.

**How it developed:** Vedic religion (~1500 BCE) → Upanishads and the rise of Buddhism and Jainism (~800–400 BCE) → Confucius and early Taoism (~500–300 BCE) → Buddhism reaches China (~1st century CE) and Japan (~6th century) → devotional Hinduism and temple-building (~500–1200 CE) → Islam enters South Asia (from ~700, ruling much of it from ~1200 to ~1700) → Sikhism (~1500) → colonial rule and reform movements (1800s) → in China, suppression of religion, especially during the Cultural Revolution (1966–76), then partial revival since ~1980.

**Effects on human life and society:** These traditions shape diet (vegetarianism is common among Hindus, Jains, and some Buddhists), family obligations, and politics. India's 1947 partition along largely Hindu–Muslim lines killed hundreds of thousands to about 2 million people [Contested] and displaced roughly 15 million. *Hindutva* (Hindu nationalism) is a major political force in India; Buddhist nationalism has fueled violence against Muslims in Myanmar and Sri Lanka. Buddhist meditation has spread worldwide in secular forms.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** Indian traditions debate whether the self is eternal (most Hindu schools) or illusory (Buddhism), and whether the ultimate reality is personal (a god) or impersonal. East Asian traditions emphasize correct conduct and ritual more than correct belief.

**Key works and figures:**
- The Rigveda (~1500–1000 BCE): among the oldest religious texts still in use.
- The *Bhagavad Gita*: dialogue on duty and devotion, Hinduism's most widely read text.
- The Buddha and the Pali Canon: foundation of Buddhist teaching.
- Confucius's *Analects* and the *Tao Te Ching*: shaped East Asian ethics for over 2,000 years.
- Guru Nanak and the *Guru Granth Sahib*: founding figure and scripture of Sikhism.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: approximate founding dates, spread, and the political history above.
- Debated: the Buddha's and Laozi's dates; how to count followers of syncretic traditions; whether Confucianism is a "religion."
- Matters of belief or value: karma, rebirth, enlightenment, the existence of kami and gods.

**Open questions:** How religious is China today, given official atheism and widespread folk practice? How will religious nationalism shape India's future?

**See also:** 4.3 Classical Empires and Universal Religions; 8.4 Social Class and Mobility (caste); 9.8 Other Movements; 10.5 Spirituality, Ritual, and Religious Experience; 11.5 World Philosophical Traditions.

### 10.4 Indigenous, Folk, and New Religions

**In one sentence:** Beyond the large organized religions are thousands of smaller traditions: the religions of indigenous peoples, everyday "folk" practices mixed into larger faiths, and new religious movements founded in the last two centuries.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- *Indigenous peoples*: groups descended from a region's inhabitants before conquest or colonization by outsiders, such as Native Americans, Aboriginal Australians, or the Sámi of northern Europe.
- *Animism*: the belief that animals, plants, rivers, mountains, and other natural things have spirits or souls. The term was coined by Edward Tylor in 1871.
- *Ancestor veneration*: honoring dead family members, who many believe continue to influence the living.
- *Shaman*: a specialist believed by their community to communicate with spirits, often in a trance, to heal or divine. The word comes from the Evenki language of Siberia.
- *New religious movement (NRM)*: a religion founded recently (usually since ~1800). Critics sometimes call such groups *cults*, a term scholars avoid because it is used loosely to mean "a religion I disapprove of."

**How it works:** Indigenous religions are usually tied to a particular people and land, passed on orally rather than through scripture, and focused on practice rather than doctrine. Common elements include spirits of place, rites marking the seasons and life stages, healing rituals, and obligations to ancestors. Many indigenous peoples do not separate "religion" from law, medicine, or kinship.

Folk religion is the layer of practice that exists alongside or inside larger religions: saints' shrines, amulets, spirit possession, divination, and local festivals. Pew estimated about 400 million followers of folk religions in 2010, most in China [highly uncertain, because many people practice both a folk and a world religion].

Examples:
- **African traditional religions**: very diverse; many include a distant creator god, lesser deities or spirits, and active ancestors. The Yoruba religion of West Africa (deities called *orisha*) traveled with enslaved Africans to the Americas, where it blended with Catholicism to form Candomblé (Brazil), Santería (Cuba), and related traditions; Haitian Vodou has similar roots. Most Africans today are Christian or Muslim, but many combine these with traditional practices.
- **Aboriginal Australian religions**: center on "the Dreaming," a body of law and story about ancestral beings who shaped the land.
- **Native American religions**: hundreds of distinct traditions; the US banned some ceremonies until a 1978 law protected them.

**New religious movements** (follower counts are mostly the groups' own claims):
- **The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints** (Mormonism): founded in New York State in 1830 by Joseph Smith. Members believe he translated the *Book of Mormon* from golden plates revealed by an angel [Belief/Value]. It reports ~17 million members.
- **Jehovah's Witnesses**: from the 1870s, US; ~9 million active members reported; known for door-to-door preaching and refusal of blood transfusions.
- **The Bahá'í Faith**: founded in Persia (Iran) by Bahá'u'lláh in the 1860s; teaches the unity of all religions; ~5–8 million [uncertain]. Bahá'ís are persecuted in Iran.
- **Scientology**: founded in the US by L. Ron Hubbard in the 1950s. The group claims millions of members; independent surveys suggest far fewer. Several governments have treated it as a commercial enterprise rather than a religion [Contested].
- Others: Cao Dai (Vietnam, 1926); Falun Gong (China, 1992; banned and persecuted there since 1999); modern Paganism and Wicca (UK, from the 1950s).

**How it developed:** Indigenous religions are the oldest forms of human religion; organized world religions grew partly by absorbing or displacing them. From the 1500s, colonizers and missionaries often suppressed indigenous practice, sometimes by force: banning ceremonies, removing children to religious boarding schools (as in Canada, the US, and Australia into the 20th century), and destroying sacred sites. Since the late 20th century, many indigenous religions have been revived and gained legal protection. New religions appear constantly; most stay small or vanish. A few end in violence: in 1978 more than 900 members of the Peoples Temple died in a mass murder-suicide at Jonestown, Guyana.

**Effects on human life and society:** Indigenous religions often shape land claims and environmental politics, because sacred sites are legally contested with mining and building projects. Missionary suppression is a major source of indigenous grievance today. New religions test how far states protect religious freedom.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** Early anthropologists ranked religions from "primitive" animism to "advanced" monotheism; this ladder model is now rejected as unsupported. Debate continues on whether "cults" use manipulative "brainwashing" [Contested; most sociologists of religion find the strong version unsupported].

**Key works and figures:**
- *Primitive Culture* (Tylor, 1871): introduced the concept of animism.
- *The Golden Bough* (James Frazer, 1890): influential but now outdated comparative survey of myth and magic.
- *Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic among the Azande* (E. E. Evans-Pritchard, 1937): showed that magical beliefs follow their own internal logic.
- Joseph Smith and the *Book of Mormon* (1830): the most successful new religion founded in modern times.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: the historical suppression of indigenous religions; founding dates of new movements.
- Debated: membership figures of most new and folk religions; the "cult" concept.
- Matters of belief or value: the existence of spirits, the powers of shamans, and the revelations claimed by founders.

**Open questions:** How many indigenous religious traditions have been lost, and how many survive? Which new religions of today will become major traditions?

**See also:** 4.5 Colonialism, Revolutions, and Industry; 8.3 Race, Ethnicity, and Identity; 14.1 What Culture Is; 14.2 Storytelling and Literature; 10.1 What Religion Is and Why Humans Have It.

### 10.5 Spirituality, Ritual, and Religious Experience

**In one sentence:** Beyond belief and institutions, religion is something humans *do* and *feel*: prayer, meditation, pilgrimage, fasting, and experiences that people describe as contact with a sacred or transcendent reality.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- *Prayer*: speaking (aloud or silently) to a god or spirit, to praise, thank, confess, or ask.
- *Meditation*: training attention, for example by focusing on the breath or a repeated phrase (*mantra*), often aiming at calm or insight.
- *Pilgrimage*: a journey to a place believed to be sacred.
- *Fasting*: going without food (or some foods) for religious reasons.
- *Mystical experience*: an experience that the person describes as direct contact with God, ultimate reality, or the unity of all things.
- *Spirituality*: a loose term for personal practices and experiences of meaning or transcendence, with or without a religion.

**How it works:** Scale gives a sense of importance:
- Muslims pray five times a day; about 2 million make the hajj to Mecca in a typical year.
- Hundreds of millions of Muslims fast during the daylight hours of Ramadan; many Christians fast or give something up during Lent; Jews fast on Yom Kippur.
- India's Kumbh Mela, a Hindu bathing festival held every few years at sacred rivers, is among the largest human gatherings; authorities reported tens of millions of bathers on peak days of the 2025 festival [counts are official estimates].
- In the US, roughly 4 in 10 adults say they pray daily [Refining; Pew surveys].

*Ritual* has features researchers observe across cultures: rigid sequences, repetition, and actions with no obvious practical purpose. Performing rituals together, especially with synchronized movement, singing, or shared hardship, is associated with stronger group bonding and trust [Refining]. Rituals are also linked to reduced anxiety in uncertain situations [Refining].

**What science observes.** Researchers can measure what happens in the body and brain during religious practice. They cannot measure whether the reported object of the experience (God, a spirit) exists.
- Brain imaging of meditating monks and praying nuns shows changes in attention and self-related brain networks [Refining].
- Certain epilepsies of the temporal lobe sometimes produce intense religious feelings [Refining].
- *Psychedelic drugs* (such as psilocybin, from certain mushrooms) can reliably produce experiences that participants describe as mystical; in controlled studies since 2006, many rated them among the most meaningful of their lives [Refining].
- Randomized trials find that meditation programs have small-to-moderate benefits for anxiety, depression, and pain [Refining].

**How it developed:** Ritual is probably older than any surviving religion (see 10.1). Monastic traditions, in which people withdraw from ordinary life to pray or meditate, arose in India by ~500 BCE and in Christianity by ~300 CE. In the 20th century, Asian meditation spread to the West; from the 1970s, "mindfulness," a secular form of Buddhist meditation, entered medicine, schools, and workplaces. The label "spiritual but not religious" became common in the West from the 1990s; in a 2017 Pew survey, about a quarter of US adults described themselves that way.

**Effects on human life and society:** Religious festivals structure calendars and economies (the Christmas shopping season, Ramadan's effect on working hours, pilgrimage tourism worth billions of dollars to Saudi Arabia). Shared ritual binds communities. Crowd crushes at pilgrimages have killed thousands over the decades (at the 2015 hajj, Saudi Arabia reported about 770 deaths; independent counts exceeded 2,000). Extreme fasting or ascetic practice can harm health.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- *Perennialism*: mystical experiences in all religions share a common core, perhaps reflecting one reality [Belief/Value; the "common core" claim about experiences is Contested].
- *Constructivism*: experiences are shaped by the believer's culture and expectations; a Catholic sees the Virgin Mary, a Hindu sees Krishna [Refining].
- *Neurological explanations*: experiences are fully produced by brain states [Belief/Value as a complete account]. Believers often reply that a brain correlate does not show the experience is false, just as seeing a tree involves the brain without the tree being imaginary [Belief/Value].

**Key works and figures:**
- *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (William James, 1902): described mystical experience as ineffable, felt as knowledge, brief, and passive.
- *The Idea of the Holy* (Rudolf Otto, 1917): described the sacred as a mystery that both terrifies and attracts.
- Teresa of Ávila, Rumi, and Shankara: mystics in Christian, Islamic (Sufi), and Hindu traditions whose writings are still widely read.
- Jon Kabat-Zinn: founded "mindfulness-based stress reduction" (1979), bringing meditation into medicine.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: these practices are widespread; they have measurable effects on mood, attention, and group bonding.
- Debated: the size of meditation's health benefits; whether mystical experiences share a cross-cultural core.
- Matters of belief or value: whether such experiences put humans in contact with anything beyond the brain.

**Open questions:** Why do some people have intense religious experiences and others never do? What exactly do rituals do to group trust, and how long does the effect last?
