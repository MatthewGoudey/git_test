# Domain 12: Technology and Infrastructure

**Technology** is the set of tools, techniques, and systems humans use to change their surroundings to suit their needs. **Infrastructure** is the large, mostly shared technology a society runs on: farms, power plants, roads, pipes, cables, and hospitals. This domain explains how humans feed themselves, power their machines, make and move things, process information, keep themselves healthy, and fight. These systems are the main reason a human born today can expect to live about 73 years, compared with about 30 in 1800. They are also the main source of the planet-scale harms described in Domain 16.

---

### 12.1 Tools and How Technology Changes

**In one sentence:** Humans make and improve tools, share the improvements, and build on them, so their technology grows over time in a way no other species' does.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- An **invention** is a new device or method. An **innovation** is an invention that comes into practical use. **Diffusion** is its spread to other users and places. Most of a technology's effect comes from diffusion, which often takes decades.
- **Productivity** means output per hour of work. Rising productivity is the main reason average human incomes have risen.
- A **general-purpose technology** is one that changes many industries at once, such as the steam engine, electricity, or the computer.
- **Research and development (R&D)** is organized, paid work to create new technology. A **patent** is a legal right, usually for about 20 years, to stop others from copying an invention (see 7.4).

**How it works:** Technology is **cumulative**. Each new tool combines older tools and ideas. A smartphone relies on mathematics from the 1600s, electromagnetism from the 1800s, transistors from 1947, and satellites from the 1960s. Because knowledge can be copied cheaply, an idea can be used by many people at once.

Humans today spend about **US$2.5–3 trillion a year on R&D**, roughly 2–3% of world economic output. Most of it is spent by companies, and most of it takes place in a few countries. The United States and China together account for roughly half.

Diffusion is uneven. Electricity was in commercial use by the 1880s, but about 700–750 million people still lack it. Mobile phones spread much faster: from rare in 1995 to roughly 5–6 billion users in the 2020s.

**How it developed:**
- **~3.3 million years ago:** oldest known stone tools, made by human ancestors before *Homo sapiens* existed.
- **~1 million to 400,000 years ago:** controlled use of fire becomes common [Refining on exact dates].
- **~12,000 years ago:** farming begins (12.2), allowing permanent settlements and specialist craftspeople.
- **~5,000–3,000 years ago:** metalworking, the wheel, and writing (13.2).
- **~1450:** the printing press in Europe speeds up the copying of knowledge.
- **~1760–1840:** the **Industrial Revolution** in Britain: machines powered by coal replace much human and animal muscle (see 4.5).
- **1870–1970:** electricity, the internal combustion engine, chemicals, telephones, and antibiotics transform rich countries.
- **1950s onward:** computing and digital technology (12.6).

Before about 1800, average income per person grew very slowly for most of history. Since then, world average income per person has risen roughly **10–15 times** [Refining on exact multiple].

**Effects on human life and society:** Technology underlies longer lives, the growth of cities, mass literacy, global trade, and modern weapons. It also changes which jobs exist. Around 1800 most humans farmed. Today about a quarter do. Each wave of new technology has destroyed some kinds of work and created others.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Why innovation sped up after ~1750** [Contested]. One view stresses culture and institutions: a European "culture of growth" that prized useful knowledge and protected property (Joel Mokyr). Another stresses economics: British wages were high and coal was cheap, so machines paid off (Robert Allen). A third stresses luck and resources: coal deposits and colonial land (Kenneth Pomeranz). Most historians think several causes combined.
- **Technological determinism** says technology drives social change. **Social construction of technology** says social choices shape which technologies are built and how they are used. Most scholars accept elements of both.
- **Is innovation slowing?** [Contested]. Robert Gordon argues the great inventions of 1870–1970 were a one-time event. Others point to computing, AI, and biotechnology as evidence of continued rapid change.

**Key works and figures:**
- Joseph Schumpeter, *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy* (1942): introduced "creative destruction," in which new technology destroys old industries.
- Everett Rogers, *Diffusion of Innovations* (1962): standard model of how new technologies spread.
- Paul Romer, "Endogenous Technological Change" (1990): modeled ideas as the engine of long-run economic growth.
- Joel Mokyr, *The Lever of Riches* (1990) and *A Culture of Growth* (2016): why innovation took off in Europe.
- Robert Gordon, *The Rise and Fall of American Growth* (2016): argues the most transformative inventions may be behind us.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: technology is cumulative; productivity growth since 1800 is unprecedented; diffusion is slow and uneven.
- Debated: why the Industrial Revolution happened when and where it did; whether innovation is slowing; how far technology versus institutions explains wealth differences between countries.
- Matters of belief or value: whether technological change is "progress"; how its gains and harms should be shared.

**Open questions:** Can innovation keep accelerating as easy discoveries are used up? Will AI speed up invention itself? Why do some societies adopt technology quickly and others slowly?

**See also:** 4.5 Colonialism, Revolutions, and Industry; 5.3 Labor, Capital, and Firms; 5.4 Growth, Development, and Poverty; 7.4 Property, Contracts, and Commercial Law.

### 12.2 Agriculture and Food Systems

**In one sentence:** Humans grow nearly all their food by controlling the breeding and growth of a small number of plant and animal species, and then move that food through long chains of processing and trade.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- Humans need roughly **2,000–2,500 kilocalories** of food energy a day, plus protein, fats, vitamins, and minerals (see 1.3). They cannot digest grass or wood.
- **Agriculture** is the deliberate growing of plants (**crops**) and raising of animals (**livestock**).
- **Domestication** is the slow reshaping of wild species, by choosing which individuals breed, until they depend on human care. Domesticated wheat, for example, keeps its seeds on the stalk instead of scattering them.
- **Yield** is harvest per unit of land, e.g., tonnes per hectare (10,000 square meters).
- **Fertilizer** adds nutrients to soil, chiefly **nitrogen**, **phosphorus**, and **potassium**. Plants cannot use the nitrogen gas that makes up most of the air.
- A **food system** is everything between soil and stomach: farming, processing, transport, retail, cooking, and waste.

**How it works:** **Rice, wheat, and maize (corn)** supply about **40–50%** of all calories humans eat. Humans grow about **3 billion tonnes** of cereal grain a year, much of it fed to animals or turned into fuel rather than eaten directly.

| Fact (mid-2020s, approximate) | Value |
|---|---|
| Share of Earth's habitable (non-ice, non-desert) land used for farming | ~45–50% |
| Of farmland, share used for livestock (pasture plus animal feed crops) | ~75–80% |
| Share of calories supplied by livestock products | ~18% |
| Share of human freshwater withdrawals used by agriculture | ~70% |
| Land animals slaughtered for food per year | ~80 billion (mostly chickens) |
| Workers in agriculture | ~850–900 million, about a quarter of the global workforce |
| Share of human greenhouse gas emissions from the food system | roughly a quarter to a third |
| People chronically undernourished | ~670–730 million |

Modern high-yield farming rests on four inputs:
1. **Improved seeds**, bred for high yield and short stalks.
2. **Synthetic nitrogen fertilizer**, made by the **Haber–Bosch process** (1909–1913), which pulls nitrogen from the air using natural gas and high pressure. Researchers estimate that **roughly 40–50% of humans alive today** could not be fed without it [Refining].
3. **Irrigation**, about 20% of cropland, producing roughly 40% of food.
4. **Machinery and pesticides**, which replaced most farm labor in rich countries. In the United States, under 2% of workers farm.

About a fifth of food calories cross a national border. Roughly **13% of food is lost** between harvest and shops, and **17–19% is wasted** in shops and homes [Refining; estimates vary].

**How it developed:**
- **~10,000–12,000 years ago:** farming arises independently in at least 7–11 places, including the Fertile Crescent (Middle East), China, New Guinea, Mesoamerica, the Andes, and the Sahel (see 4.2).
- **After 1492:** crops cross oceans; maize, potatoes, and cassava spread from the Americas to every continent.
- **1909–1913:** Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch invent industrial ammonia synthesis.
- **1940s–1970s:** the **Green Revolution** spreads high-yield wheat and rice, fertilizer, and irrigation through Mexico and Asia. Norman Borlaug receives the Nobel Peace Prize (1970).
- **1996 onward:** **genetically modified (GM) crops**, with DNA altered directly in the laboratory, are planted widely in the Americas; **gene editing** (CRISPR, 2012) follows.

Since 1960, world cereal yields have roughly **tripled** while population grew about 2.7 times.

**Effects on human life and society:** Farming made cities, states, and writing possible (Domain 4). Rising yields are the main reason the undernourished share of humans fell from roughly a third around 1970 to under a tenth today.

**Famine** (mass death from lack of food) has become much rarer. Major 20th-century famines include the Soviet famines (1921–22, 1932–33), Bengal (1943, ~2–3 million deaths), and China's Great Leap Forward (1959–61, estimates ~15–45 million deaths). Since 1950, most famines have occurred during war or under governments that suppressed information. In the mid-2020s, acute hunger is concentrated in conflict zones such as Sudan and Gaza.

Meanwhile about **2.5 billion adults are overweight** (see 1.6), and farming is the largest driver of habitat loss and nutrient pollution (16.2).

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Malthusianism:** Thomas Malthus (1798) predicted population would outgrow food. So far yields have kept ahead; whether environmental limits will eventually bind is [Contested].
- **Sen's entitlement approach:** famines usually come from people losing the means to buy or grow food, not from total shortage. Amartya Sen also argued that functioning democracies with a free press do not suffer famines [largely supported; edge cases debated].
- **Intensification vs. agroecology:** raise yields on existing land to spare land for nature, or farm diversely with fewer inputs? Organic farming yields **roughly 10–25% less** per hectare [Refining], so it trades chemical use against land area.
- **GM crops:** major scientific bodies (e.g., US National Academies, 2016) find approved GM foods as safe to eat as conventional ones. Disputes continue over seed ownership, herbicide use, and ecology.
- **Meat:** whether humans should eat less of it for environmental, health, or animal-welfare reasons mixes [Contested] evidence with [Belief/Value].

**Key works and figures:**
- Thomas Malthus, *An Essay on the Principle of Population* (1798): the classic warning about food and population.
- Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch: industrial nitrogen fixation, arguably the most important single technology for feeding today's population.
- Norman Borlaug: plant breeder central to the Green Revolution.
- Amartya Sen, *Poverty and Famines* (1981): reframed famine as a problem of access, not just supply.
- Vaclav Smil, *Enriching the Earth* (2001): the history and scale of synthetic fertilizer.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: modern yields depend on synthetic fertilizer, improved seeds, and irrigation; food supply per person has risen since 1960; hunger persists mainly due to poverty and conflict, not global shortage.
- Debated: how to feed ~10 billion people while cutting emissions and land use; the best mix of high-input and low-input farming; long-run effects of climate change on yields.
- Matters of belief or value: animal welfare; vegetarianism and religious food rules (see 14.7); whether food should be treated as a market good or a right.

**Open questions:** Can yields keep rising as climate warms? Can the nitrogen and phosphorus cycles be managed without polluting water? Will laboratory-grown meat or precision fermentation become significant food sources? How much farmland could be returned to nature?

**See also:** 1.3 The Body and Its Needs; 4.2 Farming and the First Civilizations; 8.5 Population and Migration; 16.1 Climate Change; 16.2 Biodiversity and Environmental Degradation; 16.7 Resources: Food, Water, and Materials.

### 12.3 Energy

**In one sentence:** Humans power their bodies, homes, machines, and industries by capturing energy, mostly by burning the fossilized remains of ancient life, and how much energy a society can use largely sets how rich it can become.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Energy** is the capacity to do work, such as moving or heating things (see 2.1). It is measured in **joules**; an **exajoule (EJ)** is 10^18 joules. **Power** is energy per second, measured in **watts**. A **terawatt-hour (TWh)** is a trillion watts sustained for one hour.
- A human body runs on about **100 watts**. The average human today commands roughly **2,300 watts** from outside sources, about 20 bodies' worth; residents of rich countries command several times that.
- **Fossil fuels** (coal, oil, natural gas) formed over millions of years from buried plants and microorganisms. Burning them releases energy and **carbon dioxide (CO2)**, the main gas driving climate change (2.5, 16.1).
- **Primary energy** is energy in raw form (crude oil, coal, sunlight). **Electricity** is a *carrier* made from primary sources and sent along wires through an **electricity grid**.
- **Renewable** sources (sunlight, wind, flowing water, plant matter) replenish naturally. **Nuclear** power comes from splitting heavy atoms such as uranium (**fission**).

**How it works:** Humans use about **600–650 EJ of primary energy a year**. That equals roughly **18–20 trillion watts** of continuous power. About **80% still comes from fossil fuels**.

| Source | Approx. share of world primary energy (mid-2020s) | Main uses |
|---|---|---|
| Oil | ~30–32% | Transport fuel (cars, trucks, ships, aircraft), plastics and chemicals |
| Coal | ~25–27% | Electricity, steel, cement; China and India are the largest users |
| Natural gas | ~23% | Electricity, heating, industry, fertilizer |
| Hydropower | ~6% | Electricity |
| Nuclear | ~4% | Electricity |
| Wind | ~3% | Electricity |
| Solar | ~2–3% | Electricity (fastest-growing source) |
| Other (geothermal, modern bioenergy) | ~1–2% | Electricity, heat, fuels |

*Shares follow one common accounting convention (the Energy Institute's "substitution method"); others give lower shares to non-fossil sources. Another ~5–10% of world energy comes from traditional **biomass** (wood, dung, crop waste) burned in poorer households, often left out of such tables.*

**Electricity** is about 20% of final energy use and rising. Humans generate about **30,000 TWh a year**: roughly 35% from coal, 22% gas, 14% hydropower, 9% nuclear, 8% wind, and 7% solar. Low-carbon sources supply about **40%**.

Sources differ in key ways:
- **Energy density:** oil holds about 45 million joules per kilogram, which is why it dominates transport. Lithium batteries hold roughly 1/50th to 1/100th as much.
- **Dispatchability:** coal, gas, hydro, and nuclear plants produce on demand. Wind and solar are **variable** and need storage, backup, or wide grids.
- **Cost:** solar panel prices fell roughly **90% from 2010 to 2020**. Solar and onshore wind are now among the cheapest new electricity in most places.
- **Safety:** counting accidents and air pollution, coal and oil cause by far the most deaths per unit of energy; nuclear, wind, and solar the fewest [Established ranking; exact figures vary].

About **700–750 million people** have no electricity, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa. About **2 billion** cook with polluting fuels, and the indoor smoke is a major cause of death.

**How it developed:**
- **Before ~1800:** nearly all energy came from muscle, wood, and some wind and water power.
- **1712–1780s:** steam engines (Thomas Newcomen, then James Watt) turn coal's heat into motion.
- **1859:** first commercial oil well (Pennsylvania, USA). Oil later dominates transport.
- **1880s–90s:** central power stations (Thomas Edison, 1882); alternating current (Nikola Tesla, George Westinghouse) allows long-distance transmission.
- **1954–57:** first grid-connected nuclear plants (USSR, UK, USA). Accidents at **Chernobyl** (1986) and **Fukushima** (2011) later slowed nuclear building.
- **1973 and 1979:** **oil shocks**: supply cuts by exporting states cause price spikes and recessions.
- **2010s–2020s:** rapid growth of solar, wind, batteries, and electric cars. By the mid-2020s, roughly twice as much was invested each year in low-carbon energy as in fossil fuels.

Total energy use has risen roughly **25-fold since 1800** and still grows about 1–2% a year.

**Effects on human life and society:**
- **Wealth:** energy use per person tracks income closely. No country has become rich without large energy use.
- **Climate:** fossil fuels cause roughly **three-quarters of human greenhouse gas emissions**; fossil CO2 is about **37–38 billion tonnes a year** (16.1).
- **Health:** air pollution, mostly from burning fuels, is linked to roughly **6–8 million deaths a year** [Refining].
- **Geopolitics:** oil and gas have shaped wars, alliances, and the wealth of states such as Saudi Arabia and Russia. Europe's reliance on Russian gas became a crisis after Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine. China dominates production of solar panels, batteries, and refined critical minerals.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **The energy transition** (from fossil fuels to low-carbon sources) is endorsed in principle by almost all governments (Paris Agreement, 2015). **Its pace is [Contested]**: advocates of speed cite falling costs and climate damage; skeptics cite reliability, consumer costs, hard-to-electrify industries (steel, cement, shipping, aviation), and poor countries' needs.
- **Renewables-only vs. nuclear-inclusive:** Germany closed its last reactors in 2023; France and China rely on or expand nuclear. The dispute turns on cost, build time, waste, and accident risk.
- **Peak oil:** 2000s predictions that geology would soon cap supply proved wrong once **fracking** (cracking rock with high-pressure fluid) opened new reserves. The live debate is when *demand* will peak [Contested; estimates from the late 2020s to after 2040].
- **Decoupling:** many rich countries have grown their economies while cutting emissions since ~2005; whether this can happen fast enough worldwide is [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- James Watt: improved steam engine (patent 1769), symbol of the industrial energy shift.
- Thomas Edison and Nikola Tesla: central power generation and alternating-current systems.
- Daniel Yergin, *The Prize* (1990): history of oil in politics and war.
- Vaclav Smil, *Energy and Civilization* (2017): energy as the basis of human history.
- International Energy Agency (IEA), *World Energy Outlook* (annual): the most cited global energy projections.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: fossil fuels supply ~80% of energy; their combustion is the main cause of recent warming; solar and wind costs have fallen dramatically; energy use and prosperity are strongly linked.
- Debated: how quickly fossil fuels can be replaced; the role of nuclear power; how to run grids with very high shares of variable renewables; future oil and gas demand.
- Matters of belief or value: how to balance climate harm against affordable energy for poorer people; how much risk from nuclear power is acceptable; who should pay for the transition.

**Open questions:** Can grids run reliably on mostly wind and solar? Will cheap long-duration storage, advanced nuclear, or nuclear **fusion** (joining light atoms, which has produced net energy only in brief laboratory experiments since 2022) become practical? Can heavy industry and aviation be decarbonized affordably?

**See also:** 2.1 Physics; 2.5 Climate and Atmosphere; 5.7 International Trade and the Global Economy; 6.5 International Relations and Diplomacy; 16.1 Climate Change; 16.7 Resources: Food, Water, and Materials.

### 12.4 Manufacturing and Materials

**In one sentence:** Manufacturing is the conversion of raw materials into finished goods, now done mostly in large factories linked by global supply chains.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Raw materials** are things taken from nature: ores (rocks containing metals), oil, sand, wood, cotton.
- **Mass production** means making very large numbers of identical items using standardized parts, specialized machines, and divided labor.
- A **supply chain** is the sequence of firms and places a product passes through, from raw material to buyer. One phone may contain parts from dozens of countries.
- A **semiconductor** is a material, usually silicon, whose ability to conduct electricity can be precisely controlled. A **chip** (integrated circuit) is a small piece of semiconductor carrying billions of tiny switches called **transistors** (see 12.6).

**How it works:** Manufacturing produces roughly **16% of world economic output** (about US$16 trillion of value added a year). **China** produces roughly **30%** of the world's manufactured value, more than the United States, Japan, and Germany combined.

Four materials underpin modern physical life (Vaclav Smil's "four pillars"):

| Material | Approx. yearly production | Main uses |
|---|---|---|
| Cement (for concrete) | ~4 billion tonnes | Buildings, roads, dams |
| Steel | ~1.9 billion tonnes (over half in China) | Buildings, vehicles, machines |
| Plastics | ~400–450 million tonnes | Packaging, consumer goods, construction |
| Ammonia | ~180–190 million tonnes | Fertilizer (12.2), explosives, chemicals |

Making cement and steel releases roughly **7–8%** of human CO2 emissions each, mainly from coal and from chemical reactions. Only about **10% of plastic** ever made has been recycled. Most is buried, burned, or lost into the environment.

About **4 million industrial robots** work in factories, over half in China, Japan, and South Korea. Advanced chips are the most demanding product humans make: one chip factory can cost US$20 billion or more. The most advanced chips are made almost entirely in **Taiwan** (mainly by TSMC), using machines from one Dutch firm (ASML), a concentration governments treat as a strategic risk.

**How it developed:**
- **~3300 BCE:** bronze (copper plus tin). **~1200 BCE:** iron working spreads.
- **Before 1700:** goods are made by hand by **artisans**; India and China lead world textile production.
- **1760s–1830s:** mechanized textile mills in Britain; the **factory system** gathers workers around powered machines.
- **1856:** the **Bessemer process** makes steel cheap. **1907:** Bakelite, the first fully synthetic plastic.
- **1913:** Henry Ford's moving assembly line cuts the time to build a car from about 12 hours to about 1.5.
- **1950s–1980s:** the **Toyota Production System** ("lean" and "just-in-time" manufacturing) reduces waste and inventory.
- **1980s–2000s:** production moves from rich countries to East Asia, especially after China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001.
- **2020–2022:** the COVID-19 pandemic exposes fragile supply chains; governments begin subsidizing domestic chip and battery factories.

**Effects on human life and society:** Mass production made clothing, cars, and electronics affordable to billions. Factory jobs drew hundreds of millions of people from farms to cities, first in Europe and North America, then in East Asia. In rich countries manufacturing's share of jobs has fallen (in the US, from about 30% in the 1950s to about 8%). Automation and import competition (especially from China after 2001) both contributed; their relative weight is [Contested]. Losses were concentrated in particular regions, with lasting local and political effects.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Division of labor:** Adam Smith (1776) argued that splitting work into specialized tasks massively raises output.
- **Critiques of factory work:** Karl Marx and later writers argued factory work alienates workers and concentrates gains with owners (see 9.4).
- **Industrial policy** is government support for particular industries. It is back in favor in the US, EU, and China after decades of skepticism. Whether it works is [Contested]: East Asian success stories are cited for it, and costly failures against it.
- **Reshoring vs. globalization:** whether to bring production home for security, or keep efficient global chains [Contested; see 5.7].

**Key works and figures:**
- Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776): the pin-factory example of specialization.
- Henry Ford: the moving assembly line and the mass-market car.
- Taiichi Ohno: architect of the Toyota Production System.
- Vaclav Smil, *How the World Really Works* (2022): material foundations of modern life.
- Chris Miller, *Chip War* (2022): the history and geopolitics of semiconductors.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: manufacturing output is concentrated in East Asia; cement and steel are major emission sources; advanced chip production is highly concentrated.
- Debated: the relative roles of trade and automation in factory job losses; whether industrial policy works; how to make steel and cement without emissions.
- Matters of belief or value: national self-sufficiency versus cheaper goods; working conditions acceptable in poorer countries.

**Open questions:** Can steel, cement, and plastics be made without fossil fuels at scale? Will AI and robotics cause another large shift in where goods are made? How resilient are supply chains to war, especially around Taiwan?

**See also:** 5.3 Labor, Capital, and Firms; 5.7 International Trade and the Global Economy; 12.6 Computing, the Internet, and Artificial Intelligence; 16.2 Biodiversity and Environmental Degradation.

### 12.5 Transportation

**In one sentence:** Humans move themselves and their goods using vehicles powered first by muscle and wind, and now mostly by oil-burning engines, with electricity a fast-growing alternative.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- Before engines, most humans rarely traveled more than a day's walk (~30 km) from where they were born. Goods moved by human porters, pack animals, river boats, and sailing ships.
- **Freight** means goods being transported. Freight is measured in tonne-kilometers (one tonne moved one kilometer).
- An **internal combustion engine** burns fuel inside a cylinder to push a piston. It powers nearly all cars, trucks, ships, and propeller aircraft. A **jet engine** compresses air, burns fuel in it, and expels the hot gas backward.
- A **shipping container** is a standard steel box (usually 20 or 40 feet long) that can be moved between ship, train, and truck without unpacking.

**How it works:** Transport uses roughly **a quarter of all final energy** and over 90% of it still comes from oil. It produces about **a fifth to a quarter of energy-related CO2**.

| Mode | Scale (mid-2020s, approximate) |
|---|---|
| Road vehicles | ~1.5 billion cars, trucks, and buses in use |
| Road deaths | ~1.2 million a year, the leading killer of humans aged 5–29 |
| Air travel | ~4.5–5 billion passenger trips a year; ~2–2.5% of CO2 emissions |
| Sea shipping | ~11–12 billion tonnes of cargo a year; carries ~80% of world trade by volume |
| Rail | Over 1 million km of track; China has ~45,000+ km of high-speed rail, more than the rest of the world combined |
| Electric cars | ~20% of new car sales in 2024 (over half in China) |

Shipping is extremely cheap per unit. Moving a container across the Pacific adds only a few cents to the price of a pair of shoes. This is a foundation of global trade (5.7).

**How it developed:**
- **~3500 BCE:** the wheel and wheeled carts in Mesopotamia and nearby regions. Horses were domesticated around the same era.
- **Ancient to 1400s:** sailing ships; road networks such as Rome's (~80,000 km of paved roads) and the Inca's; the Silk Road caravan routes.
- **1400s–1700s:** ocean-going sailing ships connect all continents (see 4.5).
- **1825–1830:** first public steam railways in England. By 1900 railways spanned continents.
- **1886:** Karl Benz's motor car. **1908:** Ford's Model T makes cars affordable to many workers.
- **1903:** the Wright brothers' first powered, controlled airplane flight. **1950s:** jet airliners.
- **1956:** Malcom McLean ships the first containers. Containerization cuts loading costs by over 90% by the 1970s.
- **1957:** Sputnik, the first artificial satellite. **1961:** Yuri Gagarin, first human in space. **1969:** Apollo 11, first humans on the Moon.
- **2010s–2020s:** electric cars and reusable rockets (first booster landing in 2015). Over 10,000 active satellites orbit Earth, most of them for internet service.

**Effects on human life and society:** Cheap transport created global trade, mass tourism, suburbs, and migration on a large scale. Cities were reshaped around cars, especially in North America. Transport also spreads disease: COVID-19 crossed the world within weeks in 2020. Road traffic kills about as many people a year as some major diseases, mostly in low- and middle-income countries.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Car-centered vs. transit-centered planning** [Contested; partly Belief/Value]: whether cities should be built around private cars or around walking, cycling, and public transport.
- **Induced demand:** building more road capacity tends to generate more traffic rather than relieve congestion [Established in many studies].
- **Electrification and alternative fuels:** electric cars are now mainstream. Trucks, ships, and aircraft are harder, and options (batteries, hydrogen, synthetic fuels) are [Contested].
- **Space:** whether private companies or governments should lead spaceflight, and whether humans should settle other planets [Belief/Value].

**Key works and figures:**
- George Stephenson: early steam railways.
- Henry Ford: mass-produced car.
- Wilbur and Orville Wright: first powered flight.
- Marc Levinson, *The Box* (2006): how the shipping container remade the world economy.
- Sergei Korolev and Wernher von Braun: chief rocket designers of the Soviet and US space programs.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: oil dominates transport; container shipping slashed trade costs; road crashes are a major cause of death.
- Debated: how fast freight and aviation can be decarbonized; the future role of self-driving vehicles.
- Matters of belief or value: how cities should be designed; the purpose of human spaceflight.

**Open questions:** Will autonomous vehicles become widespread and safe? Can aviation become low-carbon? Will humans establish permanent settlements beyond Earth?

**See also:** 5.7 International Trade and the Global Economy; 8.6 Cities and Settlement; 12.3 Energy; 16.1 Climate Change.

### 12.6 Computing, the Internet, and Artificial Intelligence

**In one sentence:** Humans have built machines that store, process, and transmit information automatically, connected about 6 billion people through a global network, and recently built software that can perform many tasks once thought to require human intelligence.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Information** can be encoded as **bits**, choices between two states written 0 and 1 (Claude Shannon, 1948). Any text, image, or sound can be turned into bits.
- A **computer** follows a list of instructions (a **program**, or **software**) to transform information. An **algorithm** is a precise step-by-step procedure for solving a problem.
- A **transistor** is a tiny electrical switch with no moving parts. Billions of them on one **chip** compute by switching on and off billions of times a second.
- The **internet** is a worldwide network of networks that exchange data using shared rules (**protocols**). The **World Wide Web** is one service on it: linked documents ("pages"). Email and most apps use the internet without being "the web."
- **Artificial intelligence (AI)** is making computers do tasks such as recognizing images, translating, or reasoning. In **machine learning**, the dominant approach, a system is not given rules; it adjusts billions of internal numbers (**parameters**) to fit patterns in large amounts of **data**.

**How it works:**
- **Chips:** the most advanced chips in the mid-2020s hold tens of billions of transistors, each with features only a few nanometers (billionths of a meter) across. **Moore's law**, an observation by Gordon Moore (1965), held that the number of transistors per chip doubles about every two years. It held for about five decades and has slowed since the 2010s.
- **Scale of computing:** about **5 billion people** use smartphones, each far more powerful than the computers used for the 1969 Moon landing. Large **data centers** (buildings full of servers) run the internet's services and used roughly **1.5% of world electricity** in 2024, a share rising fast with AI [Refining].
- **The internet:** data is broken into small **packets** that travel independently and are reassembled at their destination. About **6 billion people (~70%)** were online by 2025. Most long-distance traffic travels through roughly **500 undersea fiber-optic cables**, not satellites.
- **Modern AI:** today's leading systems are mostly **neural networks**, loosely inspired by brain cells (the analogy is imperfect: they are mathematical functions, not biological tissue). **Large language models (LLMs)** are trained on trillions of words of text to predict the next piece of a text. Through this training they acquire a broad, though uneven, ability to answer questions, write, summarize, translate, and write computer code. Most are based on the **transformer** design (2017). Training a leading model in the mid-2020s requires tens of thousands of specialized chips running for months, at a cost of hundreds of millions of US dollars or more. The computing power used for the largest training runs has grown roughly **4–5 times per year** since about 2010 [Refining].

**How it developed:**
- **1830s:** Charles Babbage designs a mechanical general-purpose computer (never completed); Ada Lovelace writes what is often called the first program for it.
- **1936:** Alan Turing defines a theoretical "universal machine."
- **1940s:** first electronic computers, built for code-breaking and weapons work (Colossus 1943–44, ENIAC 1945). John von Neumann describes the stored-program design still used.
- **1947:** transistor (Bell Labs). **1958–59:** integrated circuit. **1971:** first commercial microprocessor.
- **1956:** "artificial intelligence" named at a workshop at Dartmouth College, USA.
- **1969:** ARPANET, a US military-funded network, sends its first message; it adopts the TCP/IP protocols in 1983.
- **1989–1991:** Tim Berners-Lee creates the World Wide Web. **2007:** the iPhone popularizes the smartphone.
- **2012:** a neural network (AlexNet) leaps ahead at image recognition, starting the **deep learning** boom. **2017:** the transformer.
- **November 2022:** ChatGPT reaches about 100 million users in two months. By 2025, AI chatbots had hundreds of millions of weekly users.

**Effects on human life and society:**
- **Economy:** most of the world's most valuable companies in the mid-2020s are computing firms (e.g., Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Alphabet/Google, Amazon, Meta).
- **Communication:** near-free, instant global contact for most humans (13.4).
- **Surveillance:** states and companies can analyze data on billions of people; some governments use this for mass surveillance and censorship (6.4).
- **Access gap:** about **2.5 billion people** remain offline, mostly in poorer and rural areas.
- AI's effects on jobs, science, and risk are covered in 16.5.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Open vs. concentrated:** the internet was built on open protocols, but activity has concentrated on a few platforms. Whether this harms competition is [Contested] and is being litigated in the US and EU.
- **Internet governance:** one global internet, or national internets under state control (China's "Great Firewall" is the largest example).
- **AI trajectory** [Contested]: some researchers expect systems matching or exceeding humans at most intellectual tasks within years to decades; others argue current methods have fundamental limits (see 16.5).
- **Privacy** [Belief/Value]: whether personal data is property, a right, or a resource; laws differ widely (e.g., the EU's General Data Protection Regulation, 2018).

**Key works and figures:**
- Alan Turing, "On Computable Numbers" (1936) and "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950): foundations of computing and of the question "can machines think?"
- Claude Shannon, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" (1948): founded information theory and defined the bit.
- John von Neumann: the stored-program computer design.
- Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn: designers of TCP/IP. Tim Berners-Lee: inventor of the World Wide Web.
- Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio, and Yann LeCun: pioneers of deep learning (Turing Award, 2018).
- Vaswani et al., "Attention Is All You Need" (2017): introduced the transformer.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: computing power grew exponentially for decades; the internet reaches about 70% of humans; machine learning now outperforms older methods at many perception and language tasks.
- Debated: how far current AI methods can scale; AI's effect on jobs and productivity; whether AI systems "understand" anything in a meaningful sense; how concentrated the industry should be.
- Matters of belief or value: privacy versus security; free expression versus content control; whether and how to slow or regulate AI.

**Open questions:** How capable will AI become, and how fast? Can the energy and chip demands of AI be met? Will quantum computers (which use quantum physics to solve certain problems) become practical? How should a global network be governed across states with very different values?

**See also:** 3.1 Mathematics; 5.3 Labor, Capital, and Firms; 6.4 Authoritarianism; 12.4 Manufacturing and Materials; 13.4 The Internet and Social Media; 16.5 Artificial Intelligence.

### 12.7 Public Health and Medical Technology

**In one sentence:** Humans have roughly doubled their life expectancy since 1900, mainly through clean water, sanitation, vaccines, and antibiotics, and they now spend about a tenth of their total economic output on organized health care.

*This field covers the technologies and systems humans use to protect health. For the biology of disease itself, see 1.6 Health, Disease, and Medicine.*

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Public health** protects whole populations, mainly by preventing disease, as opposed to treating sick individuals (**clinical medicine**).
- **Sanitation** is the safe removal of human waste. Diseases such as cholera and typhoid spread through water contaminated by feces, so clean water and sanitation go together.
- A **vaccine** trains the immune system to recognize a disease-causing organism (**pathogen**) without causing the disease. If enough people are vaccinated, the pathogen cannot spread easily even to the unvaccinated (**herd immunity**).
- An **antibiotic** kills or stops bacteria. **Antimicrobial resistance** arises when microbes evolve to survive these drugs.
- A **health system** is the set of hospitals, clinics, workers, insurers, and agencies that delivers and pays for care.
- **Epidemiology** studies how diseases spread and what causes them in populations, using statistics (3.4).

**How it works:** The biggest health gains came from **cheap, population-wide measures**, not from advanced hospital care.

| Measure | Approximate effect |
|---|---|
| Clean water and sanitation | Large falls in deaths from diarrheal disease, cholera, and typhoid in rich countries ~1850–1950. Diarrhea still kills ~1–1.5 million people a year, mostly where these are lacking |
| Vaccines | Smallpox eradicated in 1980 (it killed an estimated 300 million people in the 20th century). Childhood vaccination is estimated to have saved ~150 million lives since 1974 [Refining] |
| Antibiotics | Made many bacterial infections, surgical wounds, and childbirth infections curable from the 1940s |
| Oral rehydration therapy | A simple salt-and-sugar solution, estimated to have saved tens of millions of children from diarrhea since the 1970s |
| Insecticide-treated bed nets | A major factor in cutting malaria deaths since 2000 |
| Tobacco control | Smoking rates have fallen in most countries, but tobacco still kills ~7–8 million a year |

**Child mortality** shows the scale of change. Around 1800, roughly **4 in 10 children** died before age 5 everywhere. Today the world figure is under **4 in 100**. About **4.8–5 million** children under 5 still die each year, mostly from preventable causes, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.

**Gaps remain.** About **2 billion people** lack safely managed drinking water, and about **3.5 billion** lack safely managed sanitation. The World Health Organization estimates that **about half of humanity** lacks full access to essential health services.

**Medical technology** includes imaging (X-rays, 1895; CT and MRI scans, 1970s), anesthesia, transplants, intensive care, and a large pharmaceutical industry. A new drug typically takes **10–15 years** to reach market; estimated cost per approved drug ranges from about US$1 billion to over US$2.5 billion [Contested; depends on method]. **mRNA vaccines** against COVID-19 were developed and tested in under a year (2020).

**Health spending** is roughly **10% of world GDP** (~US$9–10 trillion a year): about 17% of GDP in the United States, 10–12% in most of Western Europe and Japan, and under US$50 per person a year in many low-income countries.

**How it developed:**
- **1796:** Edward Jenner uses cowpox to protect against smallpox, the basis of vaccination.
- **1847:** Ignaz Semmelweis shows hand-washing cuts childbirth deaths (initially rejected). Anesthesia spreads in the 1840s.
- **1854:** John Snow traces a London cholera outbreak to a contaminated water pump, a founding moment of epidemiology.
- **1860s–1880s:** **germ theory** (Louis Pasteur, Robert Koch) shows microorganisms cause many diseases; antiseptic surgery follows. Cities build sewers and filtered, chlorinated water supplies (~1850s–1920s).
- **1928:** Alexander Fleming discovers penicillin; mass-produced from the 1940s.
- **1948:** the World Health Organization (WHO) is founded; the UK creates its tax-funded National Health Service.
- **1955:** polio vaccine; polio cases have fallen over 99% since 1988. **1980:** smallpox eradicated.
- **1996:** combination therapy turns HIV/AIDS into a manageable chronic condition where treatment is available.
- **2020–2023:** COVID-19 kills ~7 million (officially reported) to ~15–20 million (estimated excess deaths) people (16.3).

**Effects on human life and society:** Falling child mortality was followed, decades later, by falling birth rates, the **demographic transition** (8.5). Longer, healthier lives raised economic output. Health care is now among the largest employers in rich countries. As infections declined, chronic diseases (heart disease, cancer, diabetes, dementia) became the leading causes of death.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **How to organize and pay for care** [Contested; partly Belief/Value]: tax-funded national systems (UK), compulsory social insurance (Germany, Japan), or mixed systems with large private insurance (US). The US spends the most per person yet has lower life expectancy than most rich countries; the causes are [Refining].
- **Universal health coverage** is a UN goal (Sustainable Development Goals, 2015); how fast poor countries can afford it is debated.
- **Vaccine hesitancy:** a minority distrusts vaccines. That approved vaccines are safe and their benefits far exceed their risks is [Established]; the claim that vaccines cause autism has been extensively investigated and is not supported.
- **Liberty vs. collective protection** [Belief/Value]: how far governments may mandate vaccination, quarantine, or lockdowns, a major political dispute during COVID-19.

**Key works and figures:**
- Edward Jenner: vaccination against smallpox (1796).
- John Snow: mapped cholera to a water pump (1854); founded modern epidemiology.
- Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch: germ theory and the identification of specific pathogens.
- Alexander Fleming, with Howard Florey and Ernst Chain: discovery and mass production of penicillin.
- Thomas McKeown, *The Modern Rise of Population* (1976): argued that nutrition, not medicine, drove early mortality decline [Contested; many historians now credit sanitation and public health more].
- Angus Deaton, *The Great Escape* (2013): history of health and wealth improvements.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: sanitation, vaccines, and antibiotics caused most of the rise in life expectancy; child mortality has fallen roughly tenfold since 1800; health outcomes vary hugely by country income.
- Debated: the relative roles of nutrition, sanitation, and medicine in historical mortality decline; which health-system designs give the best value; drug pricing and the true cost of drug development.
- Matters of belief or value: whether health care is a right or a market good; how to balance personal liberty with public-health rules.

**Open questions:** Can antimicrobial resistance be contained? Can malaria, tuberculosis, and HIV be eliminated? How will health systems pay for care for rapidly aging populations? How much will AI and gene-based therapies change medicine?

**See also:** 1.6 Health, Disease, and Medicine; 5.6 Government and the Economy; 8.5 Population and Migration; 16.3 Pandemics and Global Health; 16.6 Demographic Change and Aging.

### 12.8 Weapons and Military Technology

**In one sentence:** Humans have developed tools for killing and coercing one another from sharpened sticks to nuclear weapons able to destroy cities in seconds, and military competition has repeatedly driven technological change.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- A **military** is an organized armed force controlled by a state (see 6.1, 6.6). Non-state armed groups also exist.
- **Firearms** use a chemical explosion (originally **gunpowder**: saltpeter, charcoal, and sulfur) to propel a projectile.
- A **nuclear weapon** releases energy by splitting heavy atoms (**fission**) or fusing light ones (**fusion**, in "hydrogen bombs"). One modern warhead can equal hundreds of thousands of tonnes of conventional explosive.
- **Deterrence** is preventing attack by threatening unacceptable retaliation.
- A **drone** is a vehicle with no human aboard, steered remotely or by software. A **cyberweapon** is software built to damage or disrupt computer systems.

**How it works:** World military spending was about **US$2.7 trillion in 2024**, roughly 2.5% of world GDP. The United States accounts for about 37%, China an estimated 12%, and Russia about 5% [Refining; Chinese and Russian figures are estimates].

| Category | Scale (mid-2020s, approximate) |
|---|---|
| Nuclear warheads | ~12,000 held by 9 states; ~90% belong to the US and Russia |
| Firearms | Over 1 billion worldwide, ~85% held by civilians |
| Active-duty military personnel | ~20–30 million worldwide |
| International arms trade | Over US$100 billion a year (estimated); the US is the largest exporter |

The deadliest weapons in most wars are not the most advanced: **small arms**, **artillery**, and **explosives** cause most deaths. Since 2022, Russia and Ukraine have each used **millions of cheap drones a year**, changing battlefield tactics.

**How it developed:**
- **Prehistory:** spears; bows and arrows by at least ~60,000 years ago [Refining].
- **~3000–1000 BCE:** bronze, then iron weapons; horse-drawn chariots, then cavalry.
- **9th century CE:** gunpowder invented in China; cannons spread across Eurasia by the 1300s–1400s. Firearms and armed sailing ships later help European states conquer much of the world (4.5).
- **1860s–1918:** industrial weapons: machine guns (Maxim gun, 1884), steel warships. World War I (1914–18) brings tanks, aircraft, submarines, and poison gas; ~15–20 million die.
- **1939–1945:** World War II, the deadliest war in human history (~70–85 million deaths): radar, jets, rockets, mass bombing of cities.
- **August 1945:** US atomic bombs on **Hiroshima and Nagasaki** kill roughly 110,000–210,000 people by the end of 1945, the only nuclear weapons used in war.
- **1952–1960s:** hydrogen bombs; intercontinental ballistic missiles that cross the planet in ~30 minutes. Stockpiles peak at ~70,000 warheads around 1986.
- **1990s–2020s:** precision-guided weapons, armed drones, cyberattacks (e.g., Stuxnet, which sabotaged Iranian nuclear equipment, discovered 2010), and AI-assisted targeting.

**Effects on human life and society:** Weapons shaped which states and empires won and lost (Domain 4). Military research produced or sped up jet engines, radar, nuclear power, computers, GPS satellite navigation, and the internet. Nuclear weapons made all-out war between major powers potentially suicidal for all sides. Whether the share of humans dying violently has fallen over centuries is [Contested] (6.6).

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Nuclear deterrence:** the theory that nuclear weapons have prevented great-power war since 1945 by making it too costly ("mutually assured destruction"). Critics point to near-misses such as the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962) and argue luck played a part [Contested].
- **Arms control:** treaties such as the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968), Biological Weapons Convention (1972), and Chemical Weapons Convention (1993). Several US–Russia nuclear treaties have lapsed or been suspended.
- **Autonomous weapons:** whether weapons that select and attack targets without a human decision should be banned [Contested; Belief/Value]. No binding ban existed in the mid-2020s.
- **Just war theory and pacifism:** ethical views on when, if ever, war is justified [Belief/Value; see 11.3].

**Key works and figures:**
- Carl von Clausewitz, *On War* (1832): war as the continuation of politics by other means.
- J. Robert Oppenheimer: scientific director of the US atomic bomb project (1942–45).
- Bernard Brodie, *The Absolute Weapon* (1946): early statement of nuclear deterrence theory.
- Geoffrey Parker, *The Military Revolution* (1988): how gunpowder weapons reshaped states.
- Richard Rhodes, *The Making of the Atomic Bomb* (1986): the history of nuclear weapons.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: nuclear weapons can destroy cities and, in large numbers, kill a large fraction of humanity; small arms and explosives cause most war deaths; military research has driven many civilian technologies.
- Debated: whether nuclear deterrence is stable long-term; whether violence has declined over history; how AI and drones will change war.
- Matters of belief or value: whether nuclear weapons should be abolished; whether some weapons are inherently unacceptable; the ethics of the arms trade.

**Open questions:** Will nuclear weapons spread to more states? Will AI-enabled weapons make wars faster and harder to control? Can cyberattacks cause physical catastrophe? Can arms-control agreements be rebuilt?

**See also:** 4.6 The World Wars and the Cold War; 6.6 War and Organized Violence; 7.5 International Law and Human Rights; 16.4 Nuclear Weapons and Catastrophic Risks; 16.5 Artificial Intelligence.
