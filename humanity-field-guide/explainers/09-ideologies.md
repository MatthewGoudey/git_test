# Domain 9: Ideologies and Political Thought

An ideology is a connected set of beliefs about how society works and how it *should* work. Ideologies have organized parties, inspired revolutions, justified wars and genocides, and built welfare states. Each explainer below separates three things. First, an ideology's **core values**, which are [Belief/Value] and cannot be settled by evidence. Second, its **empirical claims** and **track record**, which can be checked. Third, the **strongest case** made by its supporters and by its critics. This guide does not rank ideologies. It reports what they claim and what happened where they were tried.

---

### 9.1 What an Ideology Is

**In one sentence:** An ideology is a shared mental map that tells its holders what society is like, what is wrong with it, what a good society would look like, and how to get there.

**What an alien needs to know first:** Humans cannot personally check most facts about their society, so they rely on frameworks that simplify it. An ideology combines four parts:
1. **Values**: what matters most, for example liberty, equality, order, tradition, the nation, or God [Belief/Value].
2. **Empirical beliefs**: claims about cause and effect, for example "markets allocate goods efficiently" or "capitalism impoverishes workers." These can be tested.
3. **A diagnosis**: what is wrong now.
4. **A program**: what to do about it.

The word "ideology" was coined in France in 1796 by Antoine Destutt de Tracy. Karl Marx later used it to mean beliefs that serve a ruling class while appearing neutral. Today scholars use it neutrally, for any political belief system.

**How it works:**
- **The left–right spectrum.** The terms come from the seating of the French National Assembly in 1789. Supporters of the king sat on the right, and supporters of revolutionary change on the left. Roughly, the **left** today stresses equality and change, and the **right** stresses order, tradition, markets, or the nation. The spectrum is a crude simplification. Many people combine views that do not fit one line, for example economically left and socially traditional. Two-axis models, with economic and social or cultural axes, often describe voters better.
- **Words change meaning.** In the United States, "liberal" means center-left. In much of Europe and Australia, "liberal" often means pro-market and center-right. The labels in this domain use their broad, international meaning.
- **How people acquire ideologies.** Parents strongly shape children's political and party leanings. Twin studies suggest that roughly 30–50% of the variation in broad political attitudes is associated with genetic differences, presumably via temperament [Refining]. Religion, education, class, region, and life events also matter.
- **How ideologies shape perception.** People tend to accept evidence that fits their side and reject evidence that does not (**motivated reasoning**). This happens in all ideological camps [Established in psychology; size of effect debated].
- **Most citizens are not strict ideologues.** A classic finding (Philip Converse, 1964) is that most voters hold loosely connected views and follow party or group cues more than coherent doctrines [Refining].

**How it developed:** Ancient and medieval thought addressed politics, but modern ideologies arose with the political revolutions of 1688–1848 and the Industrial Revolution. These events forced questions about who should rule and who should own what. The 19th century produced liberalism, conservatism, socialism, nationalism, and anarchism. The 20th added communism in power, fascism, social democracy, neoliberalism, environmentalism, and political Islamism. In 1989 Francis Fukuyama argued that liberal democracy had won the contest of ideas ("the end of history"). The 2000s–2020s rise of authoritarian and populist movements led many to question this [Contested].

**Effects on human life and society:** Ideologies decided the economic systems of billions of people in the 20th century. They motivated conflicts that killed tens of millions, and they shaped welfare systems, constitutions, and borders. In many democracies today, ideological identity predicts whom people befriend and marry and which news they trust. This is called **polarization**.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Marxist view:** ideology masks class interest.
- **Liberal pluralist view:** ideologies are competing value systems, and democracy manages the competition.
- **Moral foundations theory (Jonathan Haidt):** left and right weight moral intuitions differently. Care and fairness weigh heavily on the left. Loyalty, authority, and sanctity weigh more on the right [Refining; measurement debated].
- **"End of ideology" theses (Daniel Bell, 1960; Fukuyama, 1989):** big ideological conflict would fade. This has not clearly happened [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- Karl Mannheim, *Ideology and Utopia* (1929): founding sociology of ideology.
- Philip Converse, "The Nature of Belief Systems in Mass Publics" (1964): most voters lack coherent ideology.
- Francis Fukuyama, "The End of History?" (1989): liberal democracy as the end-point of ideological evolution [Contested].
- Jonathan Haidt, *The Righteous Mind* (2012): moral psychology of left and right.
- Michael Freeden, *Ideologies and Political Theory* (1996): ideologies as clusters of concepts.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Ideologies combine values with factual claims. The left–right labels began in 1789. Political views are strongly shaped by family and group identity.
- Debated: The degree of genetic influence on political attitudes. Whether polarization is rising worldwide or mainly in some countries. Whether one side is more prone to bias (research finds bias on all sides).
- Matters of belief or value: every ideology's core priorities.

**Open questions:** How do social media and AI change how people form political beliefs? Can societies with deep value disagreements keep a shared set of facts?

**See also:** 6.3 Democracy and Elections; 11.4 Political Philosophy; 13.5 Propaganda, Persuasion, and Misinformation; 15.3 Cognitive Biases and Decision-Making; 15.6 Cooperation, Conflict, and Group Behavior.

### 9.2 Liberalism

**In one sentence:** Liberalism is the family of ideas holding that individuals have rights that governments must respect, and that political power is legitimate only when it is limited, accountable, and based on the consent of the governed.

**What an alien needs to know first:** Before roughly 1700, most human societies were ruled by hereditary monarchs or aristocracies who claimed authority from tradition or from God, and many had official religions that punished dissent. A **right** is a claim a person has that others, including the state, must respect, such as freedom of speech or a fair trial. **Rule of law** means that everyone, including rulers, is bound by known laws applied by independent courts. **Liberal democracy** combines elected government with protected individual rights and limits on power.

**Core values [Belief/Value]:** individual liberty; equal legal and political rights; government by consent; religious toleration; free expression; private property; the rule of law.

**How it works:**
- **Classical liberalism** (17th–19th centuries) stresses limited government, free markets, and protection from state power.
- **Social or modern liberalism** (from about 1880) argues that poverty, ignorance, and illness also limit freedom. It therefore supports public education, social insurance, and regulation.
- Both strands share **constitutionalism**: written or customary limits on power, separation of powers, and courts that can check rulers (see 7.2).

**How it developed:** Liberalism grew out of the English Civil War and Glorious Revolution (1688), Locke's *Two Treatises* (1689), the European Enlightenment, the American Declaration of Independence (1776), and the French Revolution (1789). In the 19th century, liberals pushed for wider voting rights, free trade, and the abolition of slavery and serfdom. After 1945, and again after the end of the Cold War in 1989–91, liberal democracy spread widely. Since around 2006, democracy monitors have recorded a net decline, often called **democratic backsliding**.

**Where applied, and documented outcomes:**
- **Coverage.** The V-Dem project counts about 30 "liberal democracies" in the mid-2020s, containing roughly 1 in 8 humans. Many more countries are "electoral democracies" with weaker protections [Refining; depends on index].
- **Wealth and wellbeing.** Long-established liberal democracies (Western Europe, North America, Japan, Australia, New Zealand) are heavily over-represented among the countries with the highest incomes, life expectancy, and reported life satisfaction. Whether liberal institutions *caused* this, or wealth produced liberalism, is debated. One influential study (Acemoglu and colleagues, 2019) estimated that democratization raises GDP per capita by about 20% over 25 years [Contested].
- **Peace.** Established democracies have rarely, if ever, fought full wars against each other. This is the "democratic peace" [Refining; explanations debated].
- **Famine.** Amartya Sen observed that no major famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press [Refining; definitions debated].
- **Blind spots.** Liberal states coexisted with slavery (the United States until 1865), colonial empires (Britain, France, the Netherlands), and exclusion of women from voting into the 20th century. John Stuart Mill himself justified "despotism" over colonized peoples.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Strongest case for:** Liberalism lets people with different religions and values live together peacefully. It protects minorities and dissenters. It allows error-correction through free debate and elections. And it is associated with the most prosperous, longest-lived, and freest societies so far recorded.
- **Strongest criticisms:** From the **left**: formal rights mean little without material equality, and liberal markets produce large inequality. From **conservatives and communitarians**: liberalism dissolves family, religion, and community, leaving isolated individuals. From **postcolonial critics**: liberalism's universal claims coexisted with empire. From **"illiberal" nationalists** (e.g., Hungary's Viktor Orbán, 2014): majorities and national culture should take precedence over individual rights and courts.
- **What the evidence shows:** The correlation between liberal institutions and prosperity, peace, and health is strong. The causal direction and the role of other factors (geography, history, resources) are [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- John Locke, *Two Treatises of Government* (1689): rights to life, liberty, and property; government by consent.
- Montesquieu, *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748): separation of powers.
- Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776): economic liberty.
- John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty* (1859): the "harm principle," the idea that power over individuals is justified only to prevent harm to others.
- Isaiah Berlin, "Two Concepts of Liberty" (1958): freedom *from* interference versus freedom *to* act.
- John Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* (1971): the leading modern egalitarian-liberal theory.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Liberal ideas shaped most modern constitutions and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948). Liberal democracies are among the richest and healthiest societies.
- Debated: Whether liberalism causes prosperity, and why liberal democracy has been in retreat since the mid-2000s.
- Matters of belief or value: the priority of individual rights over collective goals, how much equality liberty requires.

**Open questions:** Is the recent democratic decline temporary or a long-term shift? Can liberal societies maintain cohesion amid polarization and mass digital media?

**See also:** 6.2 Forms of Government; 6.3 Democracy and Elections; 7.2 Constitutions and Rights; 9.5 Capitalism and Free-Market Thought; 11.4 Political Philosophy.

### 9.3 Conservatism

**In one sentence:** Conservatism is the disposition and ideology that values inherited institutions and traditions, distrusts sweeping plans to remake society, and prefers gradual, tested change.

**What an alien needs to know first:** Human societies accumulate customs, laws, religions, and institutions (lasting organized practices such as marriage, churches, courts, and monarchies) over centuries. Conservatism argues that these contain practical wisdom no single generation fully understands. Because it defends *what exists*, its content differs by place. An American conservative may defend free markets and the 1787 Constitution. A Saudi conservative may defend monarchy and religious law. A Japanese conservative may defend the imperial family and national traditions.

**Core values [Belief/Value]:** tradition and continuity; social order and stability; authority and hierarchy where seen as legitimate; family, religion, and local community; national identity; prudence and skepticism of abstract theory; a view of human nature as imperfect and limited.

**How it works:** Conservative parties are usually among the two largest political blocs in democracies, for example Britain's Conservatives, Germany's Christian Democrats, the US Republicans, Japan's Liberal Democratic Party, and India's BJP (which is often classed as Hindu nationalist, see 9.8). Most modern Western conservatism combines three strands:
- **Traditionalist or religious** conservatism: moral and social order.
- **Market or fiscal** conservatism: low taxes and limited government. It overlaps with classical liberalism.
- **National** conservatism: sovereignty, borders, and national culture.

**How it developed:** Modern conservatism is usually dated to Edmund Burke's *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790). Burke argued that the revolutionaries' attempt to rebuild society from abstract principles would end in tyranny, and the Terror of 1793–94 seemed to many to confirm this. Continental "reactionaries" such as Joseph de Maistre went further, seeking to restore monarchy and church. In the 19th century, conservatives such as Benjamin Disraeli in Britain and Otto von Bismarck in Germany extended voting rights and created the first state social insurance (Germany, 1880s) to win workers' loyalty and blunt socialism. After 1945, **Christian Democracy** led postwar reconstruction in West Germany and Italy. From the 1970s, figures such as Margaret Thatcher (UK, 1979–90) and Ronald Reagan (US, 1981–89) fused conservatism with free-market economics. Since the 2010s, **national conservatism** and right-wing populism have gained ground (see 9.8).

**Where applied, and documented outcomes:** Because conservatism is about the pace and direction of change, not a fixed end-state, it has no single measurable "experiment." Notable records:
- West Germany under Christian Democrats (1949–69) built the "social market economy." It saw very rapid growth (the *Wirtschaftswunder*, "economic miracle"), with GDP per capita rising roughly threefold in two decades.
- Conservative governments created early welfare states (Bismarck) and also resisted many expansions of rights. Conservatives in various countries opposed the abolition of slavery, women's suffrage, decolonization, and the end of racial segregation. Others supported some of these reforms.
- In most countries, Thatcher- and Reagan-era policies coincided with lower inflation, renewed growth, and rising inequality (see 9.5).

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Strongest case for:** Complex societies are poorly understood. Radical redesigns, such as revolutionary France, Soviet collectivization, and Maoist China, often caused catastrophe through unintended consequences. Institutions like family, religion, and community provide meaning, trust, and mutual support that the state and markets cannot easily replace. Stable institutions also allow long-term planning.
- **Strongest criticisms:** "Tradition" has often protected the privileges of dominant groups and justified slavery, caste, and the subordination of women. Status-quo bias can block needed change. Conservatism's content is inconsistent across places. Some critics also argue that conservative parties have at times shifted toward authoritarian or nativist positions.
- **What the evidence shows:** The evidence that hasty, sweeping reform can backfire is strong. So is the evidence that many reforms conservatives once resisted, such as universal suffrage and the end of legal segregation, became broadly accepted and did not produce the disasters predicted. Psychological studies find conservatives on average place more weight on order, loyalty, and purity, and are more averse to uncertainty [Refining; measurement disputed].

**Key works and figures:**
- Edmund Burke, *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790): founding text of conservatism.
- Joseph de Maistre, *Considerations on France* (1797): reactionary, religious counter-revolution.
- Russell Kirk, *The Conservative Mind* (1953): defined postwar American traditionalism.
- Michael Oakeshott, "On Being Conservative" (1956): conservatism as a disposition.
- Roger Scruton, *How to Be a Conservative* (2014): contemporary statement.
- Confucian political thought: an older, non-Western tradition emphasizing hierarchy, ritual, and continuity (see 11.5).

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Conservatism arose as a reaction to the French Revolution. It takes different forms in different countries. Conservative parties have governed most democracies for long periods.
- Debated: Whether conservative policies (e.g., on family or religion) produce measurable social benefits. The direction of current national-conservative movements.
- Matters of belief or value: which traditions deserve preservation, the proper balance between stability and justice.

**Open questions:** What does conservatism mean in societies where tradition itself is rapidly changing? How will tensions between its market, religious, and nationalist strands be resolved?

**See also:** 4.5 Colonialism, Revolutions, and Industry; 9.2 Liberalism; 9.6 Nationalism; 10.7 Religion's Effects on Society; 11.5 World Philosophical Traditions.

### 9.4 Socialism and Communism

**In one sentence:** Socialism is the family of ideologies that seeks to replace or restrain private ownership of productive resources with collective or social ownership, in the name of equality and an end to exploitation. Communism is its most radical form, which in practice meant one-party states running planned economies.

**What an alien needs to know first:** The **means of production** are the resources used to produce goods: land, factories, machines, mines. Under capitalism (see 9.5), they are mostly privately owned, and most people work for wages. A **planned economy** is one where government planners, not market prices, decide what is produced, how, and for whom. A **one-party state** allows only one legal political party.

**Core values [Belief/Value]:** equality of material conditions; solidarity and cooperation; ending the exploitation of workers by owners; democratic or collective control of the economy; meeting human needs rather than producing for profit.

**How it works: the main branches.**
- **Marxism** (Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels) claims that history is driven by class struggle. Capitalists profit by paying workers less than the value they create. Capitalism will produce crises and be overthrown by workers, leading eventually to a classless, stateless "communist" society. Mainstream economics rejects Marx's labor theory of value, and the predicted worker revolutions in advanced industrial countries did not occur [Established as historical record; the theory remains Contested in parts of academia].
- **Marxism-Leninism** (Vladimir Lenin) adds a disciplined "vanguard" party that seizes power and rules on the workers' behalf.
- **Social democracy** (after Eduard Bernstein, 1899) abandons revolution. It seeks gradual reform through elections: welfare states, strong unions, and regulated markets.
- **Democratic and market socialism** propose collective or worker ownership within a democracy. They have been applied only partially, as in Yugoslavia's worker self-management or cooperatives such as Mondragón in Spain.

**How it developed:** Early "utopian" socialists (Robert Owen, Charles Fourier, Henri de Saint-Simon) reacted to early industrial poverty. *The Communist Manifesto* appeared in 1848. Lenin's Bolsheviks seized power in Russia in 1917. After 1945, communist parties ruled Eastern Europe, China (1949), North Korea, Vietnam, Cuba (1959), and others. Around 1980, roughly one-third of humanity lived under communist-party rule. Most of these regimes collapsed in 1989–91. China, Vietnam, Laos, Cuba, and North Korea remain ruled by communist parties, but China and Vietnam have adopted market economies.

**Where applied, and documented outcomes (approximate):**

| Case | Documented outcomes |
|---|---|
| **USSR (1917–1991)** | Rapid industrialization from 1928. Near-universal literacy. Large gains in life expectancy. Defeated Nazi Germany. Growth slowed sharply from the 1970s, with chronic shortages. Soviet famine of 1930–33 after forced collectivization: ~5.5–8.7 million deaths, including ~3.5–5 million in Ukraine (the "Holodomor") and ~1.5 million Kazakhs. Gulag labor camps held ~18 million people over 1930–53, with ~1.5–1.8 million documented deaths. ~680,000–750,000 people were executed in the Great Terror of 1937–38. |
| **Maoist China (1949–1976)** | Great Leap Forward famine (1959–61): ~15–45 million deaths, with most scholarly estimates around 30 million. Cultural Revolution (1966–76): hundreds of thousands to ~2 million killed [range Contested]. Life expectancy still rose substantially from a very low base. |
| **Cambodia, Khmer Rouge (1975–79)** | ~1.5–2 million deaths from execution, starvation, and forced labor, roughly a fifth to a quarter of the population. |
| **Divided countries** | East Germany's output per person was roughly one-third to one-half of West Germany's by 1989. North and South Korea were at similar levels around 1970. South Korea's income per person is now more than 20 times higher [estimates vary]. North Korea's 1990s famine killed roughly 0.2–3.5 million, with ~0.5–1 million a common estimate. |
| **Post-1978 China** | Market reforms under continued Communist Party rule lifted roughly 800 million people out of extreme poverty (World Bank). Whether this counts as a success of socialism or of markets is debated. |
| **Cuba (1959–)** | High literacy and life expectancy comparable to rich countries. Low incomes, rationing, and political repression. Large-scale emigration, including perhaps 1 million+ people (~10%) since 2021 [Refining]. |
| **Venezuela (1999–)** | "Socialism of the 21st century": nationalizations and price controls. GDP fell by roughly three-quarters from 2013 to 2021, with hyperinflation. ~7–8 million people emigrated. The relative roles of policy, the oil-price collapse, and US sanctions are debated. |
| **Nordic social democracies** | Denmark, Finland, Norway, and Sweden have market economies with private ownership and free trade, plus high taxes (~40–45% of GDP) and union membership of roughly 50–65% (excluding Norway, which is lower). They have very low inequality (Gini ~0.25–0.28 after taxes), high social mobility, top-tier life expectancy, and top happiness rankings. Sweden had a severe crisis in the early 1990s, followed by market-oriented reforms. |

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Strongest case for:** Capitalism produces large, often inherited inequality, insecurity, and crises. Workers create value but lack control. Socialist and labor movements won the 8-hour day, universal suffrage, public health care, and pensions. Nordic social democracy shows that high equality and high prosperity can coexist. Communist states achieved rapid literacy, industrialization, and health gains from very poor starting points.
- **Strongest criticisms:** Without market prices, planners cannot calculate efficiently (Ludwig von Mises, 1920; Friedrich Hayek). This leads to chronic shortages (János Kornai's "shortage economy"). Concentrating economic and political power in one party produced repression, mass killing, and famine in every case of full Marxist-Leninist rule. Nordic success rests on markets, high trust, and private enterprise, not on socialist ownership.
- **What the evidence shows:** Every Marxist-Leninist state with full central planning had lower living standards than comparable market economies and suppressed political freedom. Several suffered famines killing millions. Whether this is inherent to socialism or to Leninist one-party rule is [Contested]. Social democracy has a strong record on equality and wellbeing, but how much comes from policy versus pre-existing conditions (small, high-trust, homogeneous populations) is [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, *The Communist Manifesto* (1848); Marx, *Capital*, vol. 1 (1867).
- Eduard Bernstein, *Evolutionary Socialism* (1899): reformist path.
- Vladimir Lenin, *The State and Revolution* (1917): theory of revolutionary seizure of power.
- Ludwig von Mises, "Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth" (1920): the price-calculation critique.
- János Kornai, *The Socialist System* (1992): the economics of planned economies, from an insider.
- Gøsta Esping-Andersen, *The Three Worlds of Welfare Capitalism* (1990): comparative welfare states.
- Frank Dikötter, *Mao's Great Famine* (2010); Robert Conquest, *The Great Terror* (1968): histories of the mass deaths.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: The famines and mass repression listed above. The collapse of Soviet-type planning by 1991. Strong Nordic welfare outcomes.
- Debated: Exact death tolls. Whether non-authoritarian socialism can work at national scale. What explains Nordic success. How to classify modern China.
- Matters of belief or value: how much equality matters relative to liberty and efficiency, and whether private ownership of productive resources is just.

**Open questions:** Can collective ownership be combined with democracy and efficient prices at large scale? Will automation and AI renew interest in collective ownership of capital?

**See also:** 4.6 The World Wars and the Cold War; 5.5 Inequality; 5.6 Government and the Economy; 6.4 Authoritarianism; 8.4 Social Class and Mobility; 9.5 Capitalism and Free-Market Thought.

### 9.5 Capitalism and Free-Market Thought

**In one sentence:** Capitalism is an economic system in which productive resources are mostly privately owned and goods are mostly produced for sale in markets for profit. Free-market thought is the family of ideologies that argues this system should operate with minimal government interference.

**What an alien needs to know first:** A **market** is any arrangement where people voluntarily exchange goods, usually for money. **Prices** emerge from supply and demand and signal what is scarce (see 5.1). **Capital** means productive assets such as machines, buildings, and money invested in businesses. **Profit** is revenue minus costs. Capitalism is often described both as a **system** that exists in some degree almost everywhere and as an **ideology** defending it. These are different things.

**Core values [Belief/Value]:** economic freedom and voluntary exchange; private property; individual responsibility and reward for effort and risk; limited government; competition.

**How it works: the main variants.**
- **Laissez-faire** (French: "let it be"): the state limits itself to enforcing contracts and property, defense, and courts.
- **Libertarianism:** individual liberty, including economic liberty, is the supreme political value. Taxation beyond a minimal state is a rights violation (Robert Nozick, Ayn Rand) [Belief/Value].
- **Neoliberalism:** a term mostly used by critics for the policy turn of about 1975–2008. It involved privatization, deregulation, free trade, lower top tax rates, and independent central banks focused on inflation.
- **Varieties of capitalism:** "Liberal market" economies (US, UK) rely more on markets. "Coordinated market" economies (Germany, Japan, the Nordics) rely more on unions, banks, and business associations. **State capitalism** (China, Singapore, Gulf states) combines markets with large state-owned firms and state direction.
- In practice, every rich country runs a **mixed economy**. Government spending is typically 35–55% of GDP in rich democracies.

**How it developed:** Markets are ancient. Capitalism as a dominant system emerged in the Dutch Republic and Britain in the 1600s–1700s and spread with the Industrial Revolution. Adam Smith (1776) argued that self-interested exchange in competitive markets tends to serve the common good (the "invisible hand"). The Great Depression (1929–39) discredited laissez-faire and led to Keynesian managed economies. Stagflation in the 1970s (high inflation plus high unemployment) led to the market-oriented turn associated with Friedrich Hayek and Milton Friedman, Thatcher, and Reagan. In 1975–1990, Chile's military dictatorship under Augusto Pinochet implemented market reforms designed by economists trained in Chicago while repressing opponents (~3,000 killed or disappeared, tens of thousands tortured). China (from 1978), India (1991), and the former Soviet bloc (from 1989) all moved toward markets.

**Where applied, and documented outcomes (approximate):**
- **Growth.** World GDP per person has risen roughly 10–15-fold since 1800, mainly in market economies [Refining in exact numbers]. The share of humans in extreme poverty fell from roughly three-quarters around 1800 to about 10% today [Refining].
- **Transitions.** After its 1991 reforms, India's growth rose from about 3–4% a year to about 6–7%. The four "Asian Tigers" (South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore) grew rich after about 1960 through export-oriented markets, often with heavy state guidance. Russia's rapid 1990s privatization saw output fall by roughly 40% and male life expectancy drop to about 58 in 1994. Poland's similar but better-sequenced transition grew strongly.
- **Inequality.** In the United States, the top 1%'s share of pre-tax income roughly doubled, from about 10% in 1980 to about 20% [Refining]. In much of continental Europe, the rise was smaller.
- **Instability.** Market economies have recurring recessions and financial crises, such as 1929 and 2008 (see 5.8).
- **Chile.** Chile became Latin America's richest country per person, and poverty fell from about 40–45% in the late 1980s to under 10% by the late 2010s. Inequality stayed high, and mass protests in 2019 targeted the economic model.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Strongest case for:** Markets coordinate billions of decisions through prices without central control (Hayek's "knowledge problem"). Competition and profit drive innovation. Every country that has reached high income has done so with a predominantly market economy. Economic freedom underpins political freedom (Friedman).
- **Strongest criticisms:** Markets produce large inequalities of wealth and power and periodic crises. They exploit workers, especially without unions or labor law. They ignore **externalities**, such as pollution and climate change, unless regulated. They turn everything into a commodity (Karl Polanyi). They can capture governments through lobbying. Libertarians add a different critique: "crony capitalism" (government favors to connected firms) betrays free markets.
- **What the evidence shows:** The association between market economies and long-run growth is very strong. Equally, every successful case also had substantial government (law, education, infrastructure, and usually social insurance). The ideal *degree* of government involvement is [Contested] and is the core of everyday political dispute.

**Key works and figures:**
- Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776): the founding case for markets.
- Karl Polanyi, *The Great Transformation* (1944): markets as socially destructive when unregulated.
- Friedrich Hayek, *The Road to Serfdom* (1944) and "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945).
- Milton Friedman, *Capitalism and Freedom* (1962).
- Robert Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974): philosophical libertarianism.
- Peter Hall and David Soskice, *Varieties of Capitalism* (2001).
- Thomas Piketty, *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2013): long-run wealth concentration [Refining/Contested in parts].

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Market economies generated the vast majority of modern growth. They also produce cycles and crises, and they underprice pollution without regulation.
- Debated: The optimal size of government, the causes of rising inequality, and whether neoliberal reforms on balance helped or hurt.
- Matters of belief or value: whether market outcomes are fair, and how much liberty should yield to equality.

**Open questions:** Can capitalism decarbonize fast enough? How will AI-driven automation change the split of income between labor and capital?

**See also:** 5.1 Scarcity, Trade, and Markets; 5.4 Growth, Development, and Poverty; 5.5 Inequality; 5.8 Economic Crises; 9.2 Liberalism; 9.4 Socialism and Communism.

### 9.6 Nationalism

**In one sentence:** Nationalism is the belief that humanity is divided into nations, that each person owes primary political loyalty to their nation, and that each nation should govern itself, ideally in its own state.

**What an alien needs to know first:** A **state** is a government with control over a territory (see 6.1). A **nation** is a large group of people who see themselves as sharing a common identity, usually based on language, culture, history, religion, or ancestry. The two do not always match. Kurds (~30–40 million) are a nation without a state. Switzerland is a state with four official languages. A **nation-state** is a state whose people largely see themselves as one nation. **Self-determination** is the principle that peoples should choose their own government.

**Core values [Belief/Value]:** loyalty to the nation; national independence and sovereignty; preservation of national culture and identity; the nation as a community of mutual obligation.

**How it works:** Nationalism binds strangers into a community that most members will never meet. Benedict Anderson called this an "imagined community." States build it through a shared language, mass schooling, national histories, flags, anthems, conscription, and media. Scholars distinguish:
- **Civic nationalism**: membership is based on citizenship and shared political values. The United States and France are often cited.
- **Ethnic nationalism**: membership is based on ancestry, language, or religion.

The distinction is useful but blurry in practice [Contested].

**How it developed:** Scholars disagree on origins. **Modernists** (Ernest Gellner, Benedict Anderson, Eric Hobsbawm) argue nations are largely products of the last ~250 years, created by printing, industrialization, and mass education. **Ethno-symbolists** (Anthony D. Smith) stress older ethnic roots [Contested]. Key moments:
- The American (1776) and French (1789) revolutions made "the people" or "the nation" sovereign. France introduced the mass conscription of citizens in 1793.
- German and Italian nationalists unified their countries (Italy 1861, Germany 1871).
- Collapsing empires after World War I produced new nation-states. US President Woodrow Wilson promoted "self-determination" in 1918.
- Anti-colonial nationalism drove **decolonization**. UN membership grew from 51 states in 1945 to 193 today.

**Where applied, and documented outcomes:**
- **Building states and solidarity.** Nationalism underpinned mass literacy, standardized languages, and welfare states (shared identity can increase willingness to pay taxes for strangers [Refining]). A well-known comparison: Tanzania's deliberate nation-building, using a common language and suppressing ethnic politics, is associated with better local cooperation on public goods than in neighboring Kenya [Refining].
- **Independence.** Anti-colonial movements in India (independent 1947), Indonesia, Ghana, Algeria, and elsewhere ended European rule over hundreds of millions of people.
- **War and ethnic violence.** Nationalism helped drive World War I (~15–20 million dead). The **Partition of India** (1947) killed roughly 200,000–2 million people and displaced ~15 million. The Yugoslav wars (1991–2001) killed ~130,000–140,000, including the Srebrenica genocide (1995, ~8,000 Bosniak men and boys). Studies find wars become more likely when nation-states form out of empires [Refining]. Extreme ethnic nationalism fed the Armenian genocide and Nazism (see 9.7).
- **Today.** Nationalist themes are central to many 2010s–2020s movements and governments, from Britain's vote to leave the European Union (2016) to Chinese "national rejuvenation," Russian claims over Ukraine, India's Hindu nationalism, and anti-immigration parties across Europe.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Strongest case for:** Democracy and welfare states require a shared identity and trust ("we" must decide together and share). National self-determination freed colonized peoples. Nations give individuals meaning and belonging. Liberal nationalists such as David Miller and Yael Tamir argue nationhood and liberal rights are compatible.
- **Strongest criticisms:** Nationalism excludes or persecutes minorities, inflames conflict over mixed territories, and has justified ethnic cleansing and genocide. Cosmopolitans argue that moral obligations should not stop at borders [Belief/Value].
- **What the evidence shows:** Nationalism has been central both to the building of functioning states and to many of the deadliest conflicts of the last two centuries. Its effects depend heavily on whether it is inclusive or exclusionary, and on how borders and minorities are handled.

**Key works and figures:**
- Johann Gottfried Herder (1770s–1790s): the nation as a cultural-linguistic community.
- Giuseppe Mazzini (1830s–1860s): liberal nationalism and Italian unification.
- Ernest Renan, "What Is a Nation?" (1882): the nation as a "daily plebiscite."
- Hans Kohn, *The Idea of Nationalism* (1944): the civic/ethnic distinction.
- Ernest Gellner, *Nations and Nationalism* (1983), and Benedict Anderson, *Imagined Communities* (1983): modernist theories.
- Frantz Fanon, *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961): anti-colonial nationalism.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Most of today's states were formed or reshaped by nationalism since 1800. Nationalist conflict has caused mass violence.
- Debated: How old nations are. Whether civic nationalism is truly distinct. Whether national identity is necessary for welfare states.
- Matters of belief or value: the moral weight of compatriots versus foreigners; whether nations have a right to their own state.

**Open questions:** Will globalization weaken national identities or provoke stronger ones? How can multi-ethnic states hold together without coercion?

**See also:** 4.5 Colonialism, Revolutions, and Industry; 6.1 The State; 6.6 War and Organized Violence; 8.3 Race, Ethnicity, and Identity; 9.7 Fascism and Totalitarianism.

### 9.7 Fascism and Totalitarianism

**In one sentence:** Fascism is a 20th-century ideology of extreme nationalism that rejected democracy, liberalism, and Marxism in favor of a single-party state led by an all-powerful leader, glorified violence and war, and in its Nazi form organized genocide. Totalitarianism is a term for regimes, of left or right, that seek total control over society and individual life.

**What an alien needs to know first:** After World War I (1914–18), much of Europe faced economic ruin, fear of communist revolution, humiliating peace terms (especially for Germany), and weak new democracies. The **Holocaust** is the name for Nazi Germany's systematic murder of European Jews. **Genocide** is the deliberate destruction of a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group.

**Core values of fascism, as stated by its adherents [Belief/Value]:** the nation (or, in Nazism, the "race") is supreme and the individual is subordinate to it; the nation must be reborn from decadence; unity under one leader and one party; war and struggle are ennobling; class conflict is to be replaced by national unity, with employers and workers organized under the state (**corporatism**); liberal democracy and communism are enemies. **Nazism** added biological racism, extreme **antisemitism** (hostility to Jews), and a demand for "living space" (*Lebensraum*) in Eastern Europe.

**How it developed:**
- **Italy.** Benito Mussolini founded the Fascist movement in 1919. He took power after the "March on Rome" in 1922 and built a dictatorship by 1925–26.
- **Germany.** Adolf Hitler's National Socialist (Nazi) party won about 37% of the vote in July 1932, its peak in a free election. Hitler was appointed chancellor in January 1933, and the Enabling Act (March 1933) let him rule without parliament. Within months, other parties were banned, opponents were sent to concentration camps, and Jews were excluded from public life. Jews were stripped of citizenship by the Nuremberg Laws of 1935, and the state-organized "Kristallnacht" pogrom followed in November 1938.
- **Related regimes.** Similar or allied regimes arose in Spain (Franco, 1939–1975, after a civil war that killed roughly 500,000), Croatia (Ustaše), Romania, Hungary, and elsewhere. Imperial Japan, a militarist-nationalist state allied with Germany and Italy, is classed as fascist by some scholars and not by others [Contested].

**Documented outcomes:**
- **War.** Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939, starting World War II in Europe. Japan had been at war in China since 1937. The war killed roughly 70–85 million people, about 3% of the world's population, most of them civilians.
- **The Holocaust.** About **6 million Jews**, roughly two-thirds of Europe's Jews, were murdered by Nazi Germany and its collaborators. They were killed by mass shooting, in ghettos, and in extermination camps such as Auschwitz-Birkenau, where about 1 million Jews were killed.
- **Other victims of Nazi Germany:** roughly 200,000–500,000 Roma; roughly 250,000–300,000 disabled people murdered in "euthanasia" programs; about 3 million Soviet prisoners of war, mostly through deliberate starvation; and millions of Polish and other Slavic civilians.
- **Fascist Italy** used poison gas in its 1935–36 conquest of Ethiopia.
- **Imperial Japan** killed millions of civilians in China and Southeast Asia, including the Nanjing massacre of 1937–38 (tens of thousands to ~300,000 killed [range Contested]).
- **Economy.** Nazi Germany cut unemployment from about 6 million in 1932 to near zero by 1938, largely through rearmament and deficit spending. Historians such as Adam Tooze argue the economy was organized for a war of conquest and was unsustainable without it.
- **End.** Fascist Italy and Nazi Germany were destroyed by military defeat in 1945. Leading Nazis were tried at Nuremberg (1945–46), which helped found modern international criminal law (see 7.5). Fascist parties remain banned or restricted in Germany, Italy, and other countries.

**Totalitarianism:** Hannah Arendt (1951) and others grouped Nazi Germany with Stalin's USSR as **totalitarian**: regimes with an official ideology, a single mass party, terror police, a monopoly on media and weapons, and central control of the economy. The term is also applied to Mao's China and North Korea. Critics argue the concept was shaped by Cold War politics and overstates how completely these regimes controlled society [Contested].

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Why it attracted followers:** Supporters at the time claimed fascism would restore national pride after defeat, end class conflict and political chaos, defeat communism, and deliver jobs and order. Many conservatives and business leaders backed it as a bulwark against the left.
- **What the record shows:** In both major cases, fascism produced dictatorship, wars of aggression, genocide, and national destruction. No major scholarly school defends it today. Scholars debate the definition, for example Roger Griffin's "palingenetic ultranationalism" (ultranationalism built on national rebirth) and Robert Paxton's focus on what fascists *did*. They also debate whether any contemporary far-right movements should be called fascist [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- Benito Mussolini and Giovanni Gentile, "The Doctrine of Fascism" (1932): official statement of fascist ideology.
- Adolf Hitler, *Mein Kampf* (1925): Hitler's racial and expansionist program, studied as a historical document.
- Hannah Arendt, *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951).
- Raul Hilberg, *The Destruction of the European Jews* (1961): foundational Holocaust history.
- Primo Levi, *If This Is a Man* (1947): survivor's account of Auschwitz.
- Robert Paxton, *The Anatomy of Fascism* (2004); Adam Tooze, *The Wages of Destruction* (2006).

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: The Holocaust and the scale of Nazi crimes are among the most thoroughly documented events in history. Holocaust denial contradicts overwhelming evidence, including Nazi Germany's own records.
- Debated: The precise definition of fascism, the usefulness of "totalitarianism" as a category, and the causes of fascism's rise (economic crisis, nationalism, fear of communism, institutional failure).
- Matters of belief or value: fascism's core values themselves.

**Open questions:** Which conditions allow democracies to fall to extremist movements, and which safeguards reliably stop them?

### 9.8 Other Movements

**In one sentence:** Beyond the major ideologies above, several movements have shaped politics on particular questions: the state itself (anarchism), the status of women (feminism), the natural environment (environmentalism), the people versus elites (populism), and the role of religion in government (religious political movements).

**What an alien needs to know first:** These are not all full ideologies. Populism, for example, is usually described as a "thin" ideology that attaches itself to left or right programs. Each has many internal strands that disagree with one another.

**Anarchism**
- **Core values [Belief/Value]:** all coercive hierarchy, especially the state, is illegitimate. Society should be organized through voluntary cooperation.
- **Origins and thinkers:** Pierre-Joseph Proudhon (*What Is Property?*, 1840), Mikhail Bakunin, Peter Kropotkin (*Mutual Aid*, 1902), Emma Goldman.
- **Where tried:** Revolutionary Catalonia in Spain (1936–39), the Makhnovist territory in Ukraine (1918–21), the Zapatista areas of Chiapas, Mexico (1994–), and Kurdish-led northeast Syria (from ~2012, influenced by Murray Bookchin).
- **Outcomes:** These experiments were mostly small or short-lived, and most were ended by military force.
- **Case for:** Humans lived without states for most of their history. Elinor Ostrom showed that communities often manage shared resources well without central control.
- **Case against:** No large, durable stateless society has existed in the modern world. Stateless areas are vulnerable to armed groups and neighboring states.

**Feminism**
- **Core values [Belief/Value]:** women and men should have equal rights and status.
- **History:** Often described in "waves":
  - First wave (c. 1848–1920): votes and property rights.
  - Second wave (1960s–80s): work, reproduction, and family law.
  - Third and fourth waves (1990s–): diversity, sexual harassment, online activism.
- **Strands:** liberal feminism (equal legal rights); socialist feminism (links to class); radical feminism (patriarchy as the root system); intersectional feminism (Kimberlé Crenshaw, 1989: overlapping disadvantages, e.g., of race and sex).
- **Outcomes:** Women's suffrage is now near-universal, and girls' schooling has reached parity in most countries (see 8.2).
- **Disputes:** Within feminism, debates continue over sex work and over sex versus gender identity. From outside, religious and traditionalist critics argue it undermines family and natural roles [Belief/Value]. Others argue that remaining gaps reflect choices more than discrimination [Contested].

**Environmentalism**
- **Core values [Belief/Value]:** protection of nature, either for human wellbeing or for its own sake.
- **History:** Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* (1962), the first Earth Day (1970), and Green parties (Germany's from 1980, later in coalition governments).
- **Outcomes:** The Montreal Protocol (1987) phased out most ozone-destroying chemicals, and the ozone layer is recovering [Established]. Clean-air laws sharply cut urban air pollution in rich countries. The Paris Agreement (2015) set climate goals, but global CO₂ emissions were still at record highs in the mid-2020s.
- **Strands:** These range from "eco-modernists" (technology, including nuclear power, will decouple growth from damage) to "degrowth" advocates (rich economies must shrink) [Contested].

**Populism**
- **Definition:** In political science, populism (Cas Mudde, 2004) is the view that society is split between a virtuous "pure people" and a "corrupt elite," and that politics should express the people's will directly.
- **Examples:** Left-wing populists include Hugo Chávez (Venezuela) and Syriza (Greece). Right-wing populists include Viktor Orbán (Hungary), Donald Trump (United States), and Jair Bolsonaro (Brazil).
- **Case for:** Supporters argue it gives voice to citizens ignored by technocratic elites.
- **Case against:** Critics argue it weakens courts, media, and minority rights. One cross-country study (2023) found populist governments associated with GDP per person about 10% lower after 15 years, and with erosion of judicial independence and press freedom [Refining/Contested].

**Religious political movements**
- **Core values [Belief/Value]:** law and government should follow religious principles or protect a religious identity.
- **Islamism.** This is the project of organizing politics around Islam and Islamic law. It ranges from electoral parties, such as Tunisia's Ennahda and the Muslim Brotherhood (founded in Egypt, 1928), to Iran's clerical state (since the 1979 revolution), the Taliban (ruling Afghanistan 1996–2001 and again since 2021), and the Islamic State. At its 2014–15 peak, the Islamic State controlled territory with roughly 8–10 million people and carried out mass killings, including genocide against the Yazidis.
- **Hindu nationalism.** Hindu nationalism (*Hindutva*) is the view that India is fundamentally a Hindu nation (V.D. Savarkar, 1923; the volunteer organization RSS, founded 1925). Its party, the BJP, has led India's government since 2014. Supporters credit it with development and national confidence. Critics cite rising pressure on India's ~200 million Muslims [Contested].
- **Christian nationalism** seeks to make national identity and law explicitly Christian, with influence in the United States and parts of Eastern Europe and Latin America. **Christian democracy** is a moderate, pro-democratic tradition (see 9.3).
- **Buddhist nationalism** has been linked to persecution of Myanmar's Rohingya Muslims, more than 700,000 of whom fled in 2017. **Liberation theology** (Latin America, 1960s–) combined Catholicism with socialist ideas.

**Key works and figures:** Kropotkin, *Mutual Aid* (1902); Simone de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex* (1949); Carson, *Silent Spring* (1962); Sayyid Qutb, *Milestones* (1964), an influential radical Islamist text; Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990); Cas Mudde and Cristóbal Rovira Kaltwasser, *Populism: A Very Short Introduction* (2017).

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: The ozone recovery, the expansion of women's rights, and the documented mass killings by the Islamic State.
- Debated: Whether anarchism can scale. The economic effects of populism. The best path for climate policy. Whether particular parties should be labeled populist, Islamist, or nationalist.
- Matters of belief or value: the core goals of every movement listed here.

**Open questions:** Will climate change produce new mass political movements? Is the 2010s–2020s rise of populism and religious nationalism a lasting realignment?
