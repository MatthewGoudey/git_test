# Domain 8: Society and Social Structure

Humans never live as isolated individuals. Each person is born into a family, a kin network, often an ethnic group, a social class, a settlement, and a state. Those groups shape the person's life chances long before the person can choose anything. This domain describes the main structures that sort humans into groups, how those structures work, how they have changed, and what is measured about their effects. On identity topics, where disputes are intense, it separates what has been measured from what humans argue about values.

---

### 8.1 Family and Kinship

**In one sentence:** Kinship is the set of rules by which humans decide who counts as related to whom, and the family is the small group, usually built on kinship, marriage, or both, that raises children and shares a household.

**What an alien needs to know first:** Human infants are helpless for years. A human child needs about 15–20 years of care before it is self-sufficient. That is far longer than most animals. **Kinship** is partly biological, meaning shared descent through birth, and partly social. Adoption, marriage, and "in-law" ties create relatives with no genetic link. **Marriage** is a socially recognized union, usually between two people, that sets rights and duties over sex, children, property, and inheritance. A **household** is the group that lives and eats together. It is not always the same as a family.

**How it works:**
- **Descent systems.** Societies trace lineage through the father (patrilineal, the most common pattern historically), through the mother (matrilineal, e.g., the Minangkabau of Indonesia and the Akan of Ghana), or through both (bilateral, typical of modern Europe and the Americas). Descent decides surnames, inheritance, and who must help whom.
- **Marriage forms.** **Monogamy** (one spouse) is the legal norm in most countries today. **Polygyny** (one man, several wives) was permitted in roughly 80–85% of the societies anthropologists have recorded. Even there, most men had only one wife. Today it is practiced by about 2% of humans, mostly in West and Central Africa [Refining]. **Polyandry** (one woman, several husbands) is rare, found for example in parts of Tibet and Nepal. Marriages are **arranged** by families in much of South Asia and parts of the Middle East and Africa, and are chosen by the partners themselves elsewhere.
- **Households.** Average household size ranges from about 2 people in Northern Europe to more than 8 in some West African countries. In Sweden, Germany, and Finland, around 40% of households are a single person living alone.
- **Fertility.** The **total fertility rate (TFR)** is the average number of children a woman would have over her lifetime at current rates. A TFR of about 2.1 is "replacement": it keeps a population stable in the long run. The world TFR fell from about 5 in 1950 to about 2.2–2.3 in the mid-2020s. It ranges from below 0.8 in South Korea to around 6 in Niger.

**How it developed:** Hunter-gatherer bands were built on flexible kin ties. Farming (from ~10,000 years ago) made land and herds heritable. That raised the stakes of descent and often of controlling women's sexuality and marriage. In medieval Western Europe, the Catholic Church banned cousin marriage and promoted consent-based marriage. Historians link this to the region's unusual pattern of late marriage and small nuclear households [Contested in its causal role]. Since about 1950 nearly every society has seen falling fertility, later marriage, more divorce, and more births outside marriage. Births outside marriage are about 40% of births across rich countries, over 60% in France and Iceland, and under 5% in Japan and South Korea. **Same-sex marriage** was first legalized in the Netherlands in 2001 and is legal in about 35–40 countries today.

**Effects on human life and society:** The family is the main channel through which wealth, religion, language, and status pass between generations (see 8.4). About 1 in 5 young women worldwide were married before age 18 (UNICEF estimate, early 2020s). The share is falling, but it remains above 1 in 3 in parts of sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Strong extended-kin networks provide insurance in poor societies. They are also linked to nepotism and to weaker trust in strangers [Contested]. Falling fertility is reshaping economies and pension systems (see 8.5).

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Functionalist view:** the family exists because it performs needed tasks, such as raising children, passing on culture, and pooling resources.
- **Economic view (Gary Becker):** family decisions about marriage and how many children to have respond to costs and incentives. For example, women's rising wages make childbearing more costly in lost earnings. This is well supported for fertility trends [Refining].
- **Feminist critique:** the family has historically been a site of unequal power and unpaid female labor.
- **Traditionalist view [Belief/Value]:** the two-parent married family, often defined religiously, is the foundation of a healthy society and should be protected by law and policy.

**Key works and figures:**
- Lewis Henry Morgan, *Systems of Consanguinity and Affinity* (1871): founded the comparative study of kinship.
- Claude Lévi-Strauss, *The Elementary Structures of Kinship* (1949): argued that marriage exchange binds groups together.
- John Hajnal, "European Marriage Patterns in Perspective" (1965): identified late marriage in Western Europe.
- Gary Becker, *A Treatise on the Family* (1981): economic analysis of family decisions.
- Joseph Henrich, *The WEIRDest People in the World* (2020): links church marriage rules to Western psychology [Contested].

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Global fertility has roughly halved since 1950. Kinship rules vary widely across cultures. Marriage forms are culturally variable.
- Debated: Effects of family structure on child outcomes once income is accounted for. Causes of the very low fertility in East Asia. Whether policy (cash payments, childcare) can raise birth rates much. Evidence so far suggests small effects.
- Matters of belief or value: what counts as a "real" family, whether same-sex or plural marriage should be legal, the proper role of the state in family life.

**Open questions:** Will fertility recover in very-low-fertility countries, or stay far below replacement? How will kinship change as households shrink and lifespans lengthen?

### 8.2 Sex, Gender, and Sexuality in Society

**In one sentence:** This field covers how human societies assign roles, rights, and expectations to people based on sex, and how they treat the different ways humans experience sexual attraction and identity.

**What an alien needs to know first:** Humans reproduce sexually. Nearly all are born either **female** (two X chromosomes, ovaries) or **male** (X and Y chromosomes, testes). A small minority are born with **intersex** variations that do not fit typical patterns. Estimates range from about 0.02% to 1.7% of births, depending on which conditions are counted [Contested in definition]. **Gender** usually refers to the social roles and expectations attached to sex, and to a person's own sense of being a man, a woman, or something else (**gender identity**). **Transgender** people have a gender identity different from the sex recorded at their birth. **Sexual orientation** describes whom a person is sexually attracted to: the other sex (heterosexual), the same sex (homosexual: gay or lesbian), or both (bisexual). **LGBTQ+** is a common umbrella acronym for these minorities.

**How it works:** On average, human males are about 10–15% taller than females and have much greater upper-body strength. On many psychological traits, the sexes overlap heavily. Some average differences are consistently measured, for example in physical aggression and in interest in working with things versus people. How much of these differences comes from biology and how much from upbringing is [Contested].

In almost every recorded society, roles were divided by sex. Women did most childcare and domestic work. Men held most formal political, military, and religious power. Measured gaps today (mid-2020s, approximate):

| Measure | Women | Men |
|---|---|---|
| Labor-force participation, world | ~50% | ~72% |
| Seats in national parliaments | ~27% | ~73% |
| Victims of homicide | ~20% | ~80% |
| Ever-partnered women who have experienced partner violence | ~1 in 4 | (not measured on same basis) |

Women now outnumber men in university enrollment in most countries. Women live about 5 years longer than men on average. Much of the remaining pay gap in rich countries appears when women have children (the "child penalty"). In Denmark, for example, mothers' earnings fall about 20% relative to fathers' and stay lower long term.

On sexuality: about 5–10% of adults in surveyed rich countries identify as LGBTQ+. The share is much higher among young adults, and survey methods strongly affect the result. Large genetic studies, such as one of about 500,000 people in 2019, find that same-sex attraction is influenced by many genes, each with tiny effects, plus non-genetic factors. There is no single "gay gene" [Refining]. Attempts to change orientation through "conversion therapy" have not been shown to work and are associated with harm [Established].

**How it developed:** Women had no vote anywhere at national level until New Zealand granted it in 1893. Saudi Arabia was among the last to allow women to vote (2015, in local elections). Mary Wollstonecraft (1792) and the Seneca Falls convention in the United States (1848) mark early organized women's rights movements. Effective contraception, especially the birth-control pill (1960), let women control fertility and accelerated their entry into higher education and paid work. Same-sex acts were crimes in most of Europe and its colonies until the late 20th century. They remain criminal in about 60–65 countries, and a handful can impose the death penalty. Afghanistan has barred girls from secondary school since 2021.

**Effects on human life and society:** Sex shapes life expectancy, income, occupation, risk of violence, legal rights, and political voice for all 8 billion humans. Women's education is strongly associated with lower fertility and lower child mortality [Established as association; largely causal].

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Traditional or religious views [Belief/Value]:** distinct sex roles are natural or divinely ordained, marriage is between a man and a woman, and same-sex relations are wrong.
- **Liberal feminist and equal-rights views [Belief/Value]:** people should have equal legal rights and opportunities regardless of sex or orientation.
- **Gender-identity debates (active in the 2020s):** These cover whether legal sex should follow self-identified gender, whether transgender women should compete in women's sports, and how to treat gender-distressed minors. On the last, a 2024 independent review in England (the Cass Review) found weak evidence for puberty-blocking drugs, and several European countries and US states have restricted them. Other medical bodies continue to support access. These disputes mix [Contested] empirical questions with [Belief/Value] questions about fairness and identity.

**Key works and figures:**
- Mary Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* (1792): early argument for women's education and reason.
- John Stuart Mill, *The Subjection of Women* (1869): liberal case for sex equality.
- Simone de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex* (1949): "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman."
- Alfred Kinsey, reports on male (1948) and female (1953) sexual behavior: early large-scale surveys.
- Judith Butler, *Gender Trouble* (1990): influential theory of gender as performance [Contested].
- Claudia Goldin, *Career and Family* (2021): Nobel-winning work on the history of women's work and pay.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Women's legal rights, education, and work have expanded greatly since 1900. Large sex gaps in political power and violence persist. Sexual orientation is not chosen by simple decision.
- Debated: The relative weight of biology and socialization in average psychological sex differences. Causes of remaining pay gaps. Outcomes of youth gender medicine.
- Matters of belief or value: the proper roles of men and women, the morality of same-sex relations, how to balance competing claims of sex-based and gender-identity-based rights.

**Open questions:** What biological and developmental processes shape sexual orientation and gender identity? Why do sex differences in occupational choice tend to be *larger* in some richer, more gender-equal countries [Contested]?

**See also:** 1.5 Sex, Reproduction, and the Life Cycle; 8.1 Family and Kinship; 9.8 Other Movements (feminism); 7.2 Constitutions and Rights; 10.7 Religion's Effects on Society.

### 8.3 Race, Ethnicity, and Identity

**In one sentence:** Humans sort themselves and others into groups by ancestry, appearance, language, and culture, and these groupings strongly affect how people are treated, even though they map only loosely onto human biology.

**What an alien needs to know first:** An **ethnic group** is a group whose members believe they share ancestry, history, and culture, often including a language or religion. Examples are Han Chinese, Yoruba, Kurds, and Basques. One survey counted about 800 ethnic groups that each make up at least 1% of some country's population. **Race** is a category, mostly based on visible traits such as skin color and facial features, that sorts humans into a few large groups (e.g., "Black," "white," "Asian"). **Racism** is the belief that some races are inherently superior, or treatment of people that disadvantages them because of their race. **Discrimination** is unequal treatment based on group membership.

**How it works: what genetics shows.**
- Humans are a young species with low genetic diversity. Two random humans differ at only about 1 in 1,000 DNA letters, less than two chimpanzees from different groups typically differ.
- Most human genetic variation, roughly 85–90%, is found *within* any given population. Only about 5–15% lies between continental groups (first shown by Richard Lewontin in 1972 and confirmed with whole genomes) [Established].
- Variation is mostly **clinal**: it changes gradually across geography rather than in sharp breaks. Africa holds the most genetic diversity, because humans lived there longest.
- Ancestry *can* be estimated statistically from a genome. Some traits, such as skin color (an adaptation to sunlight levels), lactose tolerance, and sickle-cell risk, do differ in frequency between populations.
- Racial categories differ between countries and eras. The United States, Brazil, and South Africa each sort people differently, and US census categories have changed many times. Most geneticists and anthropologists therefore describe race as a **social category with a loose, imperfect relationship to ancestry**, not as discrete biological types [Established as majority view; details debated].

**How it works: what discrimination studies show.** In **audit studies**, researchers send identical job applications that differ only in a name signaling ethnicity. A 2017 meta-analysis of US studies found that white applicants received about 36% more callbacks than Black applicants, with no decline since 1989. Similar studies in France, Germany, the Netherlands, the UK, and elsewhere find penalties for applicants with Arab, African, or other minority names [Established].

**How it developed:** Group prejudice is ancient, but modern "race science" arose in 18th–19th-century Europe. It ranked humans into hierarchies and was used to justify slavery and colonial rule. The Atlantic slave trade (~1500s–1860s) shipped about 12.5 million Africans to the Americas, and roughly 1.5–2 million died during the crossing. Racial ideology underpinned the Nazi Holocaust (see 9.7), US racial segregation (ended in law by the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965), and apartheid in South Africa (1948–1994). Ethnic conflict has also driven genocides without "race" in the Western sense: Armenians in the Ottoman Empire (1915–1917, roughly 0.6–1.5 million killed) and Tutsi in Rwanda (1994, roughly 500,000–800,000 killed in about 100 days).

**Effects on human life and society:** Many countries show large group gaps in wealth, health, and incarceration. In the United States, the median white household holds roughly 5–6 times the wealth of the median Black household. Ethnic divisions predict civil-war risk in some studies [Contested]. Identity shapes voting in many democracies (see 6.3).

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Colorblind or universalist view [Belief/Value]:** the state should ignore race and treat individuals identically. Some countries, such as France, do not collect ethnic statistics.
- **Race-conscious view [Belief/Value]:** past and ongoing discrimination requires targeted remedies, such as affirmative action (preferences for disadvantaged groups) or reparations. The US Supreme Court banned race-conscious university admissions in 2023. India constitutionally reserves posts for disadvantaged castes and tribes (see 8.4).
- **Causes of group gaps [Contested]:** Explanations include current discrimination, inherited wealth gaps, neighborhood and school effects, and cultural factors. Measured average test-score gaps between groups exist in some countries and have narrowed over time. A small minority of researchers argue that part of these gaps is genetic. No genetic evidence has shown this, and most researchers attribute the gaps to environmental factors.
- **Contact hypothesis:** structured contact between groups reduces prejudice. It is supported by many studies, with weaker evidence for lasting effects [Refining].

**Key works and figures:**
- W.E.B. Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903): described the "color line" and "double consciousness."
- Gunnar Myrdal, *An American Dilemma* (1944): landmark study of US racial inequality.
- Gordon Allport, *The Nature of Prejudice* (1954): founding psychology of prejudice.
- Fredrik Barth, *Ethnic Groups and Boundaries* (1969): ethnicity as boundaries groups maintain, not fixed traits.
- Richard Lewontin, "The Apportionment of Human Diversity" (1972): within- vs. between-group genetic variation.
- David Reich, *Who We Are and How We Got Here* (2018): ancient-DNA evidence on human population mixing.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Most genetic variation is within groups. Racial categories vary by society. Measurable hiring discrimination exists in many countries. Slavery and segregation produced lasting wealth gaps.
- Debated: The relative weight of the causes of present-day gaps. The effectiveness of affirmative action and diversity training (evidence for the latter's lasting effects is weak).
- Matters of belief or value: whether law should be race-blind or race-conscious, reparations, how much ethnic identity should shape national identity.

**Open questions:** How fast do historical group disadvantages fade, and what speeds that up? How will rising intermarriage and migration change racial categories?

**See also:** 1.1 Evolution and Human Origins; 1.2 Genetics and Heredity; 4.5 Colonialism, Revolutions, and Industry; 6.6 War and Organized Violence; 9.6 Nationalism; 15.6 Cooperation, Conflict, and Group Behavior.

### 8.4 Social Class and Mobility

**In one sentence:** Human societies are layered into groups with unequal wealth, power, and prestige, and "mobility" measures how easily people move between those layers across a lifetime or between parent and child.

**What an alien needs to know first:** **Stratification** means the ranking of a society into layers. A **class** is a layer defined mainly by economic position: what a person owns, what work they do, and how much they earn. **Status** is social prestige, which can differ from wealth. A **caste** is an inherited rank, fixed at birth, usually with rules against marrying outside it. **Social mobility** is movement between layers. **Intergenerational mobility** compares children with their parents. An **elite** is the small group at the top that holds outsized wealth or power.

**How it works:**
- **Economic layering.** By World Inequality Lab estimates for the early 2020s, the richest 10% of humans receive about half of global income and own about three-quarters of global wealth. The poorest 50% own about 2% [Refining]. By one common definition (spending above roughly $11 a day), about half of humanity now lives in "middle-class or richer" households, a share reached only around 2018 [Refining].
- **Caste.** India's caste system combines four broad ritual ranks (**varnas**) with thousands of hereditary occupational groups (**jatis**). Below and outside the varnas were groups treated as "untouchable," who now often call themselves **Dalits**. India's 1950 constitution outlawed untouchability and reserved university places, government jobs, and legislative seats for "Scheduled Castes" and "Scheduled Tribes." Scheduled Castes are about 16–17% of India's population, over 200 million people. Inter-caste marriage remains uncommon, roughly 5–10% of marriages [Refining]. Caste-like hereditary groups also exist elsewhere, for example the Burakumin of Japan and some groups in West Africa.
- **Measuring mobility.** A common measure is the **intergenerational earnings elasticity**: the share of a parent's income advantage that shows up in the child's income. Approximate values are 0.15–0.2 in Denmark, Norway, and Finland; about 0.4–0.5 in the United States and the United Kingdom; and 0.5–0.7 in Brazil, Chile, and China [Refining]. Higher numbers mean less mobility. Countries with more inequality tend to have less mobility, a pattern nicknamed the **"Great Gatsby curve"** [Refining as a correlation; causes debated].
- **Absolute mobility.** In the United States, about 90% of children born in 1940 grew up to earn more than their parents (adjusted for inflation). For those born in 1980, it was about 50%.

**How it developed:** Hunter-gatherer bands were relatively equal in material terms. Farming allowed storable surplus, inheritance, and the rise of hereditary elites: kings, priests, aristocrats, and enslaved underclasses. For most of recorded history, rank was largely fixed at birth. The industrial era created a mass **working class** (wage laborers) and a growing **middle class** of professionals, managers, and small owners. In the 20th century, rich democracies expanded mass schooling, progressive taxes, and welfare states. Inequality within them fell from about 1914 to 1980, then rose again in many countries, most sharply in the United States [Refining].

**Effects on human life and society:** Class predicts health, lifespan, education, and political influence. In the United States, the richest 1% of men live about 15 years longer than the poorest 1%. Children from top-income families are vastly over-represented at elite universities. At some elite US colleges, more students come from the top 1% of families than from the bottom 60%. Large studies also find that friendships across class lines strongly predict upward mobility [Refining].

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Marxian view:** class is defined by ownership of the **means of production** (land, factories, capital). Owners (the bourgeoisie) and workers (the proletariat) have opposed interests, and class conflict drives history (see 9.4) [Contested as a full theory].
- **Weberian view:** stratification has several independent dimensions: economic class, social status, and political power.
- **Functionalist view (Davis and Moore, 1945):** inequality motivates people to fill demanding, important roles [Contested].
- **Meritocracy [Belief/Value]:** positions should go to the most able and hardworking. Critics, including the man who coined the word (Michael Young, 1958), warn that it can harden into a new, self-justifying elite.
- **Cultural capital (Pierre Bourdieu):** elites pass on advantage through tastes, manners, and networks, not just money.

**Key works and figures:**
- Karl Marx, *Capital* (1867): class as the engine of history.
- Max Weber, *Economy and Society* (1922): class, status, and party as separate dimensions.
- B.R. Ambedkar, *Annihilation of Caste* (1936): Dalit leader's attack on caste. Ambedkar later chaired the drafting of India's constitution.
- Michael Young, *The Rise of the Meritocracy* (1958): satire that coined "meritocracy."
- Pierre Bourdieu, *Distinction* (1979): taste and culture as class markers.
- Raj Chetty and colleagues, Equality of Opportunity / Opportunity Insights studies (2014 onward): large tax-data studies of mobility.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Class background strongly predicts life outcomes everywhere. Mobility varies a great deal between countries. Caste discrimination persists in India despite legal bans.
- Debated: How much low mobility comes from unequal opportunity versus inherited traits and family environment. Whether rising inequality reduces mobility. Which policies raise mobility most.
- Matters of belief or value: how much inequality is fair, whether outcomes or only opportunities should be equalized, the legitimacy of inheritance.

**Open questions:** How much of the transmission of advantage between generations is genetic, how much is wealth, and how much is environment? Will automation and AI widen or narrow class gaps?

**See also:** 5.5 Inequality; 5.6 Government and the Economy; 8.7 Education; 9.4 Socialism and Communism; 10.3 Religions of South and East Asia.

### 8.5 Population and Migration

**In one sentence:** Demography is the study of how many humans there are, how they are born, age, and die, and how they move across the planet.

**What an alien needs to know first:** A population changes through births, deaths, and **migration** (people moving to live somewhere else). The **birth rate** and **death rate** are usually stated per 1,000 people per year. **Life expectancy** is the average age at death that a newborn would reach at current death rates. A **migrant** is anyone living outside their place of birth. A **refugee** is someone who has fled their country because of persecution or war and is protected under international law (the 1951 Refugee Convention). An **internally displaced person** has fled but remains inside their own country.

**How it works: the demographic transition.** Nearly every society has followed the same broad sequence [Established as a pattern; timing varies]:
1. High births, high deaths, slow growth. This was the human condition until about 1800.
2. Deaths fall as food, sanitation, and medicine improve. Births stay high, so the population booms.
3. Births fall as children survive, education spreads (especially for women), cities grow, and contraception becomes available.
4. Low births, low deaths. Growth stops, and the population ages.

The result is shown in the table below.

| Year | World population |
|---|---|
| 1800 | ~1 billion |
| 1950 | ~2.5 billion |
| 2000 | ~6.1 billion |
| 2022 | 8 billion |
| 2080s (UN projection) | peak ~10.3 billion [Refining] |

Growth peaked at about 2.1% per year in the late 1960s and is now under 1%. The global median age is about 31. It is about 49 in Japan and about 15 in Niger. About 10% of humans are over 65, a share projected to roughly double by 2070–2100. Most future growth will be in sub-Saharan Africa, whose population is projected to grow from about 1.2 billion to over 2 billion by 2050.

**Migration in numbers (mid-2020s, approximate):**
- About 300 million international migrants, roughly 3.7% of humanity. Most migrate for work or family.
- About 120 million people forcibly displaced. Of these, roughly 40 million are refugees or in need of international protection, and roughly 70 million are internally displaced.
- Migrants sent about US$650–700 billion home to low- and middle-income countries in 2024 (these payments are called **remittances**). That is more than all foreign aid and foreign direct investment to those countries.
- The United States has the most immigrants (about 50 million). In the United Arab Emirates and Qatar, about 85–90% of residents are foreign-born.

**How it developed:** Humans have migrated since they left Africa ~60,000–70,000 years ago. Large historical movements include the forced transport of Africans in the slave trade and European emigration to the Americas and Oceania (~50–60 million people, 1815–1930). The **Partition of India** in 1947 displaced about 15 million people. Thomas Malthus (1798) predicted that population would always outrun food. Over the next two centuries, food production grew faster than population [Established]. Paul Ehrlich's *The Population Bomb* (1968) predicted mass famines in the 1970s that did not happen at that scale. China's one-child policy (1980–2015) is the largest attempt at direct state control of fertility.

**Effects on human life and society:** Aging populations raise pension and health costs and shrink workforces, for example in Japan, South Korea, Italy, and China. Young, fast-growing populations need jobs and schools. Migration is among the most politically divisive issues in rich democracies (see 9.6, 9.8).

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Economic effects of immigration [Contested at the margins]:** Most studies find small average effects on native workers' wages. Some find larger effects on low-skilled workers. A well-known example is the dispute over the 1980 Mariel boatlift into Miami: David Card found little effect, and George Borjas's re-analysis found sizable losses for some groups. Fiscal effects depend heavily on migrants' age and skills.
- **Restrictionist views [Belief/Value]:** nations have a right to control membership and preserve culture, and high immigration strains wages, housing, and cohesion.
- **Open or high-immigration views [Belief/Value]:** freedom of movement is a right or a large economic gain. Some economists estimate that removing migration barriers could raise world output greatly [Contested].
- **Population concerns:** Debate has shifted from fear of overpopulation toward concern about very low fertility in some countries [Belief/Value about which is worse].

**Key works and figures:**
- Thomas Malthus, *An Essay on the Principle of Population* (1798): population tends to outrun food.
- Frank Notestein and Warren Thompson (1929–1945): formalized the demographic transition.
- Paul Ehrlich, *The Population Bomb* (1968): influential, largely failed predictions.
- UN *World Population Prospects* (biennial): the standard global projections.
- The 1951 Refugee Convention and its 1967 Protocol: the legal basis of refugee protection.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Population growth is slowing. Fertility has fallen in nearly every country. Remittances are a major financial flow.
- Debated: When and at what level world population will peak. The labor-market and fiscal effects of immigration. Whether climate change will drive large migrations.
- Matters of belief or value: who should be allowed to migrate, duties to refugees, whether governments should try to raise or lower birth rates.

**Open questions:** Will African fertility fall as fast as Asia's did? How will rich countries sustain aging societies? How many people will climate change displace?

**See also:** 1.5 Sex, Reproduction, and the Life Cycle; 8.1 Family and Kinship; 8.6 Cities and Settlement; 7.5 International Law and Human Rights; 16.6 Demographic Change and Aging.

### 8.6 Cities and Settlement

**In one sentence:** Cities are dense, permanent concentrations of people who mostly do not grow their own food, and since 2007 more humans have lived in urban areas than in the countryside.

**What an alien needs to know first:** A **city** is a large, dense settlement whose residents mostly work in trade, manufacturing, services, and government rather than farming. **Urbanization** is the shift of a population from rural areas to cities. A **megacity** is an urban area of more than 10 million people. A **slum** or **informal settlement** is housing built without legal title or planning, often lacking safe water, sanitation, or durable buildings. Countries define "urban" very differently, from settlements of 200 people to settlements of 50,000. Global totals therefore depend on definitions [Refining].

**How it works:**
- **Scale.** About 55–58% of humans live in urban areas by national definitions, roughly 4.5–4.7 billion people. A UN method released in 2025, which applies one standard definition everywhere, counts about 45% in cities, about a third in towns, and under a fifth in rural areas [Refining].
- **Size.** There are about 35–45 megacities, depending on how boundaries are drawn. The largest urban regions include Tokyo, Jakarta, Dhaka, Delhi, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Manila, Cairo, São Paulo, and Mexico City, each with roughly 20–40 million people.
- **Why cities exist.** Cities create **agglomeration economies**: workers, firms, and ideas gain from being close together. Studies find that larger cities have higher productivity and wages. Many outputs, such as patents and income, rise somewhat faster than population when a city grows [Refining in exact size].
- **Costs.** Crowding raises land and housing prices, congestion, pollution, and historically disease. About 1 billion people live in slums or informal settlements, mostly in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa. Cities produce an estimated ~70–80% of world GDP and ~70% of energy-related carbon dioxide emissions [Refining].

**How it developed:** Permanent villages followed farming. The first true cities arose in Mesopotamia ~5,500–5,000 years ago; Uruk held perhaps 40,000+ people. Others arose independently in Egypt, the Indus Valley, China, Mesoamerica, and the Andes. Ancient Rome reached roughly 1 million people around 100 CE. For most of history only about 5–10% of humans lived in cities, because farming could not free more people from producing food. The Industrial Revolution changed this. Britain became majority-urban around 1850. The world was ~30% urban in 1950 and passed 50% around 2007. Urban growth today is fastest in Africa and South Asia.

**Effects on human life and society:** On average, city dwellers have higher incomes, better access to schooling and health care, and lower fertility than rural people. Moving to cities has been a main route out of poverty, as in China, whose urban population grew by roughly 700 million between 1980 and 2020, largely through rural-to-urban migration [Refining]. In many rich-country cities, housing costs have risen much faster than incomes since the 2000s. This has become a major political issue. Rural areas in many countries are aging and losing population.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Urban economics:** density raises productivity, and restrictive zoning (rules on what may be built where) limits housing supply and raises prices. There is strong evidence for supply effects [Refining], though the size of effects is debated.
- **Planning traditions:** Modernist planning (Le Corbusier) favored towers and highways. Jane Jacobs argued for mixed-use, walkable neighborhoods. Both shaped real cities, and the outcomes of large modernist projects (e.g., some 1950s–70s public housing) were often poor [Refining].
- **Urban critique:** from Friedrich Engels (1845) to Mike Davis (2006), cities as sites of exploitation and squalor.
- **Values debates [Belief/Value]:** the car versus public transit, growth versus preservation, and whether existing residents should be able to block new housing.

**Key works and figures:**
- Friedrich Engels, *The Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845): early industrial city life.
- Max Weber, *The City* (1921): comparative sociology of cities.
- Lewis Mumford, *The City in History* (1961): sweeping history of urban life.
- Jane Jacobs, *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* (1961): defense of dense, diverse neighborhoods.
- Mike Davis, *Planet of Slums* (2006): global informal settlements.
- Edward Glaeser, *Triumph of the City* (2011): economic case for cities.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: The world is majority-urban by most definitions. Cities have higher average productivity. Urbanization accompanies economic development.
- Debated: The exact urban share (a definitional question). How much zoning, versus demand, drives housing costs. Whether remote work (which expanded after COVID-19) will reshape cities long-term.
- Matters of belief or value: how cities should look and who should be able to live where.

**Open questions:** Can fast-growing African and South Asian cities provide housing, water, and jobs at the needed scale? How will cities adapt to heat and sea-level rise?

### 8.7 Education

**In one sentence:** Education is the deliberate transmission of knowledge and skills, and over the last two centuries humans have built mass school systems that now enroll most of the world's children.

**What an alien needs to know first:** Humans learn a great deal informally, from family and peers. **Formal education** happens in **schools**, institutions where trained teachers instruct groups of students according to a set **curriculum** (a plan of what to learn). Systems are usually divided into **primary** (ages ~6–11), **secondary** (~12–18), and **tertiary** (universities and colleges) levels. **Literacy** is the ability to read and write. **Numeracy** is basic skill with numbers.

**How it works:**
- **Scale.** Roughly 1.5 billion students are enrolled in schools and universities worldwide. Primary enrollment is near-universal in most countries. Still, about 250–270 million school-age children are out of school, concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia.
- **Schooling versus learning.** Attendance does not guarantee learning. The World Bank estimated (2022) that about 70% of 10-year-olds in low- and middle-income countries could not read and understand a simple text. That figure rose after COVID-19 school closures [Refining].
- **Who pays.** Most primary and secondary schooling is publicly funded and compulsory. Governments spend roughly 4–5% of GDP on education on average.
- **Universities.** About 250 million students are in tertiary education, and roughly 40% of young people worldwide enroll in it, from under 10% in some poor countries to over 80% in some rich ones.
- **International testing.** The **PISA** tests (since 2000), run by the OECD, compare 15-year-olds across about 80 education systems. East Asian systems (e.g., Singapore, Japan, mainland Chinese provinces) usually score highest.

**How it developed:** Early schools trained scribes and priests in Mesopotamia and Egypt (~2000s BCE). China's imperial civil-service examinations (605–1905 CE) selected officials by tested knowledge of classical texts. Major early centers of higher learning include Nalanda in India (~5th century CE), al-Qarawiyyin in Fez (859) and al-Azhar in Cairo (~970), and Bologna (1088) and Paris (~1150) in Europe. The printing press (~1450) and the Protestant Reformation, which urged believers to read scripture, spread literacy in Europe. Prussia made schooling compulsory in 1763. Most industrial nations did so in the 1800s, and most former colonies after independence. World adult literacy rose from about 12% in 1820 to about 87% today. Average years of schooling for adults rose from about 2 in 1950 to about 8–9 today [Refining].

**Effects on human life and society:** Each extra year of schooling is associated with about 8–10% higher earnings worldwide [Refining]. More educated people live longer and are healthier. Women's education is strongly linked to lower fertility and child mortality. Education has historically been a main channel of social mobility, and also of reproducing advantage (see 8.4). States use schools to build national identity and loyalty (see 9.6).

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Human capital theory (Becker, 1964):** education raises productivity, which is why it raises wages.
- **Signaling theory [Contested]:** much of education's payoff comes from proving to employers that a person is capable and diligent, not from what was learned. Economists dispute how large this share is.
- **Progressive education (John Dewey):** learning by doing and inquiry. **Traditional or direct instruction:** structured, teacher-led teaching of a set body of knowledge. Rigorous trials tend to support structured teaching of early reading, such as phonics [Refining].
- **Critical pedagogy (Paulo Freire):** education as a tool of liberation or of oppression.
- **Values disputes [Belief/Value]:** religious versus secular schooling, public versus private schools and parental choice, what history and values to teach, and debates over how sex, race, and national history are taught.

**Key works and figures:**
- Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *Emile* (1762): child-centered education.
- John Dewey, *Democracy and Education* (1916): progressive schooling.
- Gary Becker, *Human Capital* (1964): education as investment.
- The Coleman Report (US, 1966): found that family background predicts achievement more than school resources [Refining; still discussed].
- Paulo Freire, *Pedagogy of the Oppressed* (1968): education and power.
- Eric Hanushek and Ludger Woessmann, *The Knowledge Capital of Nations* (2015): measured skills, not years, predict growth [Refining].

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Literacy and enrollment have expanded enormously since 1800. Education strongly correlates with income and health. Learning outcomes vary greatly between countries.
- Debated: How much of education's payoff is skills versus signaling. Which reforms (class size, teacher pay, school choice, technology) improve learning most. The long-term effects of COVID-era learning losses.
- Matters of belief or value: the purposes of education (work, citizenship, faith, personal growth) and who should control curricula.

**Open questions:** How can poor countries turn school attendance into actual learning? How will AI tutoring and AI-driven job change alter what and how humans need to learn?
