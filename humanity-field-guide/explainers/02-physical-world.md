# Domain 2: The Physical World as Humans Understand It

This domain summarizes humans' current model of the universe: what matter and energy are, how they behave, what lies beyond Earth, how Earth itself works, and how life on it is organized. It is the most rigorously tested knowledge humans have, checked by measurement and prediction, and it underlies all their technology. The reader may well know more physics than humans do; the purpose here is to show *what humans know and how confidently they know it*.

*Figures are approximate and describe the mid-2020s unless a date is given.*

### 2.1 Physics

**In one sentence:** Physics is the human study of matter, energy, space, and time, and of the small number of rules that describe how they behave everywhere humans have looked.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- Humans measure with an agreed international system of units (see 3.4): the **meter** (length), **kilogram** (mass), **second** (time), and units built from them, such as the **joule** (energy) and **watt** (one joule per second, a rate of energy use).
- **Energy** is the capacity to do work or cause change. It comes in forms (motion, heat, chemical, electrical, nuclear) that convert into one another.
- A **force** is a push or pull that changes an object's motion.
- In science, a **law** is a precise description of a regularity, and a **theory** is a well-tested explanation of many facts, not a guess (see 3.3).

**How it works:**
Humans' current physics rests on a few frameworks, each extremely well tested within its range:

| Framework | Covers | Key claims |
|---|---|---|
| **Classical mechanics** (Newton, 1687) | Everyday objects, planets | Objects keep moving unless a force acts; force equals mass times acceleration; gravity pulls all masses toward each other |
| **Electromagnetism** (Maxwell, 1860s) | Electricity, magnetism, light | Electric and magnetic fields are linked; light is a traveling electromagnetic wave moving at ~300,000 km per second |
| **Thermodynamics** (1800s) | Heat and energy | Energy is never created or destroyed, only converted; **entropy** (roughly, disorder or the spreading-out of energy) of an isolated system tends to increase, which is why heat flows from hot to cold and no engine is 100% efficient |
| **Relativity** (Einstein, 1905 and 1915) | High speeds, strong gravity | The speed of light is the same for all observers; time and space stretch depending on motion; mass and energy are equivalent (E = mc²); gravity is the curving of space and time by mass |
| **Quantum mechanics** (1900–1930) | Atoms and smaller | Energy comes in discrete packets ("quanta"); particles behave like waves; outcomes are given as probabilities |

Humans identify four **fundamental forces**: gravity, electromagnetism, the **strong force** (holds atomic nuclei together), and the **weak force** (causes some radioactive decay). The **Standard Model of particle physics** (assembled in the 1970s) describes all known particles and three of the forces. Its last predicted particle, the **Higgs boson**, was detected in 2012 at the Large Hadron Collider, a 27-km particle accelerator near Geneva.

These theories are used daily. Satellite navigation (GPS) would drift by about 10 km per day without corrections from relativity. Transistors, lasers, and solar panels depend on quantum mechanics. Nuclear power and nuclear weapons depend on E = mc².

**How it developed:** Many ancient societies studied motion and light; Greek thinkers such as Aristotle (~350 BCE) proposed theories that dominated for centuries. Ibn al-Haytham's *Book of Optics* (~1021) used experiment to study light. Galileo Galilei (early 1600s) combined experiment with mathematics. Isaac Newton's *Principia* (1687) unified motion on Earth and in the heavens. Michael Faraday and James Clerk Maxwell unified electricity, magnetism, and light (1830s–1860s). Thermodynamics grew alongside the steam engine. Max Planck (1900) and Albert Einstein (1905) began quantum theory; Einstein's relativity followed (1905, 1915). Nuclear fission was discovered in 1938, leading to atomic bombs in 1945. Gravitational waves (ripples in space-time) predicted by Einstein were detected in 2015.

**Effects on human life and society:** Physics underlies electricity (which ~90% of humans have access to), engines, electronics, computers, medical imaging, telecommunications, and nuclear weapons (about 12,000 warheads exist). The application of physics, through engineering, is a central cause of the rise in material wealth since 1800 (see 12.1, 12.3).

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Interpretations of quantum mechanics** [Contested]: the mathematics is agreed and predicts experiments extremely well, but what it means is not. The **Copenhagen interpretation** says measurement produces definite outcomes; the **many-worlds interpretation** says all possible outcomes occur in branching realities; other views exist. No experiment has yet distinguished them.
- **Unification:** many physicists seek a single "theory of everything." **String theory** and **loop quantum gravity** are candidates; neither has experimental support yet [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- Isaac Newton, *Principia Mathematica* (1687): laws of motion and gravity.
- James Clerk Maxwell, equations of electromagnetism (1860s).
- Albert Einstein, special (1905) and general (1915) relativity.
- Niels Bohr, Werner Heisenberg, Erwin Schrödinger (1913–1927): quantum mechanics.
- Marie Curie (1890s–1910s): radioactivity; first person to win two Nobel Prizes.
- Emmy Noether (1918): showed that conservation laws follow from symmetries.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: classical mechanics, electromagnetism, thermodynamics, relativity, and quantum mechanics within their tested domains; the Standard Model's particles.
- Debated: how to combine gravity with quantum mechanics; what dark matter and dark energy are (see 2.3); the meaning of quantum mechanics.
- Matters of belief or value: whether the universe has a purpose or creator; why the laws of physics are as they are (sometimes argued from both religious and non-religious positions).

**Open questions:** A quantum theory of gravity. Why the universe contains much more matter than antimatter. The nature of dark matter and dark energy. What happens inside black holes. Whether the physical constants could have been different.

### 2.2 Chemistry and Matter

**In one sentence:** Chemistry is the study of what things are made of, namely about a hundred kinds of atoms, and of how those atoms bond and rearrange to make every substance humans know.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- An **atom** is the smallest unit of a chemical element. It has a tiny, dense **nucleus** of **protons** (positive charge) and **neutrons** (no charge), surrounded by much lighter **electrons** (negative charge). A typical atom is about one ten-billionth of a meter across.
- An **element** is a substance made of only one kind of atom, defined by its number of protons. Hydrogen has 1, carbon 6, oxygen 8, gold 79.
- A **molecule** is two or more atoms bonded together, for example water (H₂O: two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom).
- A **chemical reaction** rearranges atoms into new molecules; the atoms themselves are not created or destroyed.

**How it works:**
Humans know 118 elements; about 90 occur naturally on Earth and the rest have been made in laboratories, most lasting only fractions of a second. The **periodic table** arranges elements by proton number in rows and columns so that elements with similar behavior line up. That behavior is set mainly by each atom's outer electrons.

Atoms join through **chemical bonds**: **covalent bonds** (sharing electrons, as in water and most living matter), **ionic bonds** (one atom gives electrons to another, as in table salt), and **metallic bonds** (electrons shared across many atoms, which lets metals conduct electricity). Matter commonly appears as solid, liquid, or gas, depending on temperature and pressure.

Reactions either release energy (burning fuel, the body's digestion of food) or absorb it (photosynthesis in plants, smelting metal from ore). **Catalysts** speed up reactions without being used up; **enzymes** are the catalysts of living things.

**Biochemistry**, the chemistry of life, is built mostly on carbon, which can form long chains and rings. About 96% of the human body's mass is oxygen, carbon, hydrogen, and nitrogen. Life's key molecules are proteins, DNA and RNA, fats, and sugars, all working in water.

**How it developed:** Ancient metalworkers, potters, dyers, and brewers practiced chemistry long before it had theory. **Alchemy** (practiced in Egypt, China, India, the Islamic world, and Europe) sought to turn base metals into gold and to find elixirs of life; it failed in those goals but developed laboratory methods. Antoine Lavoisier (1780s) showed that mass is conserved in reactions and explained burning as combination with oxygen. John Dalton proposed modern atomic theory (1808). Dmitri Mendeleev's periodic table (1869) predicted undiscovered elements. The electron was discovered in 1897 and the atomic nucleus in 1911. Quantum mechanics explained chemical bonding in the 1920s–30s (Linus Pauling).

**Effects on human life and society:**
- **Food:** the Haber–Bosch process (1909–1913) makes ammonia from nitrogen in the air for fertilizer. Food grown with synthetic nitrogen fertilizer is estimated to sustain roughly half of the human population [Refining].
- **Materials:** steel, cement, glass, and plastics. Humans now produce over 400 million tonnes of plastic a year, much of which ends up as long-lived waste.
- **Medicine:** nearly all modern drugs are products of chemistry.
- **Harm:** chemical weapons were used on a large scale in World War I (chlorine gas, 1915), killing roughly 90,000 people in that war, and in later conflicts; most states have banned them under the Chemical Weapons Convention (1997). Leaded gasoline, used from the 1920s until finally phased out worldwide in 2021, raised lead levels in the blood of billions of people, damaging children's brains. Chemicals called CFCs thinned the protective ozone layer until they were banned under the Montreal Protocol (1987); the ozone layer is now slowly recovering.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** Chemistry's foundations are not seriously contested. Debate centers on regulation: how much testing new chemicals need before use, and who bears the burden of proof [Belief/Value]. The **precautionary principle** (restrict a substance if harm is plausible, even before proof) is favored more in the European Union than in the United States. "**Green chemistry**" seeks processes that use and produce fewer hazardous substances.

**Key works and figures:**
- Antoine Lavoisier, *Elementary Treatise of Chemistry* (1789): conservation of mass, modern naming.
- John Dalton (1808): atomic theory.
- Dmitri Mendeleev (1869): the periodic table.
- Marie Curie (1898 onward): discovered polonium and radium.
- Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch (1909–1913): industrial fertilizer. Haber also led Germany's poison gas program in World War I.
- Linus Pauling, *The Nature of the Chemical Bond* (1939).

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: atomic structure, the periodic table, bonding, conservation of mass in reactions.
- Debated: the long-term health effects of many industrial chemicals at low doses (for example some "forever chemicals," PFAS) [Refining]; how life's chemistry first arose (see 2.6) [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: how strictly to regulate chemicals; the trade-off between cheap materials and pollution.

**Open questions:** How life first emerged from non-living chemistry. How to design better batteries, catalysts, and materials, including superconductors (materials that carry electricity without resistance) that work at ordinary temperatures. How to recycle plastics and other materials at scale.

**See also:** 1.2 Genetics and Heredity; 2.1 Physics; 12.2 Agriculture and Food Systems; 12.4 Manufacturing and Materials; 16.2 Biodiversity and Environmental Degradation.

### 2.3 Astronomy and Cosmology

**In one sentence:** Astronomy studies objects beyond Earth, and cosmology studies the universe as a whole, which humans have measured to be about 13.8 billion years old and expanding.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- A **star** is a huge ball of gas that shines because of **nuclear fusion** in its core (light atoms merging into heavier ones and releasing energy). The **Sun** is Earth's star.
- A **planet** is a large body orbiting a star that has cleared its orbital path of other large objects. A **moon** orbits a planet.
- A **galaxy** is a gravitationally bound system of billions of stars, gas, and dust.
- A **light-year** is the distance light travels in a year: about 9.5 trillion km. Looking far away means looking back in time, because light takes time to arrive.

**How it works:**
*The solar system.* The Sun is a middle-aged, middle-sized star about 4.6 billion years old, about 150 million km from Earth (light takes about 8 minutes to cross that distance). Eight planets orbit it: four small rocky ones (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars) and four giants (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune), plus dwarf planets such as Pluto (reclassified from "planet" in 2006), asteroids, and comets. Earth's Moon is about 384,000 km away. Humans have sent robotic probes to every planet. Twelve humans walked on the Moon between 1969 and 1972; no human has gone farther.

*Stars and galaxies.* Earth's galaxy, the **Milky Way**, contains roughly 100–400 billion stars and is about 100,000 light-years across. The observable universe holds hundreds of billions of galaxies, possibly more. Stars form from collapsing gas clouds, fuse hydrogen into heavier elements for millions to trillions of years, and die. The heaviest stars explode as **supernovae**, scattering elements such as carbon, oxygen, and iron that later form planets and living bodies. **Black holes** are regions where gravity is so strong that not even light escapes; the first image of one was published in 2019.

*The Big Bang.* Distant galaxies are moving away from Earth, and the farther they are the faster they recede (Edwin Hubble, 1929). Running this backward points to a hot, dense early state about 13.8 billion years ago. This **Big Bang** model is supported by the faint **cosmic microwave background** radiation left over from the early universe (detected 1965) and by the observed proportions of light elements. It describes how the universe evolved from that early state; it does not say what, if anything, came "before."

*Dark matter and dark energy.* Galaxies spin and cluster as if they contain far more mass than can be seen. Humans call this unseen material **dark matter**. Since 1998 they have also measured that the universe's expansion is accelerating, attributed to an unknown **dark energy**. In the standard model of cosmology, ordinary matter is only about 5% of the universe's contents, dark matter about 27%, and dark energy about 68%. What these are is unknown.

*Exoplanets and life.* The first planet orbiting a Sun-like star was found in 1995. By the mid-2020s about 6,000 **exoplanets** had been confirmed, and estimates suggest most stars have planets. No life beyond Earth has been detected. The **Fermi paradox** asks why, if life is common, humans see no sign of it.

**How it developed:** Babylonian, Egyptian, Chinese, Indian, Maya, and Islamic astronomers kept careful sky records for thousands of years, often to make calendars and for astrology. **Astrology** (the belief that the positions of stars and planets influence human lives and personalities) is a [Belief/Value] practice; controlled tests have not found the effects it claims. Ptolemy's Earth-centered model (~150 CE) dominated for 1,400 years. Nicolaus Copernicus (1543) put the Sun at the center. Johannes Kepler (1609–1619) found the laws of planetary orbits. Galileo's telescope (1609–10) revealed Jupiter's moons; the Catholic Church tried him in 1633 and confined him to house arrest for supporting Copernicus. Newton explained orbits through gravity (1687). In the 1920s Hubble showed that other galaxies exist and that the universe expands. Space telescopes (Hubble, 1990; James Webb, 2021) extended the view.

**Effects on human life and society:** Astronomy gave humans calendars, navigation, and timekeeping. Shifting Earth from the center of the universe was a major cultural shock. Space technology now supports weather forecasting, communications, and navigation through more than 10,000 active satellites. Astronomy also shows a planetary risk: an asteroid impact about 66 million years ago helped end the age of dinosaurs, and in 2022 a spacecraft (DART) successfully nudged a small asteroid's orbit as a test of planetary defense.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- The standard cosmological model ("Lambda-CDM") is widely accepted, but a mismatch between ways of measuring the universe's expansion rate (the "Hubble tension") may signal missing physics [Refining].
- Alternatives to dark matter, such as modified gravity theories, are a minority view [Contested].
- Many religions have creation accounts; some believers see the Big Bang as consistent with creation, others reject it [Belief/Value].

**Key works and figures:**
- Nicolaus Copernicus, *On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres* (1543).
- Galileo Galilei, *Sidereus Nuncius* (1610): first telescopic discoveries.
- Johannes Kepler (1609–1619): laws of planetary motion.
- Edwin Hubble (1920s): galaxies and expansion.
- Henrietta Swan Leavitt (1912): found a way to measure cosmic distances.
- Vera Rubin (1970s): galaxy rotation evidence for dark matter.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Earth orbits the Sun; the universe is ~13.8 billion years old and expanding; stars make the heavier elements; exoplanets are common.
- Debated: the nature of dark matter and dark energy; the exact expansion rate; how the first galaxies formed so quickly [Refining].
- Matters of belief or value: whether the universe was created or has a purpose; whether humans should settle other planets.

**Open questions:** Is there life elsewhere, and is any of it intelligent? What are dark matter and dark energy? What, if anything, preceded the Big Bang? Will the universe expand forever?

**See also:** 2.1 Physics; 2.4 Earth Science; 4.4 The Medieval and Early Modern World; 10.1 What Religion Is and Why Humans Have It; 12.5 Transportation.

### 2.4 Earth Science

**In one sentence:** Earth science studies the planet itself, a 4.5-billion-year-old rocky world whose surface is broken into slowly moving plates that build mountains, open oceans, and cause earthquakes and volcanoes.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Geology** is the study of Earth's rocks, structure, and history. **Oceanography** studies the oceans; **seismology** studies earthquakes.
- A **mineral** is a naturally formed solid with a definite chemical makeup and crystal structure. A **rock** is a mixture of minerals.
- **Radiometric dating** measures the age of rocks using the steady decay of radioactive atoms inside them, like a natural clock.
- **Deep time:** Earth's history is so long that slow processes (a few centimeters a year) produce huge changes. Humans have existed for less than 0.01% of it.

**How it works:**
*Structure.* Earth is a slightly flattened sphere with a radius of about 6,371 km. It has layers: a thin rocky **crust** (about 5–10 km thick under oceans, 30–70 km under continents), a hot, slowly flowing rocky **mantle**, a liquid iron **outer core** (whose motion generates Earth's magnetic field, which shields the surface from some solar radiation), and a solid iron **inner core** about as hot as the Sun's surface.

*Plate tectonics.* The crust and upper mantle are broken into about 15 major **plates** that move a few centimeters per year, about as fast as fingernails grow. Where plates pull apart (mostly under oceans) new crust forms; where they collide, one may sink beneath the other, or mountains rise (the Himalayas formed as India pushed into Asia). Where they grind past each other, stress builds and is released as **earthquakes**. Most volcanoes and earthquakes occur along plate edges, such as the "Ring of Fire" around the Pacific Ocean. Over hundreds of millions of years, continents have merged into supercontinents and split apart again.

*The rock cycle.* **Igneous** rocks form from cooled molten rock; **sedimentary** rocks from layers of particles pressed together (these hold most fossils and all fossil fuels); **metamorphic** rocks from others transformed by heat and pressure. Erosion by water, wind, and ice wears mountains down.

*Oceans and water.* Oceans cover about 71% of Earth's surface, average about 3.7 km deep, and reach about 11 km at their deepest. About 97% of Earth's water is salty; most fresh water is locked in ice, and humans rely heavily on rivers, lakes, and **groundwater** (water stored in underground rock layers).

*Hazards.* Each year there are roughly 15–20 earthquakes of magnitude 7 or more. About 1,300–1,400 volcanoes are considered potentially active, and roughly 50–70 erupt in a typical year. A **tsunami** is a series of huge waves, usually set off by an undersea earthquake.

**How it developed:** Ancient Greek, Chinese, and Islamic scholars speculated about fossils and earthquakes; China's Zhang Heng built an earthquake detector in 132 CE. Many religious traditions taught a young Earth [Belief/Value]. James Hutton (1788) and Charles Lyell (1830–33) argued that today's slow processes, acting over vast time, shaped Earth. Radiometric dating (early 1900s) proved Earth's great age; Clair Patterson fixed it at about 4.55 billion years (1956). Alfred Wegener proposed **continental drift** in 1912, noting that continents fit together like puzzle pieces, but most geologists rejected it because he had no mechanism. Mapping of the seafloor in the 1950s–60s revealed spreading ridges and magnetic striping, and plate tectonics was accepted by the late 1960s, a classic example of science changing its mind on evidence (see 3.3).

**Effects on human life and society:**
- **Resources:** nearly all metals, building stone, fertilizer minerals, and fossil fuels come from geological deposits. Their uneven distribution shapes trade, wealth, and war (see 5.7, 12.3).
- **Disasters:** natural hazards kill on average tens of thousands of people a year, with huge variation; single events can kill far more. The 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami killed about 230,000 people; the 2010 Haiti earthquake roughly 100,000–300,000 [Contested; counts vary widely]; the 2023 Turkey–Syria earthquakes about 60,000. The 1815 Tambora eruption in Indonesia cooled the planet and caused crop failures in 1816, the "year without a summer." Deaths per disaster have fallen where building codes, warning systems, and wealth have improved; poorer places suffer far higher death tolls from similar events.
- **Groundwater depletion** is occurring in major farming regions, including parts of India, the Middle East, and the United States.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** The proposed **Anthropocene** ("age of humans") would mark a new geological time unit defined by human impact, such as nuclear-test fallout and plastics in sediments. A formal proposal was rejected by geologists' official body in 2024, but the term remains widely used informally [Contested]. **Young-Earth creationism** [Belief/Value] holds that Earth is only thousands of years old; this conflicts with all geological dating evidence.

**Key works and figures:**
- James Hutton, *Theory of the Earth* (1788): deep time.
- Charles Lyell, *Principles of Geology* (1830–33): influenced Darwin.
- Alfred Wegener, *The Origin of Continents and Oceans* (1915): continental drift.
- Marie Tharp and Bruce Heezen (1950s): mapped the ocean floor.
- Harry Hess (1962): seafloor spreading.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Earth's age and layered structure; plate tectonics; the rock cycle; the causes of earthquakes and volcanoes.
- Debated: when plate tectonics began on Earth [Contested]; where Earth's water came from [Refining]; how to forecast eruptions reliably [Refining].
- Matters of belief or value: how much risk to accept in building near faults and volcanoes; mining versus conservation.

**Open questions:** Earthquakes cannot be predicted to the day or week; humans can only estimate probabilities over years. The detailed workings of the deep mantle and core. Why Earth has plate tectonics when its neighbors do not.

**See also:** 2.3 Astronomy and Cosmology; 2.5 Climate and Atmosphere; 12.3 Energy; 16.7 Resources: Food, Water, and Materials; 8.6 Cities and Settlement.

### 2.5 Climate and Atmosphere

**In one sentence:** Earth's climate is set by the balance of energy arriving from the Sun and leaving as heat, and humans have measurably shifted that balance by adding carbon dioxide and other gases to the atmosphere, warming the planet by about 1.3 °C since the late 1800s.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- The **atmosphere** is the layer of gas around Earth. By volume it is about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, about 1% argon, and small amounts of other gases, including about 0.04% **carbon dioxide** (CO₂) and water vapor that varies from place to place.
- **Weather** is the state of the atmosphere at a given time and place (today's rain). **Climate** is the long-term pattern of weather, conventionally averaged over 30 years. An analogy: weather is one roll of the dice; climate is how the dice are weighted. (Imperfect, because climate also has slow cycles of its own.)
- **Temperatures** here are in degrees Celsius (°C): water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C at sea level.
- **Fossil fuels** (coal, oil, natural gas) are the remains of ancient organisms, buried for millions of years. Burning them releases energy and CO₂.

**How it works:**

*The greenhouse effect.* Sunlight passes through the atmosphere and warms the surface. The warm surface sends energy back out as infrared (heat) radiation. Some gases, mainly water vapor, CO₂, methane, and nitrous oxide, absorb and re-emit that infrared radiation, so the lower atmosphere and surface stay warmer than they otherwise would. Without this natural effect Earth's average surface temperature would be about −18 °C instead of about 15 °C. Adding more of these **greenhouse gases** strengthens the effect. (The name comes from a loose comparison with glass greenhouses, which actually work mostly by blocking air movement.)

*The carbon cycle.* Carbon moves constantly among the air, oceans, plants, soils, and rocks. Before industry, natural flows were roughly balanced, and CO₂ stood at about 280 parts per million (ppm). Humans now release roughly 40 billion tonnes of CO₂ a year, mostly from burning fossil fuels and also from cutting forests and making cement. About half of this is absorbed by oceans and land plants; the rest accumulates in the air. CO₂ has risen to about 420–425 ppm, higher than at any time in at least 800,000 years (known from air bubbles trapped in ice cores) and probably in millions of years. CO₂ persists, so warming from emissions lasts for centuries.

*Feedbacks.* Warming causes changes that amplify or dampen it. Warmer air holds more water vapor (amplifying). Melting ice exposes darker land and water that absorb more sunlight (amplifying). Clouds can do either [Refining]. The **climate sensitivity** (the long-term warming from doubling CO₂) is likely between about 2.5 and 4 °C, with a best estimate of about 3 °C [Refining].

*Observed changes.* Compared with the late 1800s:
- Global average surface temperature is up about 1.3 °C (2015–2024 average roughly 1.2–1.3 °C; 2024 was the first calendar year about 1.5 °C above that baseline). Land warms faster than oceans, and the Arctic about three to four times faster than the global average.
- Sea level has risen about 20–25 cm since 1900, and the rate has roughly doubled since the 1990s to about 4 mm a year, from melting ice and expanding warmer water.
- Oceans have become about 30% more acidic (pH down about 0.1) by absorbing CO₂, harming corals and shellfish.
- Glaciers and Arctic sea ice are shrinking; heatwaves and heavy rainfall have become more frequent and intense in most regions.

*Past climates.* Earth's climate has changed greatly without humans, driven by shifts in its orbit (**Milankovitch cycles**), volcanic activity, continental drift, and natural changes in CO₂. For the last ~2.6 million years Earth has alternated between **ice ages** (glacial periods) and warmer **interglacials**, roughly every 100,000 years recently. At the peak of the last ice age, about 20,000 years ago, ice sheets kilometers thick covered much of North America and northern Europe, sea level was about 120 m lower, and global temperature was roughly 5–6 °C colder. Human farming and civilization arose during the unusually stable warm period since about 11,700 years ago. The current warming is far faster than typical natural changes and is explained by human emissions, not by the Sun or orbital cycles.

**How it developed:** Joseph Fourier (1820s) reasoned that the atmosphere traps heat. Eunice Foote (1856) and John Tyndall (1859) showed CO₂ and water vapor absorb heat. Svante Arrhenius (1896) calculated that doubling CO₂ would warm Earth by several degrees. Charles David Keeling began continuous CO₂ measurements in Hawaii in 1958, showing a steady rise (the "Keeling curve"). Computer climate models developed from the 1960s (Syukuro Manabe, Nobel Prize 2021). The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), a UN body that assesses climate research, was founded in 1988; its sixth assessment (2021) called human-caused warming "unequivocal." Internal documents show that scientists at some major fossil-fuel companies projected warming accurately in the 1970s–80s, while those companies and allied groups publicly emphasized doubt for years afterward.

**Effects on human life and society:** Climate shapes where humans can farm and live; most people live in a fairly narrow band of average temperatures. Documented and projected impacts include more deadly heatwaves, droughts and floods, reduced yields of some crops in hot regions, coastal flooding (roughly 700 million to 1 billion people live in low-lying coastal zones), shifts in disease ranges, and damage to coral reefs. The IPCC estimates about 3.3–3.6 billion people live in places highly vulnerable to climate change. Harms fall most on poor countries and people, who emitted least. The richest ~10% of humans are responsible for a large share (roughly half by some estimates) of emissions [Refining]. Under current policies, warming by 2100 is projected at roughly 2.5–3 °C [Refining]. In the 2015 Paris Agreement, nearly all countries agreed to aim to hold warming "well below 2 °C," pursuing 1.5 °C. How to cut emissions (see 12.3, 16.1) is one of the largest economic and political questions humans face.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Scientific mainstream:** warming is real, mostly human-caused, and harmful at the scale projected. Major scientific bodies worldwide agree.
- **Climate change denial:** claims that warming is not happening or not human-caused are not supported by the evidence. More common today is debate over *how much* action, how fast, and at what cost.
- **Policy approaches** [Contested; partly Belief/Value]: carbon pricing (taxing emissions), regulation and subsidies for clean energy, nuclear power's role, **geoengineering** (deliberately cooling the planet, for example by reflecting sunlight), and **degrowth** (deliberately shrinking rich economies). How to share costs between rich and poor countries is a core political dispute.

**Key works and figures:**
- John Tyndall (1859) and Eunice Foote (1856): heat absorption by gases.
- Svante Arrhenius (1896): first estimate of CO₂ warming.
- Charles David Keeling (1958 onward): the CO₂ record.
- Syukuro Manabe and James Hansen: climate models; Hansen's 1988 US Senate testimony brought warming to public attention.
- Milutin Milanković (1920s–40s): orbital cycles behind ice ages.
- IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (2021–2023): the current scientific consensus summary.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: the greenhouse effect; rising CO₂ from human activity; ~1.3 °C of warming; sea level rise; ocean acidification; human activity as the dominant cause since the mid-1900s.
- Debated: exact climate sensitivity [Refining]; regional details of rainfall change [Refining]; how close **tipping points** (thresholds beyond which changes, like the collapse of ice sheets or major ocean currents, become self-sustaining) are [Contested]; the economic cost of warming [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: how to weigh the welfare of future generations against present costs; who should pay; whether economic growth and emissions cuts are compatible.

**Open questions:** How fast large ice sheets in Greenland and Antarctica will melt, which sets long-term sea level rise (meters over centuries are possible). How clouds will respond. Whether major ocean currents in the Atlantic will weaken sharply. Whether humans will cut emissions fast enough to stay near their stated targets.

### 2.6 Life on Earth: Biology and Ecology

**In one sentence:** Earth is covered with life, millions of species descended from single cells that appeared over 3.5 billion years ago, linked into ecosystems on which humans depend for food, air, and water, and which humans are now reshaping and depleting.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Life**, as humans define it on Earth, is matter organized into cells that take in energy, grow, respond to their surroundings, reproduce, and evolve. All known life uses DNA (see 1.2) and water.
- **Biology** is the study of living things. **Ecology** is the study of how organisms interact with one another and with their environment.
- An **ecosystem** is a community of organisms plus its physical setting (a forest, a lake, a coral reef).
- A **food web** maps who eats whom. Energy enters mostly through **photosynthesis**: plants, algae, and some bacteria use sunlight to turn CO₂ and water into sugar, releasing oxygen.
- **Biodiversity** is the variety of life: of genes, species, and ecosystems.
- Humans name species with a two-part Latin name (genus and species, e.g. *Homo sapiens*), a system set out by Carl Linnaeus in 1735 and 1758.

**How it works:**
*The tree of life.* All known life shares a common ancestor and falls into three broad **domains**: **bacteria** and **archaea** (single cells without a nucleus) and **eukaryotes** (cells with a nucleus, including all plants, animals, and fungi). Most of life's diversity, and most of its history, is microbial.

*A rough timeline* (years ago):

| Time | Event |
|---|---|
| ~4.5 billion | Earth forms |
| ~3.5–4 billion | First life (single cells) [Refining on exact date] |
| ~2.4 billion | Photosynthesizing microbes fill the air with oxygen |
| ~2 billion | First eukaryotic cells |
| ~540 million | "Cambrian explosion": most major animal groups appear |
| ~470 million | Plants colonize land |
| ~230–66 million | Age of dinosaurs |
| ~66 million | Asteroid impact; mass extinction; mammals diversify |
| ~300,000 | *Homo sapiens* (see 1.1) |

*How many species.* Humans have formally described about 2 million species, more than half of them insects. Estimates of the total range from about 8–10 million (for plants, animals, and fungi) to far more if microbes are counted [Contested]. Some 99% of all species that ever lived are extinct. There have been five **mass extinctions**, events in which a large share of species died out in a geologically short time.

*Who weighs what.* By mass, plants make up about 80% of Earth's living matter and bacteria about 15%; all animals together are well under 1%. Among mammals, humans make up about a third of total mass and their livestock (cattle, pigs, and others) about 60%; wild mammals are only about 4%. Farmed chickens outweigh all wild birds. Humans slaughter roughly 80 billion land animals a year for food.

*How ecosystems work.* Ecosystems recycle nutrients (decomposers such as fungi and bacteria break down dead matter) and pass energy up food chains, losing most of it at each step, which is why there are far fewer predators than prey. Removing one species can cascade: when wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park in the United States (1995), the effects on deer and vegetation were widely studied [Refining on size]. Humans call the benefits they get from ecosystems **ecosystem services**: pollination, clean water, fertile soil, fisheries, and climate regulation. Most of the world's crop types benefit from animal pollinators, though the staple grains (wheat, rice, maize) are wind-pollinated or self-pollinating.

**How it developed:** Aristotle classified animals (~350 BCE). Microscopes (1600s) revealed cells and microbes; **cell theory** (all living things are made of cells, and all cells come from cells) was established by the 1850s. Darwin and Wallace's theory of evolution (1859) explained why species exist and are related. Ernst Haeckel coined "ecology" (1866). Arthur Tansley introduced the term "ecosystem" (1935). In 1977 Carl Woese used genetics to identify archaea, redrawing the tree of life. Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* (1962) documented the effects of pesticides on wildlife and helped launch the modern environmental movement.

**Effects on human life and society:** Humans depend on a small number of domesticated species: three grasses (rice, wheat, and maize) supply roughly half of human food energy (see 12.2). Humans now use roughly a third of Earth's ice-free land for farming and grazing and have transformed most of the rest. Monitored wild vertebrate populations (mammals, birds, fish, reptiles, amphibians) have declined by about 70% on average since 1970, according to an index compiled by the conservation group WWF; this is an average of population trends, not a count of animals lost, and the method is debated [Refining]. A 2019 UN-backed assessment (IPBES) estimated that about 1 million species are threatened with extinction, and current extinction rates are tens to hundreds of times higher than the natural background rate [Refining]. Main drivers are habitat loss from farming, overfishing and hunting, climate change, pollution, and species moved to new places by humans (**invasive species**). Many scientists describe this as the beginning of a sixth mass extinction [Contested; losses so far are far smaller than in past mass extinctions, but the rate is high].

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Conservation** approaches range from protecting land in parks (about 17% of land is now in protected areas) to "sustainable use" by local communities. The international "30 by 30" target (2022) aims to protect 30% of land and sea by 2030.
- **Anthropocentric vs. ecocentric ethics** [Belief/Value]: whether nature matters mainly because it serves humans, or has value of its own.
- **Animal welfare and rights** [Belief/Value]: whether, and how much, animal suffering matters morally, which bears on farming, hunting, and research. The scientific question of which animals can feel pain is [Refining].

**Key works and figures:**
- Carl Linnaeus, *Systema Naturae* (1735; 1758 edition): naming and classifying species.
- Charles Darwin, *On the Origin of Species* (1859): evolution and common descent.
- Alexander von Humboldt (early 1800s): pioneered the study of how life is distributed across climates.
- Carl Woese (1977): discovered archaea; three domains of life.
- Rachel Carson, *Silent Spring* (1962): pesticides and ecosystems.
- E.O. Wilson (1980s–2000s): popularized the concept of biodiversity.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: common descent; the cell as the unit of life; photosynthesis as the base of most food webs; large human-caused declines in wild populations and habitats.
- Debated: total number of species [Contested]; exact current extinction rates [Refining]; how much biodiversity loss ecosystems can absorb before key services fail [Contested]; whether viruses count as alive (a matter of definition).
- Matters of belief or value: the moral status of animals and ecosystems; how to trade land for food against land for wildlife.

**Open questions:** How life began from non-living chemistry. How many species exist, especially of microbes. Whether life exists elsewhere (see 2.3). How resilient ecosystems will be to combined pressures of climate change and habitat loss.
