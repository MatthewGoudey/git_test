# Domain 1: The Human Organism

Humans are animals. They are made of cells, they need food, water, oxygen, and sleep, they reproduce sexually, they get sick, they age, and they die. This domain describes the human body as a biological object: where it came from, how its instructions are stored and inherited, how it works, how it makes new humans, and how it fails. Everything else in this guide (economics, politics, religion, art) is built by creatures with these bodies and these limits.

*Figures are approximate and describe the mid-2020s unless a date is given.*

### 1.1 Evolution and Human Origins

**In one sentence:** Humans are one branch of a family tree that includes every living thing on Earth, and they arose from earlier ape-like species in Africa through a slow natural process called evolution.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- A **species** is a group of organisms that can breed with one another and produce fertile offspring. Humans belong to the species *Homo sapiens* (Latin for "wise human"; the naming system is explained in 2.6).
- **Evolution** is change in the inherited traits of a population over many generations.
- **Natural selection** is the main mechanism that shapes evolution. Individuals vary. Some variations are inherited. Individuals whose inherited traits help them survive and reproduce in their environment leave more offspring, so those traits become more common over time. No one designs or directs this process.
- A **primate** is a member of the group of mammals that includes lemurs, monkeys, and apes. Humans are apes. Their closest living relatives are chimpanzees and bonobos.
- A **hominin** is any species on the human side of the split from the chimpanzee line.
- A **fossil** is the preserved remains or trace of an organism, usually turned to stone over long periods. **Ancient DNA** is genetic material recovered from old bones (see 1.2).

**How it works:**
Humans and chimpanzees shared a common ancestor roughly 6–7 million years ago [Refining; estimates range from about 5 to 8 million]. The two species' DNA is about 98–99% identical by the most common measure. Since that split, at least 15–20 hominin species are known from fossils. Many lived at the same time. The human line was not a single ladder but a bushy tree with many dead-end branches.

The rough sequence, with dates as approximate ranges:

| Time (years ago) | Development |
|---|---|
| ~4–2 million | *Australopithecus* species walk upright on two legs but have chimpanzee-sized brains. The famous "Lucy" skeleton is ~3.2 million years old. |
| ~3.3 million | Oldest known stone tools (East Africa). |
| ~2 million | Early *Homo* species; larger brains; *Homo erectus* spreads from Africa into Asia. |
| ~1 million to ~400,000 | Control of fire becomes regular [Refining; the earliest dates are disputed]. Cooking makes food easier to digest. |
| ~300,000 | Oldest known *Homo sapiens* fossils (Jebel Irhoud, Morocco). |
| ~70,000–50,000 | A major dispersal of *Homo sapiens* out of Africa into Asia, then Europe [Refining]. |
| ~65,000–45,000 | Humans reach Australia, which required sea crossings [Contested on exact date]. |
| ~40,000 | Neanderthals (a stocky hominin of Europe and western Asia) disappear. |
| ~25,000–15,000 | Humans reach the Americas [Contested; the earliest proposed dates are debated]. |

When *Homo sapiens* spread, it met other hominins and sometimes had children with them. Most humans whose ancestry is outside Africa carry about 1–2% Neanderthal DNA. Some populations in Oceania (the Pacific islands and New Guinea) carry several percent DNA from **Denisovans**, a group known mostly from DNA in a finger bone and teeth found in Siberia and Tibet. Small hominins such as *Homo floresiensis* (Indonesia) survived until roughly 50,000 years ago. Since then, *Homo sapiens* has been the only hominin species.

Features that set humans apart include walking upright, a very large brain for body size (about three times the size of a chimpanzee's), long childhoods, fine hand control, complex language, and the ability to accumulate culture across generations.

**How it developed:** Many human traditions told stories of how humans were created (see 10.1). In 1859 Charles Darwin published *On the Origin of Species*, arguing for evolution by natural selection; Alfred Russel Wallace reached the same idea independently. Darwin applied it to humans in *The Descent of Man* (1871). The first Neanderthal fossil was recognized in 1856. In the 1930s–40s the "modern synthesis" joined Darwin's theory with genetics. From the 1980s, DNA studies showed that all living humans descend from African populations. From 2010, sequencing of Neanderthal and Denisovan genomes (led by Svante Pääbo, who won a Nobel Prize in 2022) revealed interbreeding.

**Effects on human life and society:** Evolution explains human bodies and many human weaknesses: back pain from upright walking, difficult childbirth (see 1.5), and cravings for sugar and fat that once helped survival but now contribute to disease (see 1.3). It is the foundation of modern biology and medicine, including the understanding of how bacteria evolve resistance to drugs (see 1.6). It has also been misused. "Social Darwinism" (late 1800s) claimed that the poor or colonized were "less fit," and helped justify colonialism and **eugenics** (see 1.2). These are political claims, not conclusions of evolutionary biology.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Evolutionary biology** (scientific consensus): all life shares common ancestry; humans evolved through natural processes.
- **Out of Africa** vs. **multiregional** models of human origins: the debate is now largely settled in favor of a mostly African origin, with some interbreeding with other hominins.
- **Creationism** [Belief/Value]: some religious humans believe that God created humans directly, sometimes within the last ~10,000 years. In some surveys about 40% of United States adults have agreed with this view. Many religious groups accept evolution and see no conflict with their faith (see 10.7).

**Key works and figures:**
- *On the Origin of Species* (Charles Darwin, 1859): set out natural selection.
- Alfred Russel Wallace: co-discoverer of natural selection.
- *The Descent of Man* (Darwin, 1871): applied evolution to humans.
- Mary and Louis Leakey, Richard Leakey: fossil discoveries in East Africa, mid-1900s.
- Svante Pääbo: sequenced Neanderthal DNA, 2010.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: humans evolved; they share ancestry with all life; *Homo sapiens* arose in Africa; Neanderthals and Denisovans interbred with *Homo sapiens*.
- Debated: exact dates and routes of migration; why Neanderthals disappeared (competition, climate, absorption, or a mix); what drove the growth of the human brain; when language began.
- Matters of belief or value: whether evolution is compatible with religious creation accounts; whether evolution implies anything about human purpose or meaning.

**Open questions:** When and how fully modern language and symbolic thought appeared. Why *Homo sapiens* alone survived. How many more hominin species remain undiscovered.

**See also:** 1.2 Genetics and Heredity; 2.6 Life on Earth: Biology and Ecology; 4.1 Prehistory; 10.1 What Religion Is and Why Humans Have It; 8.3 Race, Ethnicity, and Identity.

### 1.2 Genetics and Heredity

**In one sentence:** Every human cell carries a long chemical instruction set called DNA, inherited half from each parent, which guides how the body builds and runs itself.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- A **cell** is the smallest unit of life: a tiny bag of chemicals that can take in energy and copy itself. A human body has roughly 30–40 trillion of them.
- **DNA** (deoxyribonucleic acid) is a long molecule shaped like a twisted ladder (a "double helix"). Its rungs are made of four chemical "letters," called bases and abbreviated A, C, G, and T. The order of these letters encodes information, much as the order of letters in a text does.
- A **gene** is a stretch of DNA that usually holds the instructions for making a **protein**. Proteins are molecules that do most of the body's work: building tissue, speeding up chemical reactions, and carrying signals.
- The **genome** is the complete set of an organism's DNA. In humans it is packaged into 23 pairs of **chromosomes** (bundles of DNA), one set from each parent.
- A **mutation** is a change in the DNA sequence, from copying errors or damage.

**How it works:**
The human genome is about 3.1 billion letters long. Only about 1–2% of it directly codes for proteins, in roughly 20,000 genes. The rest includes switches that turn genes on and off, structural DNA, and long stretches with no known function [Refining]. Every cell has the same genome, but different cells switch on different genes; that is why a liver cell differs from a nerve cell.

Inheritance follows clear rules for single-gene traits, first described by Gregor Mendel in 1866. Each person has two copies of most genes. A child receives one copy at random from each parent. Some disorders appear when one faulty copy is present (for example Huntington's disease); others only when both copies are faulty (for example cystic fibrosis and sickle-cell disease). Most traits, such as height, are **polygenic**: shaped by thousands of genes, each with a tiny effect, plus the environment. **Heritability** is a statistic estimating how much of the variation in a trait within a population comes from genetic differences. It is often misread as "how genetic a trait is"; it is not, and it can change when environments change.

Any two unrelated humans share about 99.9% of their DNA. Humans have less genetic diversity than chimpanzees, which suggests their population was small at some point in the past. Most human genetic variation (roughly 85%) is found *within* any given population, not between populations, and the most variation is found within Africa. This is one reason geneticists say human "races" are not sharp biological categories (see 8.3).

**How it developed:** Mendel's pea-plant work (1866) was ignored until 1900. In 1944 experiments showed DNA carries hereditary information. In 1953 James Watson and Francis Crick described the double helix, using X-ray images made by Rosalind Franklin. The genetic code was worked out in the 1960s. Scientists learned to cut and join DNA (genetic engineering) in the 1970s. The Human Genome Project produced a near-complete human sequence in 2003 at a cost of about US$3 billion; a fully complete sequence followed in 2022. Sequencing a genome now costs roughly a few hundred US dollars. In 2012 Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier showed that CRISPR, a bacterial defense system, could edit DNA precisely (Nobel Prize 2020).

**Effects on human life and society:**
- **Medicine:** genetic tests for thousands of inherited conditions; prenatal screening; cancer treatments targeted at tumor mutations. The first CRISPR-based treatment, for sickle-cell disease, was approved in 2023.
- **Food:** genetically modified crops are grown on very large areas (on the order of 200 million hectares), mostly soybeans, maize, cotton, and canola.
- **Law:** DNA evidence is used in criminal cases and has freed hundreds of wrongly convicted people in the United States alone.
- **Ancestry testing:** tens of millions of people have sent saliva samples to commercial companies, raising privacy concerns.
- **Eugenics:** in the early 1900s, movements to "improve" human heredity led to forced sterilization laws in the United States (tens of thousands sterilized), Sweden, and elsewhere. Nazi Germany sterilized roughly 400,000 people and murdered tens of thousands of disabled people, a program linked to the Holocaust (see 9.7).
- In 2018 a Chinese scientist, He Jiankui, announced the birth of twins whose embryos he had gene-edited. This was widely condemned and he was imprisoned.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Genetic determinism** (the view that genes fix destiny) is rejected by most scientists. Genes and environment interact.
- The **nature vs. nurture** debate (how much behavior comes from genes vs. upbringing) continues, though most researchers now say "both, interacting" (see 15.4).
- **Epigenetics** studies chemical marks on DNA that change gene activity without changing the sequence [Refining; claims that these marks pass across generations in humans are largely unproven].

**Key works and figures:**
- Gregor Mendel, *Experiments on Plant Hybridization* (1866): rules of inheritance.
- Rosalind Franklin, James Watson, Francis Crick (1953): DNA structure.
- The Human Genome Project (1990–2003): first human genome sequence.
- Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier (2012): CRISPR gene editing.
- Richard Lewontin (1972): showed most human genetic variation is within groups.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: DNA is the hereditary material; Mendelian inheritance for single genes; humans are genetically very similar to one another.
- Debated: how much of the variation in complex traits (intelligence, personality, disease risk) is genetic [Contested]; how much "non-coding" DNA has a function; whether polygenic scores (genetic risk predictions) are useful in practice.
- Matters of belief or value: whether humans should edit embryos; whether people should be able to select embryos for traits; who owns genetic data.

**Open questions:** How genes interact to produce complex traits. How to safely correct disease genes in living people. What most of the genome does.

**See also:** 1.1 Evolution and Human Origins; 1.6 Health, Disease, and Medicine; 8.3 Race, Ethnicity, and Identity; 12.2 Agriculture and Food Systems; 15.4 Personality and Development.

### 1.3 The Body and Its Needs

**In one sentence:** The human body is a system of cooperating organs that must be constantly supplied with oxygen, water, food, warmth, and sleep, and that fails within minutes to weeks if any of these run out.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Tissue** is a group of similar cells (for example muscle). An **organ** is a structure made of tissues that does a job (for example the heart). An **organ system** is a set of organs working together.
- **Metabolism** is the total of the chemical reactions that keep the body alive, especially those that release energy from food.
- Humans measure food energy in **kilocalories** (often written "Calories"). One kilocalorie raises a liter of water by about 1 °C.
- A **nutrient** is a substance from food the body needs. **Macronutrients** (carbohydrates, fats, proteins) supply energy and building material. **Micronutrients** (vitamins and minerals) are needed in small amounts.

**How it works:**
An adult human typically weighs 50–100 kg, is roughly 150–190 cm tall, and is about 60% water by weight. Its core temperature is held near 37 °C. The body also hosts roughly as many bacterial cells as human cells, mostly in the gut, which help with digestion and immunity [Refining].

| System | Main organs | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Circulatory | Heart, blood vessels | Pumps ~5 liters of blood around the body roughly once a minute; the heart beats ~100,000 times a day |
| Respiratory | Lungs | Takes in oxygen, removes carbon dioxide |
| Digestive | Mouth, stomach, intestines, liver | Breaks food into usable molecules |
| Nervous | Brain, spinal cord, nerves | Sensing, control, thought (see 1.4) |
| Endocrine | Glands (e.g. thyroid, pancreas) | Sends chemical signals called **hormones** through the blood |
| Immune | White blood cells, lymph nodes | Defends against infection (see 1.6) |
| Musculoskeletal | ~206 bones, ~600 muscles | Support and movement |
| Urinary | Kidneys, bladder | Filters waste from the blood |
| Skin | Skin, hair, sweat glands | Barrier; temperature control |
| Reproductive | See 1.5 | Making new humans |

**Needs and limits** (rough, varying with conditions):
- **Oxygen:** brain damage begins after a few minutes without it.
- **Water:** death usually follows within about 3–7 days without water, faster in heat.
- **Food:** a person can survive several weeks, sometimes up to two months, without food.
- **Heat:** below a core temperature of about 35 °C (hypothermia) or above about 40 °C (heatstroke), the body begins to fail.
- **Energy:** a typical adult needs roughly 1,800–3,000 kcal per day, depending on size and activity. The brain alone uses about 20% of resting energy.
- **Sleep:** adults generally need about 7–9 hours per night. Humans spend roughly a third of their lives asleep. Long-term shortage is linked to accidents, poorer health, and impaired thinking.

**Senses:** humans see light in a narrow band of wavelengths (about 380–700 nanometers, which they call the visible spectrum) and have three types of color receptors. They hear sound between roughly 20 and 20,000 vibrations per second, a range that shrinks with age. They also smell, taste, and feel touch, temperature, and pain, and they sense balance and the position of their own limbs. Vision dominates; a large part of the brain is devoted to it.

**Physical abilities:** humans are weak and slow compared to many animals of their size, but they are unusually good at long-distance running and at shedding heat through sweat. They have very precise hands.

**How it developed:** Ancient Egyptian, Greek, Indian, Chinese, and Islamic physicians described anatomy and theorized about it. Andreas Vesalius's *On the Fabric of the Human Body* (1543) corrected many errors through dissection. William Harvey showed that blood circulates (1628). Vitamins were identified in the early 1900s, explaining deficiency diseases such as scurvy (lack of vitamin C), rickets (vitamin D), and beriberi (vitamin B1).

**Effects on human life and society:** Securing food, water, and shelter is the oldest driver of human economic life (see 5.1, 12.2). Today humans face problems of both too little and too much food. Roughly 700 million people are chronically undernourished, and about 150 million children under five are stunted (too short for their age due to poor nutrition). At the same time about 2.5 billion adults are overweight, and over 1 billion people of all ages have obesity (a level of body fat linked to disease), contributing to diabetes and heart disease (see 1.6). Average height has risen by about 10 cm or more in many countries since the 1800s because of better nutrition and health.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** Nutrition science is a field with real disagreement. The basic needs (energy, protein, vitamins) are established, but the best balance of fats, carbohydrates, and specific foods for long-term health is [Contested], partly because long-term diet experiments on humans are hard to run. Diets based on ethics or religion (vegetarianism, kosher and halal rules, fasting) are [Belief/Value] choices with measurable health effects.

**Key works and figures:**
- Galen (~2nd century): Greek-Roman physician whose anatomy dominated for 1,300 years.
- Ibn Sina (Avicenna), *The Canon of Medicine* (~1025): standard text in the Islamic world and Europe for centuries.
- Andreas Vesalius, *On the Fabric of the Human Body* (1543): modern anatomy.
- William Harvey (1628): blood circulation.
- *Gray's Anatomy* (1858): standard anatomy reference, still updated.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: the structure and functions of the organ systems; basic nutrient requirements; that both undernutrition and obesity cause disease.
- Debated: optimal diet composition; the health role of gut bacteria; why humans need sleep at a cellular level [Refining].
- Matters of belief or value: what humans should eat (for ethical, religious, or environmental reasons); ideals of body shape.

**Open questions:** The exact function of sleep. How the gut microbiome affects health and mood. Why obesity has risen so fast, and the best ways to reverse it.

**See also:** 1.4 The Brain and Nervous System; 1.6 Health, Disease, and Medicine; 12.2 Agriculture and Food Systems; 14.7 Food and Everyday Customs; 16.7 Resources: Food, Water, and Materials.

### 1.4 The Brain and Nervous System

**In one sentence:** The brain is a 1.4-kilogram organ of about 86 billion signaling cells that produces everything humans sense, think, feel, remember, and do, though how it produces conscious experience is still unexplained.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- A **neuron** is a nerve cell specialized to send signals. It receives inputs through branches, and when stimulated enough it "fires" an electrical pulse down a long fiber called an **axon**.
- A **synapse** is the tiny gap where one neuron passes a signal to another, usually by releasing chemicals called **neurotransmitters** (for example dopamine, serotonin, glutamate).
- The **nervous system** has two parts: the **central nervous system** (brain and spinal cord) and the **peripheral nervous system** (nerves running to the rest of the body).
- **Consciousness** is the state of having subjective experience: there is "something it is like" to see red or feel pain. Humans can describe it but cannot yet explain it.

**How it works:**
The adult human brain weighs about 1.3–1.4 kg, about 2% of body weight, yet uses about 20% of the body's resting energy (roughly 20 watts, the power of a dim light bulb). It contains about 86 billion neurons and a similar number of support cells, joined by perhaps 100 trillion or more synapses.

Major regions, simplified:
- **Cerebral cortex:** the folded outer layer, very large in humans. Different areas specialize in vision, hearing, movement, language, planning, and more.
- **Prefrontal cortex** (at the front): planning, self-control, and decision-making. It matures last, into the mid-20s.
- **Limbic structures** (deep inside), such as the **amygdala** (threat and emotion) and **hippocampus** (forming new memories).
- **Cerebellum** (at the back): coordination and fine movement.
- **Brainstem:** breathing, heart rate, sleep and wakefulness.

Functions are distributed: most tasks involve networks across many regions rather than a single "center." **Neuroplasticity** is the brain's ability to change its connections with experience. It is strongest in childhood but continues for life, and it underlies learning and recovery after injury.

**Drugs** act on the brain mainly by mimicking, blocking, or changing neurotransmitters. Widely used ones include caffeine (most humans consume it daily), alcohol, nicotine (in tobacco), cannabis, and opioids (painkillers derived from or similar to opium). Tobacco kills roughly 7–8 million people a year and alcohol roughly 2.5–3 million, mostly through disease in other organs (see 1.6). Many drugs trigger the brain's reward system, which can lead to **addiction**: compulsive use despite harm (see 15.5).

**How it developed:** Ancient Egyptians thought the heart was the seat of thought; Greek physicians such as Galen argued for the brain. In 1848 an American railway worker, Phineas Gage, survived an iron rod through his frontal lobe and his personality changed, an early clue that brain regions have functions. Paul Broca (1861) located a speech area. Santiago Ramón y Cajal showed around 1890 that the brain is made of separate neurons. The electrical basis of nerve signals was described in detail by 1952. Recording brain waves (EEG, 1924) and imaging brain activity (fMRI, 1990s) let researchers watch living brains. Since the 2000s, implanted devices have let some paralyzed people control computer cursors with their thoughts.

**Effects on human life and society:** Neurological conditions (stroke, dementia, migraine, epilepsy, and others) are among the leading causes of illness and disability; in the early 2020s an estimated 3 billion people lived with one. About 55–60 million people live with **dementia** (loss of memory and thinking ability, most often from Alzheimer's disease), a number rising as populations age. Brain science influences education, law (for example, debates about how responsible teenagers are for crimes), and medicine. Drug policy, from legal alcohol to banned heroin, is a major area of politics and crime (see 7.3).

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Physicalism** (the dominant view among neuroscientists): the mind is what the brain does; no separate non-physical substance is needed [Contested among philosophers].
- **Dualism:** the mind or soul is distinct from the body. Many religions hold that humans have a soul that survives death [Belief/Value] (see 10.1, 11.2).
- Scientific theories of consciousness, such as **global workspace theory** (consciousness arises when information is broadcast widely across the brain) and **integrated information theory** (consciousness is a property of systems that integrate information), are all [Contested]; none is accepted.
- The "**hard problem**" (philosopher David Chalmers, 1995): even a complete map of brain activity may not explain why it is accompanied by experience.

**Key works and figures:**
- Santiago Ramón y Cajal: showed the brain is built from individual neurons (Nobel Prize 1906).
- Paul Broca (1861) and Carl Wernicke (1874): linked language to specific brain areas.
- Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley (1952): explained how neurons fire electrically.
- The patient "H.M." (studied 1957–2008): showed the hippocampus is needed to form new memories.
- David Chalmers, "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" (1995): framed the hard problem.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: the brain produces behavior and mental life; brain damage changes mind and personality; neurons communicate electrically and chemically; the brain remains plastic through life.
- Debated: how memories are stored in detail [Refining]; the causes of most psychiatric disorders [Refining]; how consciousness arises [Contested]; whether free will exists in any strong sense [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: whether humans have a soul; whether animals or machines can be conscious and what that would mean morally.

**Open questions:** How subjective experience arises. How to prevent or cure Alzheimer's disease. How the brain's networks produce reasoning and language. Whether other animals or artificial systems are conscious.

**See also:** 1.3 The Body and Its Needs; 11.2 Knowledge, Reality, and Mind; 15.1 Cognition and Perception; 15.5 Mental Health and Illness; 16.5 Artificial Intelligence.

### 1.5 Sex, Reproduction, and the Life Cycle

**In one sentence:** Humans reproduce sexually, carry one baby (usually) for about nine months, raise children who stay dependent for over a decade, and then age and die, with a maximum recorded lifespan of 122 years.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Sexual reproduction** means a new individual is formed by combining genetic material from two parents. Each parent supplies a **gamete** (a sex cell carrying half a genome).
- **Sex** in biology refers to which kind of gamete an organism's body is organized to produce. **Females** produce large gametes (**eggs**); **males** produce small, mobile gametes (**sperm**).
- **Sex chromosomes:** most human females have two X chromosomes (XX) and most males have one X and one Y (XY). Genes on the Y chromosome normally trigger male development.
- **Gender** refers to the social roles, identities, and expectations a society attaches to sex. It is covered in 8.2.
- **Puberty** is the period, usually in the early teens, when the body becomes able to reproduce.

**How it works:**
*Sex and its variation.* Most humans are clearly male or female in chromosomes, gonads (ovaries or testes), and anatomy. A small minority are born with **intersex** variations (also called differences of sex development), in which these features do not all align. How common this is depends on which conditions are counted: estimates range from about 1 in 5,000 to nearly 2 in 100 [Contested, mainly over definitions].

*Conception and pregnancy.* Females release roughly one egg per month in the **menstrual cycle** (about 28 days on average; the womb lining is shed as menstrual bleeding if no pregnancy occurs). Sperm deposited during sexual intercourse can fertilize the egg. The fertilized egg implants in the **uterus** (womb). Pregnancy lasts about 38 weeks from conception (conventionally counted as 40 weeks from the last menstrual period). About 1 in 40–80 births are twins, depending on the population.

*Birth.* Human birth is unusually difficult and risky because the baby's large head must pass through a pelvis narrowed by upright walking. Roughly 260,000 women die each year from causes related to pregnancy and childbirth, about 70% of them in sub-Saharan Africa. This number has fallen by about 40% since 2000. In most rich countries fewer than about 1 in 5,000 births ends in the mother's death; in the poorest countries a woman's lifetime risk of dying from pregnancy can be higher than 1 in 50.

*Childhood.* Human infants are born helpless and need years of care. The brain roughly triples in size after birth. Children usually walk at about one year and speak in sentences by about two to three. Puberty begins around ages 8–13 in girls and 9–14 in boys, starting earlier in well-fed populations. This long dependency means human reproduction usually involves heavy investment by parents and others, which shaped family structures (see 8.1).

*Fertility.* Women can typically have children from their teens until **menopause** (the end of menstrual cycles), around age 45–55. Men remain fertile longer, with declining quality. Worldwide, about 130 million babies are born each year. The average number of children per woman (the **total fertility rate**) is about 2.2–2.3, down from about 5 in 1950; in many countries it is now well below 2.1, the level needed to replace each generation (see 8.5, 16.6). **Contraception** (methods to prevent pregnancy) such as the birth-control pill (1960) gave humans much more control over fertility. **In vitro fertilization** (IVF: combining egg and sperm in a laboratory dish) has produced over 12 million babies since 1978.

*Aging and death.* **Senescence** is the gradual decline of the body's functions with age. Muscles, bones, eyes, immune defenses, and memory weaken, and the risk of cancer, heart disease, and dementia climbs steeply. Death occurs when the body's systems can no longer sustain the brain. Today about 60–65 million humans die each year. The longest verified human life was 122 years (Jeanne Calment of France, died 1997). On average, women live about 5 years longer than men.

**How it developed:** The female egg was not seen in mammals until 1827; how fertilization works was understood only in the late 1800s. Hormones that control reproduction were identified in the early 1900s. Hospital births, antiseptics, and caesarean section (surgical delivery) became safer in the 20th century, sharply cutting deaths of mothers and babies.

**Effects on human life and society:** Reproduction shapes marriage, inheritance, kinship, and gender roles in nearly all human societies (see 8.1, 8.2). Control over reproduction (contraception, abortion, IVF) is one of the most fiercely debated political subjects. Falling fertility and longer lives are causing populations to age, with large effects on pensions, labor, and health systems (see 16.6).

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- Biologists debate why humans live long past their reproductive years. The "**grandmother hypothesis**" proposes that older women increased their descendants' survival by helping raise grandchildren [Contested].
- Theories of why aging happens include the accumulation of cell damage and evolutionary arguments that natural selection weakens after reproductive age [Refining].
- **Abortion** (ending a pregnancy) is legal on request in many countries and banned or heavily restricted in others. Whether and when a fetus has moral status is a [Belief/Value] question; the medical facts of development are not.
- Many humans believe in life after death, reincarnation, or other continuations of the self [Belief/Value] (see 10.1).

**Key works and figures:**
- Karl Ernst von Baer (1827): first observed the mammalian egg.
- Margaret Sanger and the scientists behind the contraceptive pill (1950s–1960): contraception.
- Robert Edwards and Patrick Steptoe: first IVF birth, 1978.
- Leonard Hayflick (1961): showed normal cells divide only a limited number of times, a clue to aging.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: the basic mechanics of reproduction, development, and aging; the sharp historical fall in deaths of mothers and children.
- Debated: why aging happens at the molecular level and whether it can be slowed substantially [Refining]; the frequency of intersex variation (definitional) [Contested]; causes of falling sperm counts reported in some studies [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: abortion; assisted reproduction and surrogacy; definitions of when life begins; how societies should treat sex and gender categories.

**Open questions:** Can human aging be slowed or reversed? What sets the maximum human lifespan? Why are fertility rates falling so fast even where people say they want more children?

**See also:** 1.2 Genetics and Heredity; 8.1 Family and Kinship; 8.2 Sex, Gender, and Sexuality in Society; 8.5 Population and Migration; 16.6 Demographic Change and Aging.

### 1.6 Health, Disease, and Medicine

**In one sentence:** Humans fall ill from infections, from the slow failure of their own organs, and from injury, and over the last 200 years they have learned to prevent and treat enough of this to roughly double their average lifespan.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Disease** is any condition that impairs the body's normal functioning.
- A **pathogen** is a living or quasi-living agent that causes disease. The main kinds are **bacteria** (single-celled organisms), **viruses** (tiny packets of genetic material that can only reproduce inside cells), **fungi**, and **parasites** (for example the single-celled organism that causes malaria, spread by mosquitoes).
- **Infectious** (or communicable) diseases spread between people or from animals. **Chronic** or **non-communicable diseases** (NCDs) develop inside the body over time, such as heart disease, cancer, and diabetes.
- The **immune system** is the body's defense network. It recognizes foreign material and remembers past infections, so a second exposure is fought off faster.
- A **vaccine** trains the immune system by exposing it to a harmless version or part of a pathogen. An **antibiotic** is a drug that kills bacteria (not viruses).
- **Life expectancy at birth** is the average number of years a newborn would live if current death rates stayed the same. It is pulled down heavily by deaths in infancy, so a low figure does not mean adults died young.

**How it works:**

*What kills humans.* About 60–65 million humans die each year (more during the COVID-19 pandemic). Roughly three-quarters of deaths now come from non-communicable diseases. Leading causes worldwide, in the early 2020s, roughly:

| Cause | Approximate deaths per year |
|---|---|
| Ischemic heart disease (blocked blood supply to the heart) | ~9 million |
| Stroke (blocked or burst blood vessel in the brain) | ~7 million |
| Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (lung damage, mostly from smoking and air pollution) | ~3.5 million |
| Lower respiratory infections (e.g. pneumonia) | ~2.5 million |
| Lung cancer | ~2 million |
| Dementias, including Alzheimer's | ~2 million |
| Diabetes (including diabetes-related kidney disease) | ~2 million |
| Tuberculosis (a bacterial lung infection) | ~1.2 million |
| Road injuries | ~1.2 million |

All cancers together kill about 10 million a year. Major **risk factors** behind these deaths include high blood pressure, tobacco, air pollution, poor diet, high blood sugar, obesity, and alcohol. Air pollution alone is linked to roughly 7–8 million deaths a year [Refining on exact size].

*Where it falls.* The burden is very unequal. In rich countries most people die old, of heart disease, cancer, and dementia. In the poorest countries infectious diseases, malnutrition, and deaths of mothers and newborns remain major killers. About 4.8 million children die before age five each year, down from about 12.5 million in 1990 and from a rate of roughly 4 in 10 children in 1800. Malaria kills about 600,000 people a year, mostly young children in Africa. About 40 million people live with HIV (the virus that causes AIDS); about 600,000 die of it annually, down from about 2 million in the mid-2000s, mostly because of antiretroviral drugs.

*How medicine works.* Modern medicine combines prevention (clean water, sanitation, vaccines, screening), diagnosis (laboratory tests, imaging such as X-rays and MRI scans), and treatment (drugs, surgery, radiation, therapy). New treatments are tested in **clinical trials**, ideally **randomized controlled trials**, in which patients are assigned at random to the treatment or a comparison, so that the effect of the treatment can be separated from chance and expectation (see 3.4). The **placebo effect** (people feeling better because they expect to) is real for symptoms like pain, which is why comparisons are needed.

*Health systems.* Humans spend roughly 10% of world economic output on health care (around US$9–10 trillion a year), ranging from under 5% of GDP in many poor countries to about 17% in the United States. About half the world's population lacks full access to essential health services, and each year over a billion people face health costs that strain household budgets.

**How it developed:**
- **Before ~1850:** most medical systems explained illness through imbalances: the four "humors" (Greek-European medicine), *qi* and yin–yang (Chinese medicine), or the three *doshas* (Indian **Ayurveda**). Many also attributed disease to spirits, sin, or divine will [Belief/Value]. Some practices worked (setting bones, some herbal drugs, inoculation against smallpox in China, India, and Africa); many did not (bloodletting).
- **1796:** Edward Jenner develops a smallpox vaccine.
- **1846–1867:** anesthesia (ether, 1846) makes surgery painless; Joseph Lister introduces antiseptic surgery (1867).
- **1847–1854:** Ignaz Semmelweis shows handwashing cuts deaths of mothers; John Snow traces a cholera outbreak to a contaminated water pump in London (1854).
- **1860s–1880s:** **germ theory** (the finding that specific microorganisms cause specific diseases) is established by Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch, who identified the tuberculosis bacterium in 1882.
- **Early 1900s:** clean water and sewers cause the largest early fall in death rates. Insulin is used to treat diabetes from 1922.
- **1928–1940s:** Alexander Fleming discovers penicillin; mass production during the 1940s begins the antibiotic era.
- **1948:** the first modern randomized controlled trial (of the antibiotic streptomycin for tuberculosis). The World Health Organization (WHO) is founded.
- **1980:** smallpox, which killed an estimated 300 million people in the 20th century alone, is declared eradicated, the only human disease eliminated so far.
- **1981 onward:** the HIV/AIDS pandemic; tens of millions have died since.
- **2020–2023:** the COVID-19 pandemic. About 7 million deaths were officially reported; estimates of total excess deaths are much higher, roughly 15–25 million or more [Refining]. Vaccines using a new technology (messenger RNA) were developed in under a year.

Earlier pandemics were far deadlier as a share of population. The Black Death (a plague, 1347–1351) killed perhaps 30–50% of Europe's population. Diseases brought by Europeans to the Americas after 1492 contributed to population collapses of up to roughly 90% in many places (see 4.5). The 1918 influenza pandemic killed perhaps 50 million people worldwide (estimates range widely).

**Effects on human life and society:** Global life expectancy rose from about 30 years in 1800 to about 73 today. This is perhaps the largest measurable change in human welfare in history. It drove population growth (see 8.5), changed family life (parents expect their children to survive), and made old age common. Vaccination programs are estimated to have saved over 150 million lives since 1974. Health care is one of the largest employers in many economies. Gaps remain huge: life expectancy differs by over 25 years between the healthiest and least healthy countries, and also differs by income, ethnicity, and region within countries.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Evidence-based medicine** (dominant in scientific medicine since the 1990s): treatments should be judged by the best available evidence, especially controlled trials.
- **Traditional and complementary medicine:** systems such as traditional Chinese medicine and Ayurveda are widely used, particularly in Asia and Africa. Some remedies contain active drugs; for example, the malaria drug artemisinin came from a plant used in Chinese medicine (Nobel Prize to Tu Youyou, 2015). Many specific claims have not held up in trials. **Homeopathy** (remedies diluted until no original substance remains) performs no better than placebo in controlled trials [Established].
- **How to organize and pay for health care** is [Contested] and partly [Belief/Value]: single-payer public systems (e.g. the UK's National Health Service, founded 1948), compulsory insurance systems (e.g. Germany, Japan), and largely private insurance (the United States) each have different measured costs and outcomes.
- **Vaccine hesitancy:** some people distrust or refuse vaccines. The scientific evidence that approved vaccines are safe and effective is [Established]; specific rare side effects are monitored and documented. A 1998 study claiming that a vaccine caused autism was found to be fraudulent and retracted.

**Key works and figures:**
- Edward Jenner (1796): first vaccine.
- Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch (1860s–1880s): germ theory.
- John Snow (1854): founded epidemiology (the study of how disease spreads in populations).
- Alexander Fleming (1928), with Howard Florey and Ernst Chain (1940s): penicillin.
- Florence Nightingale (1850s–60s): nursing and the use of statistics to improve hospital hygiene.
- The WHO's smallpox eradication campaign (1967–1980): showed global cooperation can eliminate a disease.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: germ theory; vaccines and antibiotics work; smoking causes cancer and heart disease; sanitation drives large falls in death rates.
- Debated: the best ways to prevent and treat many cancers and dementias [Refining]; the exact health effects of many foods [Contested]; the cost-effectiveness of many treatments; the origin of the COVID-19 virus (spillover from animals vs. a laboratory accident) [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: whether health care is a right; how to ration expensive treatments; euthanasia and assisted dying; the role of religious healing.

**Open questions:** How to stop the spread of **antimicrobial resistance**: bacteria evolving to survive antibiotics, which directly causes over 1 million deaths a year and contributes to several million more. How to cure most cancers and dementias. Where and when the next pandemic will emerge, and how to be ready. How to provide basic care to the half of humanity without full access.
