# Domain 4: Human History

History is the study of the human past. Strictly, it covers the ~5,000 years since writing; the longer period before writing is called **prehistory** and is known from physical remains. Humans number years in a calendar that began, by tradition, with the birth of a religious figure, Jesus of Nazareth. Years before that point are written **BCE** ("before the Common Era") and counted backward; years after are **CE**. This guide was written in the mid-2020s CE, about 2,025 years after that starting point. This domain runs from the first stone tools to the present. It covers all regions, states atrocities plainly with approximate death tolls, and marks where historians disagree. Ancient population and death figures are reconstructions and should be read as orders of magnitude.

### 4.1 Prehistory (before ~10,000 BCE)

**In one sentence:** For over 95% of the time that humans and their close relatives have existed, they lived in small mobile groups that gathered plants and hunted animals, and in that time they spread from Africa to every continent except Antarctica.

**What an alien needs to know first:** **Archaeology** reconstructs the past from physical remains (tools, bones, hearths, art). **Radiocarbon dating** measures the decay of a carbon isotope in once-living material and works back to about 50,000 years. Older material is dated by other radioactive or geological methods. **Ancient DNA**, recovered from bones since the 2010s, now reveals past migrations and mixing. **Hominins** are humans and their extinct relatives after the split from the chimpanzee lineage. **Hunter-gatherers** (or **foragers**) get food from wild plants and animals rather than farming. The **Paleolithic** ("Old Stone Age") is the period of chipped-stone tools before farming.

**What happened:**
- **~7–6 million years ago:** the human lineage separates from the chimpanzee lineage in Africa.
- **~3.3 million years ago:** the oldest known stone tools (Kenya), made before the genus *Homo* is known to exist.
- **~2 million years ago:** *Homo erectus* and related species leave Africa, reaching Georgia (Caucasus) by ~1.8 million years ago and East Asia soon after.
- **~1 million to ~400,000 years ago:** the earliest traces of controlled fire, becoming habitual by ~400,000 years ago [Refining]. Cooking makes food easier to digest.
- **~300,000 years ago:** the oldest known *Homo sapiens* fossils (Jebel Irhoud, Morocco).
- **~100,000–70,000 years ago:** shell beads and engraved ochre in southern Africa, early evidence of symbolic behavior.
- **~70,000–50,000 years ago:** the main dispersal of *Homo sapiens* out of Africa. Non-African humans today carry about 1–2% Neanderthal DNA. Some Pacific populations carry several percent from Denisovans, another extinct group.
- **~65,000–50,000 years ago:** humans reach Australia, requiring sea crossings [date range Contested].
- **~51,000–40,000 years ago:** the oldest known figurative cave paintings (Sulawesi, Indonesia), then carved figurines and bone flutes (Germany, ~40,000 years ago).
- **~40,000 years ago:** Neanderthals disappear. Other hominin species are extinct soon after.
- **~36,000 and ~17,000 years ago:** major painted caves in France (Chauvet, Lascaux).
- **~26,000–19,000 years ago:** the **Last Glacial Maximum**, the coldest part of the last ice age. Sea levels are ~120 m lower than today, joining Siberia to Alaska.
- **~16,000 years ago at the latest:** humans are in the Americas. Footprints in New Mexico suggest ~21,000–23,000 years ago [Contested].
- **~15,000 years ago or earlier:** dogs are domesticated from wolves, the first domestic animal.
- **~11,700 years ago:** the ice age ends and the climate becomes warmer and more stable (the **Holocene** epoch).

Throughout this period, many large animals ("**megafauna**") disappeared: giant marsupials in Australia (~45,000 years ago), and mammoths, giant ground sloths, and native horses in the Americas (~13,000–11,000 years ago).

**How it developed (how humans learned this):** Europeans long assumed the Earth was a few thousand years old, based on readings of the Bible. Stone tools found alongside extinct animals were accepted as evidence of deep human antiquity in 1859. Fossils from Africa from the 1920s onward, radiocarbon dating (1949), and ancient-DNA studies (2010 onward, e.g., the Neanderthal genome) transformed the picture. Each new technique has pushed key dates earlier.

**Effects on human life and society:** Human bodies and minds evolved mostly under forager conditions, a fact often used (sometimes loosely) to explain modern tastes for sugar and fat, fear responses, and group loyalty [Contested in specifics]. World population at the end of this period was small, perhaps 1–10 million people. Language, art, music, long-distance exchange networks, and probably religious belief all appear in this era. Today only a few hundred thousand people live mainly by foraging.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Forager social life:** studies of recent foragers show small bands (~25–50 people) inside wider networks of hundreds, food sharing, and relatively little inherited inequality [Refining]. How far recent foragers resemble ancient ones is [Contested].
- **Violence:** one view holds prehistoric death rates from violence were high (Steven Pinker and others). Another holds that organized war was rare before farming (Douglas Fry and others) [Contested].
- **Megafauna extinctions:** human hunting, climate change, or both [Contested; human impact is strongly supported in Australia and the Americas].
- **Origin of language:** estimates range from ~50,000 to over 200,000 years ago [Contested].
- **"Original affluent society"** (Marshall Sahlins, 1966): foragers worked fewer hours than farmers. This is partly supported and partly disputed.

**Key works and figures:**
- Louis and Mary Leakey: East African fossil and tool discoveries (1930s–1970s).
- Svante Pääbo: ancient DNA and the Neanderthal genome (2010; Nobel Prize 2022).
- Marshall Sahlins, *Stone Age Economics* (1972): forager economics.
- David Reich, *Who We Are and How We Got Here* (2018): ancient-DNA history of migrations.
- The cave art of Sulawesi, Chauvet, and Lascaux: direct evidence of the prehistoric imagination.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: African origin of *Homo sapiens*; dispersal across the planet; interbreeding with Neanderthals and Denisovans; Paleolithic tool sequences.
- Debated: exact dates for Australia and the Americas; causes of megafauna loss; when language and modern cognition arose; forager violence rates.
- Matters of belief or value: whether forager life was "better" or "worse" than later life. Many religious traditions hold their own creation accounts, which their followers believe on faith.

**Open questions:** Why *Homo sapiens* outlasted other hominins. When and how language began. How many separate migrations peopled the Americas. What prehistoric people believed. Their art suggests rich symbolic lives that cannot be read directly.

### 4.2 Farming and the First Civilizations (~10,000–500 BCE)

**In one sentence:** Humans in several regions independently began farming, which allowed far larger populations, permanent villages, then cities, states, writing, kings, organized religion, and large-scale inequality.

**What an alien needs to know first:** **Domestication** is the breeding of plants and animals over generations until they depend on humans and serve human needs (e.g., wheat whose seeds stay on the stalk for harvesting). **Agriculture** is producing food by farming crops and raising animals. A **surplus** is food beyond what producers need, which can feed non-farmers. A **state** is an organization that rules a territory, taxes it, and claims the right to use force. In historians' technical usage, a **civilization** is a society with cities, specialized occupations, a state, monumental building, and usually writing. The word implies no ranking above other societies. The **Bronze Age** (~3300–1200 BCE in the Near East) is when tools and weapons were made of bronze (copper plus tin). The **Iron Age** follows.

**What happened:**
Farming began independently in at least 7–11 regions [Refining on exact count]:

| Region | Approx. start | Main domesticates |
|---|---|---|
| Fertile Crescent (Middle East) | ~9500–8500 BCE | Wheat, barley, lentils; sheep, goats, cattle, pigs |
| China (Yellow and Yangtze rivers) | ~8000–6000 BCE | Millet, rice; pigs, chickens |
| New Guinea | ~7000–5000 BCE | Taro, bananas |
| Mesoamerica (Mexico) | ~8000–7000 BCE | Squash, maize (corn), beans |
| Andes and Amazonia | ~8000–5000 BCE | Potatoes, quinoa, cassava; llamas |
| Sahel and West Africa | ~3000–2000 BCE | Sorghum, pearl millet, African rice |
| Eastern North America | ~3000–2000 BCE | Sunflower, squash, goosefoot |

Key events:
- **~9500 BCE:** Göbekli Tepe (Turkey), monumental stone enclosures built by people who may not yet have farmed.
- **~7100–5700 BCE:** Çatalhöyük (Turkey), a farming town of several thousand people.
- **~4000–3100 BCE:** the first cities in Mesopotamia (Iraq), led by Uruk, with tens of thousands of residents.
- **~3400–3200 BCE:** **writing** is invented in Mesopotamia (cuneiform), first for accounts of grain and livestock. Egyptian hieroglyphs appear around the same time. Writing was invented independently in China (by ~1250 BCE) and Mesoamerica (by ~600 BCE), and possibly elsewhere.
- **~3100 BCE:** Egypt is unified under one king (pharaoh). The Great Pyramid of Giza is built ~2560 BCE.
- **~3000–1800 BCE:** Caral and related sites in coastal Peru, the oldest monumental centers in the Americas.
- **~2600–1900 BCE:** the Indus Valley civilization (Pakistan and northwest India), with planned cities such as Mohenjo-daro and drainage systems. Its script is still undeciphered.
- **~2300 BCE:** Sargon of Akkad conquers Mesopotamian city-states, an early empire.
- **~2100 BCE:** the oldest known law code (Ur-Nammu). **~1754 BCE:** the **Code of Hammurabi** (Babylon), with 282 laws. Punishments vary by social class, and enslaved people appear as property.
- **~1600–1046 BCE:** the Shang dynasty in China, with bronze casting and oracle-bone writing. The Zhou dynasty (1046–256 BCE) introduces the idea of the "Mandate of Heaven," the belief that heaven grants rulers the right to rule and withdraws it from unjust ones.
- **~1600–400 BCE:** the Olmec culture in Mexico.
- **~1500–500 BCE:** in South Asia, the Vedic period; the Vedas, the oldest Hindu scriptures, are composed.
- **~1200–1150 BCE:** the **Late Bronze Age collapse**: many eastern Mediterranean states fall within decades.
- **~1000 BCE onward:** iron-working spreads across Eurasia and Africa. Farming spreads across sub-Saharan Africa with Bantu-speaking peoples (~3000 BCE–500 CE). Austronesian seafarers move from Taiwan into Southeast Asia and the Pacific.
- **~1050 BCE:** the Phoenician alphabet, ancestor of most of the world's alphabets.
- **~900–612 BCE:** the Assyrian Empire, known for mass deportations of conquered peoples.
- **~750 BCE:** the Kingdom of Kush (Sudan) rules Egypt for about a century.

**How it developed (how humans learned this):** In 1936, the archaeologist V. Gordon Childe called this change the "Neolithic Revolution." Decipherment of hieroglyphs (1822) and cuneiform (1850s) opened ancient records. Plant and animal genetics and radiocarbon dating now track domestication in detail.

**Effects on human life and society:** World population rose from perhaps ~5 million around 10,000 BCE to ~50 million by 1000 BCE. Farming fed more people per unit of land, but early farmers were often shorter, had more tooth decay, and suffered more infectious diseases than foragers. Living closely with animals let diseases jump to humans. Stored surplus enabled full-time rulers, priests, soldiers, and craftspeople, and also **slavery**, organized warfare, taxation, and large inequalities of wealth and gender. Writing allowed records, law, literature, and bureaucracy.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Why farming began:** climate stabilization after the ice age; population pressure; or social and religious motives such as feasting (suggested by Göbekli Tepe) [Contested].
- **Was it an improvement?** The traditional view is that farming was progress. Jared Diamond (1987) called it "the worst mistake in the history of the human race" for ordinary people's health and equality. The answer depends on what is measured [Belief/Value in part].
- **How states formed:** to manage irrigation (Karl Wittfogel); through war when land was hemmed in (Robert Carneiro, 1970); through coercion and grain taxation (James C. Scott, 2017). Others argue early societies experimented with many political forms, including egalitarian cities (Graeber and Wengrow, 2021) [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- The Code of Hammurabi (~1754 BCE): early written law and social hierarchy.
- *Epic of Gilgamesh* (~2100–1200 BCE): among the oldest surviving literature.
- V. Gordon Childe, *Man Makes Himself* (1936): framed the "Neolithic Revolution."
- James C. Scott, *Against the Grain* (2017): critique of early states.
- David Graeber and David Wengrow, *The Dawn of Everything* (2021): argues for variety in early social forms [Contested].

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: multiple independent origins of farming; the sequence from villages to cities to states in several regions; independent invention of writing at least three times.
- Debated: why farming started; why states arose; the meaning of the Indus script; the causes of the Bronze Age collapse (drought, invasions, systems failure).
- Matters of belief or value: whether "civilization" was progress. The Mandate of Heaven and the divine status claimed by pharaohs were beliefs held at the time.

**Open questions:** What the Indus Valley people wrote and how they were governed. How much early cities were egalitarian versus hierarchical. How and when slavery first became institutionalized.

### 4.3 Classical Empires and Universal Religions (~500 BCE–500 CE)

**In one sentence:** Large, long-lasting empires unified huge regions of Eurasia and North Africa, while new philosophies and religions arose that claimed to speak to all humans, and many of them still shape billions of lives.

**What an alien needs to know first:** An **empire** is a state that rules many different peoples and territories, usually by conquest, from a central power. A **dynasty** is a sequence of rulers from one family. **Philosophy** is the reasoned study of fundamental questions (see 11.1). A **universal religion** offers its teachings to all people regardless of ethnicity, unlike religions tied to a particular people or city. The **Silk Road** is a modern name (coined in 1877) for the overland trade networks linking China, Central Asia, Persia, India, and the Mediterranean. Sea routes across the Indian Ocean, driven by seasonal monsoon winds, were at least as important.

**What happened:**
- **~6th–5th c. BCE:** a cluster of influential teachers: Confucius (551–479 BCE) in China; the Buddha (Siddhartha Gautama, ~5th c. BCE, dates uncertain) and Mahavira (founder figure of Jainism) in India; the Hebrew prophets; Greek philosophers. The idea of an "Axial Age" of simultaneous change is [Contested].
- **550 BCE:** Cyrus the Great founds the **Achaemenid Persian Empire**, the largest yet, stretching from Egypt to India and ruling tens of millions of people. Zoroastrianism, a religion centered on a cosmic struggle between good and evil, is influential at court.
- **508 BCE:** Athens adopts **democracy** (rule by citizen vote). Only adult male citizens vote, perhaps 30,000 of 250,000–300,000 residents. Many residents are enslaved.
- **509 BCE:** the Roman Republic is founded (traditional date).
- **~470–322 BCE:** Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle in Athens.
- **336–323 BCE:** Alexander of Macedon conquers the Persian Empire as far as northwest India. Greek-speaking kingdoms follow.
- **~322–185 BCE:** the **Maurya Empire** unifies most of South Asia. Around 261 BCE, Emperor Ashoka conquers Kalinga. His own inscriptions state that 100,000 were killed and 150,000 deported. He then promotes Buddhism and moral rule, and sends missionaries abroad.
- **221 BCE:** Qin Shi Huang unifies China, standardizing writing, coinage, weights, and axle widths. **206 BCE–220 CE:** the **Han dynasty**. A 2 CE census counts ~58 million people. Paper is in use by ~100 CE.
- **264–146 BCE:** Rome defeats Carthage (North Africa) and destroys the city in 146 BCE.
- **~130 BCE:** Han envoys open regular contact with Central Asia; Silk Road trade expands.
- **73–71 BCE:** an uprising of enslaved people led by Spartacus is crushed in Italy; ~6,000 captives are crucified.
- **27 BCE:** Augustus becomes the first Roman emperor. At its peak (~117 CE) the empire rules ~50–70 million people. Enslaved people make up perhaps 10–30% of Roman Italy's population [Refining].
- **~30 CE:** Jesus of Nazareth is executed by Roman authorities. His followers believe he rose from the dead and was the son of God. Christianity spreads through the empire despite periodic persecution.
- **70 CE:** Rome destroys the Jewish Temple in Jerusalem after a revolt. Jewish life increasingly centers on scattered communities (the **diaspora**) and on rabbinic scholarship.
- **~1st–3rd c. CE:** the Kushan Empire (Central Asia and north India) helps carry Buddhism into China.
- **~100–940 CE:** the Kingdom of Aksum (Ethiopia and Eritrea), a Red Sea trading power, adopts Christianity in the 330s.
- **224–651 CE:** the Sasanian Persian Empire, Rome's main rival.
- **~100 BCE–550 CE:** Teotihuacan (Mexico), a city of perhaps 100,000 people at its height. The Classic Maya city-states flourish ~250–900 CE. The Moche and Nazca cultures flourish in Peru.
- **220 CE:** the Han dynasty collapses after rebellions; China fragments for over 350 years.
- **~320–550 CE:** the Gupta Empire in India; Aryabhata's astronomy (499 CE) and the decimal number system.
- **313 and 380 CE:** Rome legalizes Christianity, then makes it the state religion.
- **395–476 CE:** the Roman Empire splits permanently. The western half falls to Germanic kingdoms (last western emperor deposed in 476). The eastern half (the **Byzantine Empire**) lasts until 1453.

**How it developed (how humans learned this):** This period produced the first sustained historical writing: Herodotus and Thucydides in Greece, Sima Qian in China. Inscriptions, coins, archaeology, and, more recently, ice cores and tree rings that record climate add to the picture.

**Effects on human life and society:** Roughly 200–300 million people lived on Earth around 1 CE, and Rome and Han China each ruled perhaps a quarter of them. Institutions from this era persist: Roman law underlies the legal systems of most of Europe and Latin America (see 7.1). Confucian ideas of hierarchy, family duty, and scholar-officials shaped East Asian government for 2,000 years. Buddhism (now ~0.3–0.5 billion followers) and Christianity (~2.3 billion) grew from this era. Greek, Latin, Sanskrit, and Classical Chinese became languages of learning for over a millennium. These empires also rested on conquest, heavy taxation of peasants, and widespread slavery.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Why Rome's western half fell:** Edward Gibbon (1776–89) blamed moral decline and Christianity. Modern explanations stress invasions, civil wars, fiscal strain, plague, and climate. Some historians describe a gradual "transformation" rather than a "fall"; others stress real material collapse (e.g., Bryan Ward-Perkins, 2005) [Contested].
- **Why China reunified repeatedly while Europe did not:** geography, a shared written language, and the Confucian bureaucracy are proposed [Contested].
- **The "Axial Age"** (Karl Jaspers, 1949): a single global turning point in thought. Critics see separate developments with no common cause [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- Herodotus, *Histories* (~430 BCE): founding work of Greek historical writing.
- Sima Qian, *Records of the Grand Historian* (~94 BCE): model for 2,000 years of Chinese history writing.
- Ashoka's edicts (~260–230 BCE): rock inscriptions on rule and morality.
- Confucius, *Analects*: the foundation of Confucian ethics and statecraft.
- *Arthashastra* (attributed to Kautilya): Indian treatise on statecraft.
- Edward Gibbon, *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire* (1776–89): influential, now dated, account.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: the extent and chronology of these empires; the spread of Buddhism and Christianity; the scale of slavery in Greek and Roman society.
- Debated: population sizes; causes of imperial collapse; how far the Silk Road connected ordinary lives.
- Matters of belief or value: the religious claims of Buddhism, Christianity, Judaism, and Zoroastrianism (described in Domain 10); judgments of empires as "civilizing" or "oppressive."

**Open questions:** How much contact and influence flowed between Rome, India, and China. How Teotihuacan was governed. How much climate shifts and epidemics drove the crises of the 3rd and 6th centuries.
