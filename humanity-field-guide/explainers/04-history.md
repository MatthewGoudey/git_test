# Domain 4: Human History

History is the study of the human past. Strictly, it covers the ~5,000 years since writing; the longer period before writing is called **prehistory** and is known from physical remains. Humans number years in a calendar that began, by tradition, with the birth of a religious figure, Jesus of Nazareth. Years before that point are written **BCE** ("before the Common Era") and counted backward; years after are **CE**. This guide was written in the mid-2020s CE, about 2,025 years after that starting point. This domain runs from the first stone tools to the present. It covers all regions, states atrocities plainly with approximate death tolls, and marks where historians disagree. Ancient population and death figures are reconstructions and should be read as orders of magnitude.

### 4.1 Prehistory (before ~10,000 BCE)

**In one sentence:** For over 95% of the time that humans and their close relatives have existed, they lived in small mobile groups that gathered plants and hunted animals, and in that time they spread from Africa to every continent except Antarctica.

**What an alien needs to know first:** **Archaeology** reconstructs the past from physical remains (tools, bones, hearths, art). **Radiocarbon dating** measures the decay of a form of carbon in once-living material and reaches back ~50,000 years; older material is dated by other methods. **Ancient DNA** from bones now reveals past migrations. **Hominins** are humans and their extinct relatives after the split from the chimpanzee lineage. **Hunter-gatherers** (or **foragers**) get food from wild plants and animals rather than farming. The **Paleolithic** ("Old Stone Age") is the period of chipped-stone tools before farming.

**What happened:**
- **~7–6 million years ago:** the human lineage separates from the chimpanzee lineage in Africa.
- **~3.3 million years ago:** the oldest known stone tools (Kenya), made before the genus *Homo* is known to exist.
- **~2 million years ago:** *Homo erectus* and related species leave Africa, reaching Georgia (Caucasus) by ~1.8 million years ago and East Asia soon after.
- **~1 million to ~400,000 years ago:** the earliest traces of controlled fire, becoming habitual by ~400,000 years ago [Refining]. Cooking makes food easier to digest.
- **~300,000 years ago:** the oldest known *Homo sapiens* fossils (Jebel Irhoud, Morocco).
- **~100,000–70,000 years ago:** shell beads and engraved ochre in southern Africa, early evidence of symbolic behavior.
- **~70,000–50,000 years ago:** the main dispersal of *Homo sapiens* out of Africa. Non-African humans today carry about 1–2% Neanderthal DNA. Some Pacific populations carry several percent from Denisovans, another extinct group.
- **~65,000–50,000 years ago:** humans reach Australia, requiring sea crossings [date range Contested].
- **~51,000–17,000 years ago:** figurative cave art (Sulawesi, Indonesia, ~51,000 years ago; Chauvet and Lascaux, France, ~36,000 and ~17,000), carved figurines, and bone flutes.
- **~40,000 years ago:** Neanderthals disappear; other hominin species are extinct soon after.
- **~26,000–19,000 years ago:** the **Last Glacial Maximum**, the coldest part of the last ice age. Sea levels are ~120 m lower than today, joining Siberia to Alaska.
- **~16,000 years ago at the latest:** humans are in the Americas. Footprints in New Mexico suggest ~21,000–23,000 years ago [Contested].
- **~15,000 years ago or earlier:** dogs are domesticated from wolves, the first domestic animal.
- **~11,700 years ago:** the ice age ends and the climate becomes warmer and more stable (the **Holocene** epoch).

Throughout this period, many large animals ("**megafauna**") disappeared: giant marsupials in Australia (~45,000 years ago), and mammoths, giant ground sloths, and native horses in the Americas (~13,000–11,000 years ago).

**How it developed (how humans learned this):** Many Europeans long believed, from readings of the Bible, that Earth was a few thousand years old. Deep human antiquity was accepted in 1859, when stone tools were found with extinct animals. African fossils (1920s onward), radiocarbon dating (1949), and ancient DNA (2010 onward) transformed the picture, each pushing key dates earlier.

**Effects on human life and society:** Human bodies and minds evolved mostly under forager conditions, which is often used (sometimes loosely) to explain modern tastes for sugar and fat and strong group loyalty [Contested in specifics]. World population by 10,000 BCE was perhaps 1–10 million. Language, art, music, long-distance exchange, and probably religious belief all appear in this era. Today only a few hundred thousand people live mainly by foraging.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Forager social life:** studies of recent foragers show small bands (~25–50 people) inside wider networks of hundreds, food sharing, and relatively little inherited inequality [Refining]. How far recent foragers resemble ancient ones is [Contested].
- **Violence:** one view holds prehistoric death rates from violence were high (Steven Pinker and others). Another holds that organized war was rare before farming (Douglas Fry and others) [Contested].
- **Megafauna extinctions:** human hunting, climate change, or both [Contested; human impact is strongly supported in Australia and the Americas].
- **Origin of language:** estimates range from ~50,000 to over 200,000 years ago [Contested].
- **"Original affluent society"** (Marshall Sahlins, 1966, in *Stone Age Economics*, 1972): foragers worked fewer hours than farmers [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- Louis and Mary Leakey: East African fossil and tool discoveries (1930s–1970s).
- Svante Pääbo: ancient DNA and the Neanderthal genome (2010; Nobel Prize 2022).
- David Reich, *Who We Are and How We Got Here* (2018): ancient-DNA history of migrations.
- The cave art of Sulawesi, Chauvet, and Lascaux: direct evidence of the prehistoric imagination.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: African origin of *Homo sapiens*; dispersal across the planet; interbreeding with Neanderthals and Denisovans; Paleolithic tool sequences.
- Debated: exact dates for Australia and the Americas; causes of megafauna loss; when language and modern cognition arose; forager violence rates.
- Matters of belief or value: whether forager life was "better" or "worse" than later life. Many religious traditions hold their own creation accounts, which their followers believe on faith.

**Open questions:** Why *Homo sapiens* outlasted other hominins. When language began. How many migrations peopled the Americas. What prehistoric people believed; their art suggests rich symbolic lives that cannot be read directly.

**See also:** 1.1 Evolution and Human Origins; 2.5 Climate and Atmosphere; 4.2 Farming and the First Civilizations; 10.1 What Religion Is and Why Humans Have It; 13.1 Language; 14.4 Visual Arts and Architecture.

### 4.2 Farming and the First Civilizations (~10,000–500 BCE)

**In one sentence:** Humans in several regions independently began farming, which allowed far larger populations, permanent villages, then cities, states, writing, kings, organized religion, and large-scale inequality.

**What an alien needs to know first:** **Domestication** is breeding plants and animals over generations until they serve human needs and depend on humans (e.g., wheat whose seeds stay on the stalk for harvest). **Agriculture** is farming crops and raising animals. A **surplus** is food beyond what producers need, which can feed non-farmers. A **state** is an organization that rules a territory, taxes it, and claims the right to use force. In historians' usage, a **civilization** is a society with cities, specialized occupations, a state, monumental building, and usually writing; the word implies no ranking. The **Bronze Age** (~3300–1200 BCE in the Near East) is when tools were made of bronze (copper plus tin); the **Iron Age** follows.

**What happened:**
Farming began independently in at least 7–11 regions [Refining on exact count]:

| Region | Approx. start | Main domesticates |
|---|---|---|
| Fertile Crescent (Middle East) | ~9500–8500 BCE | Wheat, barley, lentils; sheep, goats, cattle, pigs |
| China (Yellow and Yangtze rivers) | ~8000–6000 BCE | Millet, rice; pigs, chickens |
| New Guinea | ~7000–5000 BCE | Taro, bananas |
| Mesoamerica (Mexico) | ~8000–7000 BCE | Squash, maize (corn), beans |
| Andes and Amazonia | ~8000–5000 BCE | Potatoes, quinoa, cassava; llamas |
| Sahel and West Africa | ~3000–2000 BCE | Sorghum, pearl millet, African rice |
| Eastern North America | ~3000–2000 BCE | Sunflower, squash, goosefoot |

Key events:
- **~9500 BCE:** Göbekli Tepe (Turkey), monumental stone enclosures built by people who may not yet have farmed.
- **~7100–5700 BCE:** Çatalhöyük (Turkey), a farming town of several thousand people.
- **~4000–3100 BCE:** the first cities in Mesopotamia (Iraq), led by Uruk, with tens of thousands of residents.
- **~3400–3200 BCE:** **writing** (cuneiform) is invented in Mesopotamia, first for grain and livestock accounts; Egyptian hieroglyphs follow closely. Writing was invented independently in China (by ~1250 BCE) and Mesoamerica (by ~600 BCE).
- **~3100 BCE:** Egypt is unified under one king (pharaoh). The Great Pyramid is built ~2560 BCE.
- **~3000–1800 BCE:** Caral (coastal Peru), the oldest monumental center in the Americas.
- **~2600–1900 BCE:** the Indus Valley civilization (Pakistan and northwest India), with planned cities and drains. Its script is undeciphered.
- **~2300 BCE:** Sargon of Akkad builds an early empire in Mesopotamia.
- **~1754 BCE:** the **Code of Hammurabi** (Babylon), 282 laws. Punishments vary by social class; enslaved people are property.
- **~1600–1046 BCE:** the Shang dynasty in China. The following Zhou dynasty promotes the "Mandate of Heaven," the belief that heaven grants rulers the right to rule and withdraws it from unjust ones.
- **~1600–400 BCE:** the Olmec culture in Mexico. **~1500–500 BCE:** the Vedic period in South Asia; the Vedas, the oldest Hindu scriptures, are composed.
- **~1200–1150 BCE:** the **Late Bronze Age collapse**: many eastern Mediterranean states fall within decades.
- **~1050 BCE:** the Phoenician alphabet, ancestor of most alphabets.
- **~3000 BCE–500 CE:** Bantu-speaking farmers spread across sub-Saharan Africa; Austronesian seafarers move from Taiwan into Southeast Asia and the Pacific. Iron-working spreads after ~1000 BCE.
- **~900–612 BCE:** the Assyrian Empire, known for mass deportations. **~750 BCE:** Kush (Sudan) rules Egypt for a century.

**How it developed (how humans learned this):** V. Gordon Childe named the "Neolithic Revolution" in 1936. Decipherment of hieroglyphs (1822) and cuneiform (1850s) opened ancient records; genetics and radiocarbon dating now track domestication.

**Effects on human life and society:** World population rose from ~5 million (~10,000 BCE) to ~50 million (~1000 BCE). Early farmers were often shorter, had more tooth decay, and suffered more infectious disease than foragers; living with animals let diseases jump to humans. Stored surplus supported full-time rulers, priests, soldiers, and craftspeople, and also **slavery**, organized war, taxation, and large inequalities of wealth and gender. Writing enabled records, law, literature, and bureaucracy.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Why farming began:** climate stabilization after the ice age; population pressure; or social and religious motives such as feasting (suggested by Göbekli Tepe) [Contested].
- **Was it an improvement?** The traditional view says yes. Jared Diamond (1987) called it "the worst mistake in the history of the human race" for ordinary people's health and equality. The answer depends on what is measured [Belief/Value in part].
- **How states formed:** to manage irrigation (Karl Wittfogel); through war when land was hemmed in (Robert Carneiro, 1970); through coercion and grain taxes (James C. Scott). Others stress early experiments with many political forms (Graeber and Wengrow) [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- The Code of Hammurabi (~1754 BCE): early written law and social hierarchy.
- *Epic of Gilgamesh* (~2100–1200 BCE): among the oldest surviving literature.
- James C. Scott, *Against the Grain* (2017): critique of early states.
- David Graeber and David Wengrow, *The Dawn of Everything* (2021): argues for variety in early social forms [Contested].

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: multiple independent origins of farming; the sequence from villages to cities to states in several regions; independent invention of writing at least three times.
- Debated: why farming started; why states arose; the meaning of the Indus script; the causes of the Bronze Age collapse (drought, invasions, systems failure).
- Matters of belief or value: whether "civilization" was progress. The Mandate of Heaven and the divine status claimed by pharaohs were beliefs held at the time.

**Open questions:** What the Indus Valley people wrote and how they were governed. How egalitarian early cities were. When slavery first became institutionalized.

**See also:** 12.2 Agriculture and Food Systems; 13.2 Writing and Literacy; 7.1 Legal Systems; 6.1 The State; 8.4 Social Class and Mobility; 10.2 The Abrahamic Religions; 1.6 Health, Disease, and Medicine.

### 4.3 Classical Empires and Universal Religions (~500 BCE–500 CE)

**In one sentence:** Large, long-lasting empires unified huge regions of Eurasia and North Africa, while new philosophies and religions arose that claimed to speak to all humans, and many of them still shape billions of lives.

**What an alien needs to know first:** An **empire** is a state that rules many peoples and territories, usually won by conquest, from a central power. A **dynasty** is a sequence of rulers from one family. A **universal religion** offers its teachings to all people, unlike religions tied to one people or city. The **Silk Road** is a modern name (coined 1877) for overland trade networks linking China, Central Asia, Persia, India, and the Mediterranean. Sea routes across the Indian Ocean, driven by seasonal monsoon winds, were at least as important.

**What happened:**
- **~6th–5th c. BCE:** influential teachers cluster: Confucius (551–479 BCE) in China; the Buddha (Siddhartha Gautama, dates uncertain) and Mahavira (Jainism) in India; Hebrew prophets; Greek philosophers.
- **550 BCE:** Cyrus the Great founds the **Achaemenid Persian Empire**, the largest yet, from Egypt to India, ruling tens of millions.
- **509–508 BCE:** the Roman Republic is founded (traditional date). Athens adopts **democracy** (rule by citizen vote), but only adult male citizens vote, perhaps 30,000 of 250,000–300,000 residents; many residents are enslaved.
- **~470–322 BCE:** Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle in Athens.
- **336–323 BCE:** Alexander of Macedon conquers the Persian Empire to northwest India.
- **~322–185 BCE:** the **Maurya Empire** unifies most of South Asia. Around 261 BCE Emperor Ashoka conquers Kalinga; his own inscriptions record 100,000 killed and 150,000 deported. He then promotes Buddhism and sends missionaries abroad.
- **221 BCE:** Qin Shi Huang unifies China, standardizing script, coins, and weights. **206 BCE–220 CE:** the **Han dynasty**; a 2 CE census counts ~58 million people; paper is in use by ~100 CE; Silk Road trade expands from ~130 BCE.
- **146 BCE:** Rome destroys Carthage (North Africa). **73–71 BCE:** a revolt of enslaved people led by Spartacus is crushed; ~6,000 captives are crucified.
- **27 BCE:** Augustus becomes the first Roman emperor. At its peak (~117 CE) the empire rules ~50–70 million people; enslaved people are perhaps 10–30% of Roman Italy's population [Refining].
- **~30 CE:** Roman authorities execute Jesus of Nazareth. His followers believe he rose from the dead and was the son of God. Christianity spreads despite periodic persecution.
- **70 CE:** Rome destroys the Jewish Temple in Jerusalem; Jewish life centers increasingly on scattered communities (the **diaspora**).
- **~1st–3rd c. CE:** the Kushan Empire helps carry Buddhism into China. **224–651:** the Sasanian Persian Empire, Rome's rival.
- **~100 BCE–550 CE:** Teotihuacan (Mexico), a city of perhaps 100,000. Classic Maya city-states flourish ~250–900. Moche and Nazca cultures flourish in Peru.
- **330s CE:** the Kingdom of Aksum (Ethiopia and Eritrea), a Red Sea trading power, adopts Christianity.
- **220 CE:** the Han collapses; China stays divided for over 350 years.
- **~320–550 CE:** the Gupta Empire in India; Aryabhata's astronomy (499) and decimal numerals.
- **313 and 380 CE:** Rome legalizes Christianity, then makes it the state religion.
- **395–476 CE:** the Roman Empire splits. The western half falls to Germanic kingdoms (476); the eastern half (the **Byzantine Empire**) lasts until 1453.

**How it developed (how humans learned this):** This era produced the first sustained history writing (Herodotus and Thucydides in Greece, Sima Qian in China). Inscriptions, coins, archaeology, and climate records from ice cores and tree rings add to the picture.

**Effects on human life and society:** Roughly 200–300 million people lived around 1 CE; Rome and Han China each ruled perhaps a quarter of them. Roman law underlies the legal systems of most of Europe and Latin America (see 7.1). Confucian ideas of hierarchy, family duty, and scholar-officials shaped East Asian government for 2,000 years. Buddhism (now ~0.3–0.5 billion followers) and Christianity (~2.3 billion) grew from this era. These empires also rested on conquest, heavy taxation of peasants, and widespread slavery.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Why Rome's western half fell:** Edward Gibbon (1776–89) blamed moral decline and Christianity. Modern explanations stress invasions, civil wars, fiscal strain, plague, and climate. Some describe a gradual "transformation"; others stress real material collapse (Bryan Ward-Perkins, 2005) [Contested].
- **Why China repeatedly reunified while Europe did not:** geography, a shared written language, a Confucian bureaucracy [Contested].
- **The "Axial Age"** (Karl Jaspers, 1949): a single global turning point in thought. Critics see separate developments with no common cause [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- Herodotus, *Histories* (~430 BCE): founding work of Greek history writing.
- Sima Qian, *Records of the Grand Historian* (~94 BCE): model for 2,000 years of Chinese history writing.
- Ashoka's edicts (~260–230 BCE): rock inscriptions on rule and morality.
- Confucius, *Analects*: foundation of Confucian ethics and statecraft.
- Alexander, Qin Shi Huang, Augustus: builders of the era's largest states.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: the extent and chronology of these empires; the spread of Buddhism and Christianity; the scale of slavery in Greek and Roman society.
- Debated: population sizes; causes of imperial collapse; how far the Silk Road connected ordinary lives.
- Matters of belief or value: the religious claims of Buddhism, Christianity, Judaism, and Zoroastrianism (described in Domain 10); judgments of empires as "civilizing" or "oppressive."

**Open questions:** How much contact and influence flowed between Rome, India, and China. How Teotihuacan was governed. How much climate shifts and epidemics drove the crises of the 3rd and 6th centuries.

**See also:** 10.2 The Abrahamic Religions; 10.3 Religions of South and East Asia; 11.5 World Philosophical Traditions; 6.2 Forms of Government; 7.1 Legal Systems; 5.7 International Trade and the Global Economy.

### 4.4 The Medieval and Early Modern World (~500–1750)

**In one sentence:** In these twelve centuries Islam and new empires reshaped Afro-Eurasia, Mongol conquest briefly linked it, plague killed a large share of its people, and late in the period printing, religious upheaval, and the Scientific Revolution transformed Europe.

**What an alien needs to know first:** "**Medieval**" (the "Middle Ages") is a European label for the time between Rome's western collapse (~500) and the **Renaissance** (~1400s–1500s). It fits other regions poorly. **Feudalism** describes lords granting land to followers for military service while peasants, often **serfs** legally bound to the land, farmed it. It is used mainly for Europe and Japan, and many historians find it too tidy. **Islam** is the religion based on what Muslims believe were revelations from God (Allah) to the Prophet Muhammad. A **caliphate** is a state led by a **caliph**, a ruler claiming succession to Muhammad.

**What happened:**
- **541–~750:** the **Plague of Justinian**, the same bacterium as the later Black Death, recurs around the Mediterranean [death toll Contested].
- **610–750:** Muhammad preaches Islam in Arabia (d. 632). Within a century a caliphate stretches from Spain to Central Asia. The **Abbasid** caliphate (750–1258) makes Baghdad a center of science and translation.
- **618–907:** Tang China; civil-service examinations become the main route to office.
- **~300–1600:** West African empires grow rich on trans-Saharan gold: Ghana, then **Mali** (~1235–1600), whose ruler Mansa Musa made a famously lavish pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324, then Songhai. Timbuktu becomes a center of Islamic learning.
- **~802–1431:** the Khmer Empire (Cambodia); Angkor Wat is built in the 1100s.
- **960–1279:** **Song China**: movable-type printing, paper money, the navigational compass, gunpowder weapons, ~100 million people.
- **~1000–1300:** Polynesian navigators settle Hawaii, Rapa Nui, and New Zealand, the last major lands reached by humans.
- **1054:** Christianity splits into Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic churches. **1095–1291:** the **Crusades**, European Christian campaigns to take Jerusalem, with massacres on several sides.
- **~1050–1450:** Cahokia (Mississippi valley, ~10,000–20,000 people); Great Zimbabwe in southern Africa; Swahili trading cities on the East African coast.
- **1206–1368:** the **Mongol Empire** of Genghis Khan and his heirs becomes the largest contiguous land empire ever (~24 million km²). Its conquests killed millions [totals in the tens of millions often cited, Contested]. Baghdad is sacked in 1258. Afterward, Mongol rule eases trade across Eurasia.
- **1347–1351:** the **Black Death** kills roughly one-third to one-half of Europe's people (~25–50 million) and many in the Middle East and North Africa. Europeans massacre Jewish communities, blaming them for the plague.
- **1368–1644:** Ming China. Zheng He's fleets (1405–1433), with up to ~27,000 men, reach East Africa.
- **1325–1521:** the Mexica (Aztec) capital Tenochtitlan grows to perhaps 200,000; the Aztec state practices large-scale human sacrifice [numbers Contested]. **1438–1533:** the **Inca Empire** rules ~10 million people along the Andes, with ~40,000 km of roads and knotted-cord records (quipu).
- **1450s:** Johannes Gutenberg's printing press in Europe; millions of books by 1500. (Korea printed with metal type by 1377.)
- **1453:** the **Ottoman Empire** (1299–1922) takes Constantinople, ending the Byzantine Empire.
- **1517:** Martin Luther's protest starts the **Protestant Reformation**. Wars of religion follow; the Thirty Years' War (1618–48) kills perhaps 4–8 million.
- **1526–1857:** the **Mughal Empire** rules most of South Asia; around 1700 it is among the world's largest economies. **1501–1736:** the Safavids make Shia Islam Iran's state religion.
- **1543–1687:** the **Scientific Revolution** in Europe (see 3.3).
- **1603–1868:** Tokugawa Japan, largely closed to foreign trade from the 1630s.
- **1644:** Ming China falls to the Manchu **Qing dynasty** amid famine during a cold period (the "Little Ice Age").

Slavery existed throughout. Trans-Saharan, Red Sea, and Indian Ocean trades carried several million enslaved Africans to North Africa, the Middle East, and Asia over many centuries [totals Contested]. European witch hunts (~1450–1750) executed ~40,000–60,000 people.

**How it developed (how humans learned this):** Written sources multiply in Arabic, Chinese, Persian, Latin, and Ge'ez, including Ibn Khaldun's *Muqaddimah* (1377) and Ibn Battuta's travels (1350s). For the Americas and much of Africa, archaeology and oral tradition fill gaps.

**Effects on human life and society:** World population rose from ~200–250 million (~500) to ~750–800 million (~1750). Islam (now ~2 billion followers) spread across Afro-Eurasia, and the world map of religions largely took its present form. Most humans remained peasant farmers. Printing made books cheap and spread both the Reformation and science.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **"Dark Ages":** historians largely reject this old label for early medieval Europe. How much material decline occurred after 500 is [Contested].
- **Why Europe, peripheral in 1000, later rose:** competition among small states, institutions, printing, Atlantic access, or colonial resources (see 4.5) [Contested].
- **Black Death effects:** labor shortages raised wages and weakened serfdom in western Europe [Refining].
- **Westphalia (1648):** often called the origin of the modern sovereign-state system [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- Muhammad (~570–632): founder figure of Islam (see 10.2).
- Genghis Khan (~1162–1227): founder of the Mongol Empire.
- Mansa Musa (r. ~1312–1337): ruler of Mali at its height.
- Ibn Khaldun, *Muqaddimah* (1377): early social theory of history.
- Gutenberg and Luther: printing and the Reformation.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: the rise of Islamic empires; Mongol conquests; the Black Death's scale in Europe; the sequence of printing, Reformation, and Scientific Revolution.
- Debated: Mongol and early-plague death tolls; feudalism as a concept; why scientific and industrial breakthroughs came in Europe.
- Matters of belief or value: the religious claims of Islam, Christianity, and other faiths; judgments of crusades and conquests.

**Open questions:** Population sizes in the Americas and Africa before 1500. How far trade networks reached inside Africa. How climate shifts drove political crises.

**See also:** 10.2 The Abrahamic Religions; 10.3 Religions of South and East Asia; 1.6 Health, Disease, and Medicine; 13.2 Writing and Literacy; 3.3 The Scientific Method; 8.4 Social Class and Mobility.

### 4.5 Colonialism, Revolutions, and Industry (~1500–1914)

**In one sentence:** European states conquered or dominated most of the world, enslaved and shipped millions of Africans, presided over population collapse in the Americas, and, through revolutions and industrialization, created modern ideas of rights and nations and machine-powered growth.

**What an alien needs to know first:** **Colonialism** is one country's rule over another territory and its people, often with settlers. **Imperialism** is the broader pursuit of power over other lands, including by economic or indirect control. **Chattel slavery** treats humans, and their children, as property to be bought and sold. A **revolution** is a rapid, often violent overthrow of a political order. **Industrialization** is the shift from hand and animal labor to fossil-fuel-powered machines in factories. **Nationalism** is the belief that each **nation** (a people who see themselves as sharing language, culture, or history) should have its own state.

**What happened:**
- **1488–1522:** Portuguese ships round Africa and reach India (1498). Columbus, sailing for Spain, reaches the Caribbean (1492). Magellan's expedition circles the globe.
- **1519–1533:** Spanish forces, with many Indigenous allies, conquer the Aztec and Inca empires.
- **The American collapse:** the Indigenous population of the Americas, perhaps ~50–60 million in 1492 [Contested; estimates ~8–110 million], fell by about 90% within a century. Eurasian diseases (smallpox, measles, influenza) were the main killers, compounded by war, massacre, enslavement, forced labor (e.g., the Potosí silver mines), and famine. Caribbean peoples such as the Taíno were nearly destroyed within decades.
- **The Columbian Exchange:** American maize, potatoes, and cassava spread worldwide; Eurasian livestock, wheat, and diseases reached the Americas.
- **1501–1866: the Atlantic slave trade.** About **12.5 million** Africans were forced onto ships; ~**10.7 million** survived the crossing and ~1.8 million died at sea. Unknown numbers, possibly millions, died during capture and march to the coast. About 40% went to Brazil, a similar share to the Caribbean, ~4% to North America. By 1860 the US held ~4 million enslaved people.
- **1600–1858:** English and Dutch East India Companies, private firms with armies, build trading empires; from 1757 the British company conquers most of India.
- **~1760–1840:** the **Industrial Revolution** in Britain: coal-fired steam engines, mechanized textile mills, railways (from 1825). A second wave (~1870–1914) brings steel, electricity, chemicals, and engines to Germany, the US, and Japan. Workers, including children, often labored 12–14-hour days.
- **1775–1783:** the American Revolution. The 1776 Declaration of Independence proclaims "all men are created equal" while slavery continues.
- **1789–1815:** the French Revolution abolishes feudal privileges and proclaims the Rights of Man; the Terror (1793–94) officially executes ~17,000. The Napoleonic Wars kill perhaps 3–6 million.
- **1791–1804:** the **Haitian Revolution**: enslaved Africans overthrow French rule and found the first state created by formerly enslaved people. In 1825 France forces Haiti, under threat of invasion, to pay an indemnity to former slaveholders, paid off over many decades.
- **1808–1826:** most of Latin America wins independence.
- **Abolition:** Britain bans the slave trade (1807) and colonial slavery (1833), paying £20 million compensation to slaveholders, nothing to the enslaved. Russia frees its serfs (1861). The US abolishes slavery (1865) after a civil war killing ~620,000–750,000. Brazil is last in the Americas (1888).
- **1830s–1890:** the US forcibly removes Native nations from their lands; wars and disease reduce the Native population in the US to ~250,000 by 1900. Aboriginal Australians suffer massacres, dispossession, and disease after British settlement (1788).
- **1839–1864:** Britain wins the **Opium Wars**, forcing China to accept opium and "unequal treaties." The Taiping Rebellion kills roughly 20–30 million [Contested range].
- **1876–1902:** famines in British India and in China kill tens of millions [colonial policy's role Contested].
- **1868:** Japan's **Meiji Restoration** begins rapid industrialization; Japan annexes Korea in 1910. **1861–1871:** Italy and Germany unify.
- **1884–1914:** the **"Scramble for Africa":** European control rises from ~10% of Africa (1870) to ~90% (1914); only Ethiopia (victorious over Italy at Adwa, 1896) and Liberia remain independent. In the **Congo Free State** (1885–1908), owned by Belgium's King Leopold II, forced rubber collection, mutilation, famine, and disease killed millions [toll Contested; often cited up to ~10 million]. Germany's **Herero and Nama genocide** in Namibia (1904–08) killed ~65,000 Herero (~80%) and ~10,000 Nama (~half).
- **1815–1914:** ~50 million Europeans emigrate, mostly to the Americas.

**How it developed (how humans learned this):** Colonizers' archives long dominated. Since the mid-1900s, shipping records (the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database), oral histories, and colonized societies' own sources have widened the picture.

**Effects on human life and society:** World population rose from ~1 billion (1800) to ~1.8 billion (1914). The "**Great Divergence**" opened: western Europe and its settler offshoots pulled far ahead of China and India, which together produced perhaps half of world output around 1700 [Refining]. Today's borders in Africa and Asia, the reach of European languages, and racial hierarchies in the Americas trace to this era, as do ideas of universal rights.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Why Britain industrialized first:** cheap coal and high wages (Robert Allen); institutions (Douglass North); a culture of useful knowledge (Joel Mokyr); colonial land and resources (Kenneth Pomeranz) [Contested].
- **Slavery and capitalism:** Eric Williams (1944) argued slavery's profits helped finance industrialization; the size of that contribution is [Contested].
- **Colonial legacy:** many economists find extractive colonial institutions harmed long-run development (Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson, 2001); others credit colonies with infrastructure and markets [Contested]. Colonial violence itself is [Established]; whether colonialism was justified is [Belief/Value].

**Key works and figures:**
- The Declaration of Independence (1776) and Rights of Man (1789): founding texts of rights-based politics.
- Toussaint Louverture (~1743–1803): leader of the Haitian Revolution.
- Olaudah Equiano, *The Interesting Narrative* (1789): a formerly enslaved man's account that fed abolitionism.
- Marx and Engels, *The Communist Manifesto* (1848): a response to industrial capitalism.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: the scale of the Atlantic slave trade; the Indigenous collapse in the Americas; the Congo and Herero-Nama atrocities.
- Debated: the pre-1492 American population; famine tolls and causes; slavery's and colonies' economic contribution to industrialization.
- Matters of belief or value: whether descendants of the enslaved and colonized should receive **reparations**; how to judge historical figures.

**Open questions:** How many died inside Africa because of the slave trades. Exact population histories of the Americas and Pacific. Why industrialization spread fast to some regions and slowly to others.
