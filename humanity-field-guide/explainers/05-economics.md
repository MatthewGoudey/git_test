# Domain 5: Economics and Material Life

Economics is the study of how humans produce, divide, and use things that are in limited supply: food, shelter, energy, tools, time, and each other's labor. Almost every human spends most of their waking adult life working to obtain these things, and most political conflict is, at least in part, a dispute over who gets what. This domain explains the systems humans have built for this, starting from zero: what a trade is, what money is, why prices move, and why some groups of humans are hundreds of times richer than others. *Figures describe the mid-2020s unless otherwise stated and are given in US dollars (the currency of the United States, which humans use as the main international yardstick).*

### 5.1 Scarcity, Trade, and Markets

**In one sentence:** Humans want more than they can produce, so they must choose what to make and who gets it; today most of those choices are made through voluntary trade in "markets," where prices rise and fall.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Goods and services.** A *good* is a physical thing humans want (bread, a phone). A *service* is useful work done for someone (surgery, teaching).
- **Scarcity.** Land, materials, energy, and time are limited; human wants, in practice, are not.
- **Opportunity cost.** Every choice gives up the next-best alternative. A field planted with wheat cannot also grow rice.
- **Trade (exchange).** Two parties voluntarily swap things. Each gives up something it values less for something it values more, so **both can end up better off**. This is the central fact of economic life: trade is not just dividing a fixed amount; it can create value for both sides.
- **Price.** How much of one thing (usually money; see 5.2) must be given up to get another.
- **Market.** Any setting where buyers and sellers meet: a village square, a stock exchange, a website.

**How it works:**

*Specialization.* A person who repeats one task gets better at it, and tools can be built for it. Specialization multiplies output but makes everyone dependent on trade. A modern human makes almost nothing they consume; a single phone involves thousands of people in dozens of countries, none of whom knows every step.

*Supply and demand.* The core model of a market:
- **Demand:** at lower prices, buyers generally want more of a thing.
- **Supply:** at higher prices, sellers generally offer more.
- The price tends toward the level where the amount wanted equals the amount offered (the "equilibrium").

*Prices as signals.* When frost destroys part of a coffee harvest, the price of coffee rises. With no central instruction, buyers use less and growers elsewhere plant more. The price carries compressed information about scarcity to millions of people who never learn the cause. This is the main reason most economists think markets coordinate complex economies better than central planners do [Established as a description of how prices work; how far the conclusion extends is Contested].

*Competition and monopoly.* Competing sellers are pushed to cut prices and improve quality. A dominant seller can charge more and produce less.

| Market structure | Number of sellers | Example | Power to set prices |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect competition (an idealized model) | Very many, identical products | Bulk wheat, roughly | None |
| Monopolistic competition | Many, slightly different products | Restaurants, clothing | Some |
| Oligopoly | A few large firms | Aircraft makers, mobile networks | Substantial |
| Monopoly | One | Local water utility; a patented drug | High (often regulated) |

*Market failures.* Economists agree that unregulated markets reliably go wrong in certain situations:
- **Externalities:** costs or benefits falling on people outside the trade. Pollution is the standard case (the World Health Organization attributes roughly 7 million deaths a year to air pollution [Refining]); carbon dioxide emissions (16.1) are the largest.
- **Public goods:** things hard to charge for that one person's use does not use up, such as national defense or basic research. Markets under-supply them.
- **Information gaps:** one side knows much more (a used-car seller).
- **Market power:** monopolies and cartels (sellers who agree not to compete).
- **Common-pool resources:** fisheries and groundwater, overused when no one controls access.

**How it developed:** Trade is older than writing: stone and shells moved hundreds of kilometers tens of thousands of years ago [Refining]. Markets existed in Mesopotamia, Egypt, India, China, and Mesoamerica. Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) argued that self-interested trade, guided by competition, produces broad prosperity, as if by an "invisible hand." The supply-and-demand model was formalized in the 1870s–1890s. In the 20th century, roughly a third of humanity lived under **central planning**, where the government set production targets and prices instead of markets (the Soviet Union from ~1928 to 1991; China before reforms began in 1978). Nearly every country is now a **mixed economy**: mostly markets, with substantial government rules and spending.

**Effects on human life and society:** Specialization and trade underlie the rise in living standards described in 5.4. Central planning delivered rapid heavy industry in some cases but chronic shortages of consumer goods, and policy failures contributed to famines in the Soviet Union (1932–33) and China (1959–61, roughly 15–45 million deaths; estimates vary widely) [Refining as to tolls]. Market failures such as pollution and overfishing impose large, measurable costs.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Free-market view** (Friedrich Hayek, Milton Friedman): markets usually self-correct, and "government failure" (captured, slow, or uninformed regulators) is often worse than market failure.
- **Mainstream mixed-economy view:** markets are powerful but need rules: taxes on externalities, competition (antitrust) law, public provision of public goods. Most academic economists hold some version of this.
- **Socialist and Marxist critique:** markets produce exploitation, instability, and inequality; production should serve need, not profit. Evidence: comprehensive central planning performed poorly on consumer welfare and innovation; cooperatives and partial public ownership function at smaller scale (see 9.4).
- **Commons governance:** Elinor Ostrom showed many communities manage shared resources well through their own rules, without state or private ownership.

**Key works and figures:**
- Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776): specialization, trade, the "invisible hand."
- Arthur Pigou, *The Economics of Welfare* (1920): taxing externalities.
- Friedrich Hayek, "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945): prices as information.
- George Akerlof, "The Market for 'Lemons'" (1970): how information gaps break markets.
- Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990): community management of shared resources.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: voluntary trade usually benefits both parties; prices respond to scarcity; externalities and monopolies cause markets to misfire.
- Debated: how often government intervention fixes market failures versus making things worse [Contested]; how to regulate digital platforms where one firm tends to dominate [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: whether market outcomes are *fair*; whether some things (organs, votes, sex, water) should ever be sold [Belief/Value].

**Open questions:** How to price carbon in practice; whether data-driven platforms need new competition rules; how AI will change market structure.

### 5.2 Money, Banking, and Finance

**In one sentence:** Money is a token that a whole society agrees to accept in exchange for almost anything, and banks and financial markets are the systems that store it, lend it, and move it between people and across time.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Barter's problem.** Without money, a fisher who wants shoes must find a shoemaker who wants fish at that moment. Money removes this need for a "double coincidence of wants."
- **Money's three jobs:** a *medium of exchange* (accepted in payment), a *unit of account* (prices are written in it), and a *store of value* (it can be saved).
- **Why money is valuable.** Modern money has almost no value as an object. A note or a number in a bank account is valuable because everyone expects everyone else to accept it, because the government declares it legal for paying debts, and because taxes must be paid in it. It is a clear example of a shared agreement with real effects (see the Preface). Such money is called **fiat money** ("by decree").
- **Currency.** Most states issue their own money (about 150–180 currencies exist). The US dollar dominates: it makes up roughly 55–60% of the money that governments hold in reserve and is on one side of nearly 90% of all currency trades [Established, figures approximate].
- **Debt, credit, and interest.** A *debt* is a promise to pay later. *Credit* is the ability to borrow. *Interest* is the price of borrowing, usually stated as a percentage of the amount per year.

**How it works:**

*Most money is not physical.* In rich economies, notes and coins are only around a tenth of the money supply. The rest is bank deposits: entries in banks' computer records.

*Banks create money when they lend.* When a bank grants a loan, it adds the amount to the borrower's account. That new deposit is new money. Most money in modern economies is created this way. Banks keep only a fraction of deposits as cash-like reserves, so if too many depositors ask for their money at once (a **bank run**), a healthy bank can fail. Governments respond with **deposit insurance**, capital rules (international standards known as the Basel Accords), and inspections.

*Central banks.* A **central bank** is a government-linked institution that issues the currency, acts as the bank for other banks, lends to them in a panic ("lender of last resort"), and sets short-term interest rates. Most aim to keep **inflation**, the general rise in prices, near 2% a year. Raising interest rates makes borrowing more expensive, slowing spending and inflation; cutting rates does the reverse. Important examples: the US Federal Reserve (1913), the European Central Bank (1998), the People's Bank of China, the Bank of Japan.

*Financial markets* channel savings into investment.

| Instrument | What it is | Rough global size (mid-2020s) |
|---|---|---|
| Bank loan | Money lent by a bank, repaid with interest | Largest source of credit in most countries |
| Bond | A tradable loan to a government or company paying fixed interest | ~$130–140 trillion outstanding |
| Stock (share) | Part-ownership of a company, with a claim on its profits | ~$100–120 trillion total market value |
| Derivative | A contract whose value depends on another asset (e.g., an option to buy oil at a set price) | Very large on paper; hard to summarize |
| Cryptocurrency | Digital tokens recorded on a shared public ledger ("blockchain") | Swung between ~$1 trillion and ~$4 trillion |

*Cryptocurrencies.* Bitcoin (launched 2009, designed by the pseudonymous "Satoshi Nakamoto") was the first. It runs without a central bank. Its uses are mainly speculation, with some use for cross-border payments, avoiding capital controls, and crime [Refining]. "Stablecoins," tokens tied to the dollar, grew to a few hundred billion dollars by the mid-2020s. Several central banks are testing their own digital currencies (China's e-CNY is the largest pilot).

**How it developed:**
- **~3000 BCE:** Mesopotamian temples and palaces recorded debts on clay tablets, measured in silver and barley. Credit records likely came before coins [Refining].
- Many societies used **commodity money**: cowrie shells, grain, salt, cloth, silver.
- **~600 BCE:** first coins in Lydia (in modern Turkey); coins emerged separately in China and India.
- **~1000s CE:** paper money in Song-dynasty China.
- **1300s–1600s:** Italian, then Dutch and English banking; double-entry bookkeeping. The Bank of England was founded in 1694.
- **~1870s–1914:** the **gold standard**: currencies exchangeable for fixed amounts of gold. Largely abandoned in the 1930s.
- **1944–1971:** the **Bretton Woods** system tied currencies to the dollar and the dollar to gold. The US ended gold convertibility in 1971; since then major currencies have been fiat and "float" against each other.
- **1990s onward:** most central banks become legally independent of politicians and adopt inflation targets (New Zealand first, 1990). Mobile money spreads (Kenya's M-Pesa, 2007).

**Effects on human life and society:** Credit lets humans buy homes (with loans called *mortgages*), start businesses, and smooth spending across life. About 1.3–1.4 billion adults have no bank or mobile-money account, mostly in poorer countries [Established, approximate]. Inflation surged in 2021–23 after the COVID-19 pandemic and the Russia–Ukraine war (peaks near 9% in the US and over 10% in the eurozone and UK), reducing real wages for millions. Financial crises can wipe out years of growth (see 5.8).

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Mainstream (New Keynesian) view:** central banks should adjust interest rates to stabilize inflation and employment. Dominant in central banks since the 1990s.
- **Monetarism** (Milton Friedman): inflation is "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon," caused by too much money. Partly absorbed into the mainstream; strict money-growth rules were dropped in the 1980s when the link proved unstable.
- **Austrian school and gold advocates:** central banks and fiat money cause booms, busts, and debasement; money should be tied to gold or left to private competition. A minority view among economists [Contested].
- **Modern Monetary Theory (MMT):** a government that issues its own currency cannot run out of it; the real limit is inflation. Most economists consider it misleading in practice [Contested].
- **Crypto advocates vs. skeptics:** supporters see a censorship-resistant alternative to state money; skeptics see a volatile speculative asset with few real uses [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- Walter Bagehot, *Lombard Street* (1873): the "lender of last resort" doctrine.
- John Maynard Keynes, *The General Theory* (1936): money, interest, and unemployment.
- Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz, *A Monetary History of the United States* (1963): blamed the Great Depression's depth on monetary collapse.
- Satoshi Nakamoto, "Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System" (2008).
- David Graeber, *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011): anthropological evidence that credit preceded coins.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: modern money is created mostly by bank lending; central banks influence inflation through interest rates; bank runs and deposit insurance work as described.
- Debated: how much central banks should respond to asset prices and financial risk; how large government debt can safely grow (see 5.6); crypto's long-run role [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: whether money should be controlled by states at all; whether charging interest is ethical (forbidden under Islamic law, and historically restricted by the Christian church) [Belief/Value].

**Open questions:** Whether digital central-bank currencies will replace cash; whether the dollar's dominance will last; how to prevent the next financial panic.

**See also:** 5.6 Government and the Economy; 5.8 Economic Crises; 6.1 The State; 7.4 Property, Contracts, and Commercial Law; 10.7 Religion's Effects on Society.

### 5.3 Labor, Capital, and Firms

**In one sentence:** Most humans get what they need by selling their time and effort (labor) to organizations (firms) that combine it with tools and money (capital) to make things others will buy.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Labor and wages.** *Labor* is human work used in production. A *wage* (or *salary*) is the money paid for it, usually per hour, month, or year. Human adults typically work 35–50 hours a week for several decades.
- **Capital** has three meanings: *physical capital* (machines, buildings, roads), *financial capital* (money available to invest), and *human capital* (skills and knowledge). **Investment** means spending now to build capital that produces more later. Worldwide, roughly a quarter of yearly output goes into investment.
- **Firm.** An organization that produces goods or services for sale. Its **profit** is revenue minus costs.
- **Corporation.** A firm that is a separate *legal person* (see 7.4): it can own property, sign contracts, and be sued. Its owners, the **shareholders**, have **limited liability**: if it fails, they lose only what they invested. Shareholders elect a board, which hires managers.
- **Entrepreneur.** Someone who starts a new firm, bearing the risk that it fails.

**How it works:**

*Where humans work.* The global workforce is about 3.6 billion people.

| Sector | Share of world workers (approx.) | Share in rich countries |
|---|---|---|
| Agriculture | ~25% | under 5% |
| Industry (manufacturing, mining, construction) | ~23% | ~20–25% |
| Services (trade, health, education, finance, etc.) | ~50% | ~70–80% |

Only about half of workers worldwide are wage employees; the rest are self-employed, small farmers, or unpaid family workers. About 60% work in the **informal economy**, without contracts, taxes, or legal protections. Roughly 140–160 million children do work classed as child labor, and an estimated ~28 million people are in forced labor (2021) [Established, approximate].

*How wages are set.* In the standard model, a wage tends toward the value of what one more worker adds to output (*marginal productivity*). Wages are also shaped by bargaining power, laws, discrimination, and how many employers compete for workers. When one or a few employers dominate a local job market (**monopsony**), they can pay less than workers produce.

*Unions* are organizations of workers that bargain collectively with employers over pay and conditions. Membership varies widely: roughly half or more of workers in Nordic countries, about 10% in the United States, and around 15% on average in rich countries, having declined since the 1970s.

*Firms* range from one-person shops to corporations with over a million employees and stock-market values of several trillion dollars. Other forms include partnerships, **cooperatives** (owned by workers or customers), and **state-owned enterprises** (owned by governments; large in China, energy, and transport).

*Why firms exist.* Ronald Coase (1937) asked why, if markets coordinate so well, so much activity happens inside organizations run by command. His answer: using markets has costs (searching, negotiating, enforcing contracts), and firms reduce those costs for many tasks.

**How it developed:** For most of history, most humans were farmers working family land, or were bound laborers: enslaved people, serfs, or indentured servants. Slavery existed in most ancient civilizations; the Atlantic slave trade forcibly moved about 12.5 million Africans between the 1500s and 1800s (see 4.5). The **joint-stock company**, selling shares to many investors, grew in the 1600s; the Dutch East India Company (1602) was among the first with publicly traded shares. The Industrial Revolution (from ~1760, Britain) moved workers into factories, initially with 60–80-hour weeks and child labor. Unions, labor laws, and the International Labour Organization (1919) followed. Hours in rich countries fell from ~3,000 per year in the late 1800s to roughly 1,400–1,800 today. Since the 1970s, manufacturing jobs in rich countries have shifted to services and to Asia.

**Effects on human life and society:** Work determines most humans' income, status, daily schedule, and often identity. Mechanization cut the US farm workforce from most workers in the early 1800s to under 2% today, while total employment grew. Women's participation in paid work rose sharply in the 20th century but remains lower than men's worldwide (roughly 50% vs. 70% of working-age adults).

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Marxist view:** workers produce value but owners of capital keep part of it as profit ("surplus value"), so capitalism is inherently exploitative and conflictual (see 9.4).
- **Neoclassical view:** in competitive markets, workers and capital are each paid roughly what they contribute; profit rewards risk and organization.
- **Institutional view:** pay depends heavily on laws, norms, and bargaining power, not only productivity.
- **Shareholder vs. stakeholder firms:** should corporations serve only owners' profits (Friedman, 1970) or also workers, customers, and communities? [Belief/Value, with Contested empirical claims]

*Contested policy: the minimum wage.* About 90% of countries set a legal minimum wage.
- *Case against high minimums:* the basic supply-and-demand model predicts that raising the price of labor above its market level reduces hiring, hurting the least-skilled workers most.
- *Case for:* where employers have wage-setting power (monopsony), a minimum can raise pay without cutting jobs, and higher pay can reduce turnover.
- *Evidence:* since Card and Krueger's 1994 study of fast-food jobs in New Jersey, hundreds of studies have found that moderate minimum wages (up to roughly half to 60% of a country's typical wage) have small or hard-to-detect effects on employment, while raising pay for low earners [Refining]. Some studies find reduced hours or hiring, especially for teenagers; the effects of very large increases are less known [Contested].

*Contested: automation and AI.* Historically, technology destroyed specific jobs while total employment grew. Whether AI will follow that pattern, and how fast, is unknown. Estimates of jobs "at risk" range from under 15% to nearly half, depending on method [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- Adam Smith (1776): the pin factory and the division of labor.
- Karl Marx, *Das Kapital* (1867): labor, capital, and exploitation.
- Ronald Coase, "The Nature of the Firm" (1937): why firms exist.
- Joseph Schumpeter, *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy* (1942): entrepreneurs and "creative destruction."
- Gary Becker, *Human Capital* (1964): education as investment.
- David Card and Alan Krueger, *Myth and Measurement* (1995): minimum-wage evidence.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: education raises average earnings; union membership has declined in most rich countries; mechanization shifted work from farms to factories to services.
- Debated: minimum-wage effects at high levels; why wages for less-educated workers stagnated in some rich countries; AI's effects on jobs [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: whether profit is fair reward or exploitation; what firms owe society [Belief/Value].

**Open questions:** Whether AI will raise or lower demand for human labor; why productivity growth in rich countries slowed after the mid-2000s; how to protect informal and gig workers.

**See also:** 5.5 Inequality; 7.4 Property, Contracts, and Commercial Law; 8.4 Social Class and Mobility; 9.4 Socialism and Communism; 12.6 Computing, the Internet, and Artificial Intelligence; 16.5 Artificial Intelligence.

### 5.4 Growth, Development, and Poverty

**In one sentence:** For most of history nearly all humans were poor; in the last ~200 years some societies became vastly richer, others are catching up, and explaining the difference is one of economics' central questions.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **GDP (Gross Domestic Product).** The total market value of all final goods and services produced in a territory in a year. World GDP is roughly $110–120 trillion. **GDP per capita** divides this by population: an average, not what a typical person earns.
- **PPP (purchasing power parity).** An adjustment for the fact that the same money buys more in poorer countries (a haircut costs far less in India than in Switzerland). PPP figures compare real living standards better.
- **Economic growth** is the yearly percentage rise in real (inflation-adjusted) GDP. Small rates compound: 2% a year doubles income in about 35 years; 7% a year doubles it in about 10.
- **Extreme poverty.** The World Bank's global line is about $3 a day per person (adjusted for prices, revised in 2025). Below it, people struggle to afford enough food.
- **What GDP misses:** unpaid household work, environmental damage, leisure, and how income is shared. The **Human Development Index** (1990) adds health and education.

**How it works:** Growth comes from more inputs (workers, capital) and, above all over the long run, from **productivity**: getting more output from the same inputs through better technology, skills, and organization. Robert Solow's 1956 model showed that piling up machines alone eventually runs into diminishing returns; sustained growth requires new ideas.

| Country income group (World Bank, approx.) | Income per person per year | Share of world population |
|---|---|---|
| High-income | above ~$14,000 | ~15% |
| Upper-middle-income | ~$4,500–14,000 | ~35% |
| Lower-middle-income | ~$1,100–4,500 | ~40% |
| Low-income | below ~$1,100 | ~9% |

**How it developed:**
- **Before ~1800:** most humans lived near subsistence everywhere. The richest regions were perhaps 2–3 times richer than the poorest [Refining].
- **The "Great Divergence" (~1800–1950):** industrialization in Britain, then Western Europe, North America, and Japan raised incomes there many times over, while much of Asia, Africa, and Latin America stagnated, often under colonial rule. World income per person has risen more than tenfold since 1820.
- **Catch-up (1950–today):** South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, and Hong Kong grew 6–10% a year for decades. South Korea went from roughly the income of poor African countries around 1960 to that of Western Europe. China, after market reforms from 1978, grew near 10% a year for three decades. India accelerated after reforms in 1991.

**Effects on human life and society:** In 1820 most humans lived in extreme poverty. In 1990, about 2 billion people (~38%) did. In the mid-2020s, roughly 800 million (~10%) do, about two-thirds of them in sub-Saharan Africa [Established, approximate; exact figures depend on the poverty line]. China alone accounts for about 800 million people leaving extreme poverty since 1980. Progress slowed after 2015 and briefly reversed during COVID-19 in 2020. Growth correlates strongly with longer lives, lower child mortality, and more schooling.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** Why are some countries rich? Every theory below has evidence; their relative weight is [Contested].
- **Institutions:** secure property rights, rule of law, and inclusive politics encourage investment (Douglass North; Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson, Nobel Prize 2024).
- **Geography:** climate, tropical disease, and access to the sea shape development (Jeffrey Sachs, Jared Diamond).
- **Culture and ideas:** attitudes toward work, trust, and innovation (Max Weber; Deirdre McCloskey).
- **Colonial extraction and dependency:** colonial powers extracted wealth and built institutions designed for extraction, leaving damaging legacies (Walter Rodney; dependency theorists). Kenneth Pomeranz argued Britain's lead owed much to coal and colonies.
- **The developmental state:** East Asia grew with heavy state guidance of investment and exports, not pure free markets (see 5.7).

*Contested policy: foreign aid.* Rich-country governments give about $200–225 billion a year in official aid (less after major donors made cuts in 2024–25). Migrants send home far more, roughly $650–700 billion a year in **remittances**.
- *Case for (Jeffrey Sachs):* targeted aid can break "poverty traps."
- *Case against (William Easterly, Dambisa Moyo):* top-down aid often fails, feeds corruption, and weakens local accountability.
- *Evidence:* health aid has strong documented successes (smallpox eradication, vaccination, malaria nets, HIV treatment saving many millions of lives) [Established]. The effect of aid on overall national growth is small or unclear [Contested]. Since the 2000s, randomized trials have tested specific programs; direct cash transfers to poor households often show lasting gains [Refining].

**Key works and figures:**
- Simon Kuznets: developed national income accounts (1930s), the basis of GDP.
- Robert Solow, "A Contribution to the Theory of Economic Growth" (1956).
- Amartya Sen, *Development as Freedom* (1999): development as expanding capabilities, not just income.
- Kenneth Pomeranz, *The Great Divergence* (2000).
- Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson, *Why Nations Fail* (2012).
- Abhijit Banerjee and Esther Duflo, *Poor Economics* (2011): experiments in development (Nobel Prize 2019, with Michael Kremer).
- Angus Maddison: long-run historical income estimates.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: sustained growth began ~200 years ago; extreme poverty has fallen sharply since 1990; East Asian economies converged rapidly.
- Debated: the main causes of the Great Divergence; whether poorest countries can repeat East Asia's export-led path; aid's growth effects [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: whether GDP growth should be the goal at all, given environmental limits ("degrowth" advocates vs. "green growth") [Belief/Value, with Contested empirical claims].

**Open questions:** Why many African economies have grown slowly despite reforms; whether growth can continue while cutting emissions; why productivity growth slowed in rich countries.

**See also:** 4.5 Colonialism, Revolutions, and Industry; 5.5 Inequality; 5.7 International Trade and the Global Economy; 12.1 Tools and How Technology Changes; 16.1 Climate Change; 16.7 Resources: Food, Water, and Materials.

### 5.5 Inequality

**In one sentence:** Humans differ enormously in how much income and wealth they have, both between countries and within them, and the causes, consequences, and fairness of these gaps are among the most argued-over questions in human politics.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Income** is a flow: money received over a period (wages, profits, rent, interest, government payments).
- **Wealth** is a stock: the value of everything a person owns (houses, land, savings, shares, businesses) minus their debts. Wealth is always far more unequal than income, because savings and inheritances accumulate across lifetimes and generations.
- **Pre-tax vs. post-tax.** Taxes and government payments (see 5.6) narrow gaps, so inequality measured "after taxes and transfers" is lower.
- **The Gini coefficient** summarizes inequality as a number from 0 (everyone has the same) to 1 (one person has everything). Another common measure is the share of income or wealth held by the top 1% or 10%.

**How it works:**

*Between countries.* Where a human is born is the single biggest predictor of their income. Average income per person in the richest countries is more than 50 times that in the poorest. A worker doing the same job can earn many times more in one country than another; borders and migration restrictions keep these gaps from closing.

*Worldwide, between all individuals* (2020s estimates, approximate [Refining]):
- The richest 10% of humans receive about half of all income and own about three-quarters of all wealth.
- The poorest half receive under 10% of income and own about 2% of wealth.
- Roughly 3,000 billionaires hold combined wealth of around $14–16 trillion.

*Within countries.*

| Country (approx. Gini, disposable income) | Gini |
|---|---|
| South Africa | ~0.63 (among the highest measured) |
| Brazil | ~0.52 |
| United States | ~0.40 |
| China | ~0.36–0.38 |
| India | ~0.33–0.35 (consumption-based; likely understates) |
| Germany | ~0.30 |
| Sweden, Norway | ~0.27–0.29 |
| Slovakia, Slovenia | ~0.24 (among the lowest) |

*Mobility.* Countries with higher inequality tend to have lower *social mobility*: children's incomes more closely track their parents' (the "Great Gatsby curve") [Refining as correlation; causation Contested].

**How it developed:** Hunter-gatherer groups were relatively equal in material terms, though not in all respects [Refining]. Farming allowed stored surplus, land ownership, and inheritance, and with them large, durable hierarchies of kings, nobles, enslaved people, and peasants (see 4.2). Before industrialization, gaps *between* world regions were modest; gaps *within* societies were often extreme. Industrialization created the Great Divergence between countries (5.4). In rich countries, inequality fell sharply from ~1914 to the 1970s (wars, depression, high taxes, unions, welfare states), then rose in many from about 1980. In the US, the top 1%'s share of pre-tax income roughly doubled, from ~10% in the 1970s to ~20% [Refining; the size of the rise after taxes and transfers is Contested]. Rises also occurred in China, India, and Russia after their market reforms. Latin America saw inequality fall in the 2000s. Meanwhile, inequality **between all humans worldwide** has *fallen* since ~1990, because populous Asian countries grew fast (a global Gini of very roughly 0.7 falling toward 0.6) [Refining].

**Effects on human life and society:** In some countries, such as the US, the richest 1% live roughly 10–15 years longer than the poorest 1%; the gap is smaller elsewhere [Refining]. Inequality correlates with lower trust, worse health, and more crime across countries, but whether inequality itself *causes* these is disputed [Contested]. Concentrated wealth can translate into political influence through funding parties, lobbying, and media ownership [Refining].

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** *Contested policy: what causes rising inequality?* Several explanations each have evidence; their relative importance is [Contested]:
- **Technology:** computers and automation raised demand for highly educated workers faster than schools supplied them (Claudia Goldin and Lawrence Katz). Evidence: the pay premium for university graduates rose sharply in many rich countries after 1980.
- **Globalization:** imports from low-wage countries reduced pay and jobs for less-educated workers in rich countries, while raising incomes in Asia (see 5.7).
- **Institutions and policy:** union decline, lower top tax rates (the top US income tax rate was over 90% in the 1950s and is under 40% today), deregulation of finance, and lower minimum wages. Evidence: countries with similar technology and trade exposure saw very different trends.
- **Capital vs. labor:** Thomas Piketty argues that when the return on wealth exceeds economic growth ("r > g"), wealth concentrates over time. Critics dispute his data and mechanism.
- **Market power:** a few "superstar" firms capture large profits in winner-take-all markets.

*Does inequality matter in itself?*
- *View that it does:* large gaps harm health, mobility, social cohesion, and democracy, and are unfair [partly Belief/Value, partly Contested empirics].
- *View that poverty, not inequality, is the problem:* if the poor are getting richer, gaps reflect differences in effort, skill, and risk-taking, and attempts to equalize reduce the incentives that drive growth [partly Belief/Value, partly Contested empirics].
- *Historical view (Walter Scheidel):* large, rapid declines in inequality have historically come mainly through mass war, revolution, state collapse, and plague.

**Key works and figures:**
- Simon Kuznets (1955): proposed that inequality first rises then falls as countries develop (the "Kuznets curve"); later evidence is mixed.
- John Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* (1971): influential argument for when inequality is just.
- Claudia Goldin and Lawrence Katz, *The Race between Education and Technology* (2008).
- Branko Milanovic, *Global Inequality* (2016): global distribution and the "elephant curve."
- Thomas Piketty, *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2013): long-run wealth concentration.
- Walter Scheidel, *The Great Leveler* (2017).

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: wealth is more unequal than income; between-country gaps are very large; inequality within many countries rose after 1980 while global inequality among individuals fell.
- Debated: the main causes; how much taxes and transfers offset the rise; whether inequality itself harms growth [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: how much inequality is fair, and whether equality of outcomes or of opportunity matters more [Belief/Value].

**Open questions:** Whether AI will widen or narrow gaps; how to measure hidden wealth (an estimated ~8–10% of world household financial wealth is held offshore [Refining]); whether between-country convergence will continue in Africa.

**See also:** 5.4 Growth, Development, and Poverty; 5.6 Government and the Economy; 8.4 Social Class and Mobility; 9.4 Socialism and Communism; 11.4 Political Philosophy.

### 5.6 Government and the Economy

**In one sentence:** Governments take a large share of what their economies produce through taxes, spend it on services and transfers, write the rules markets run on, and try to steer the economy away from slumps and inflation.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Government** here means the state's ruling institutions (see 6.1), which can make binding rules and use force to collect taxes.
- **Tax:** a compulsory payment to government. Main types: on income, on consumption (sales tax or value-added tax, used in about 170 countries), on company profits, on property, and on imports (tariffs).
- **Public spending:** on services (schools, roads, police, armies, health care) and on **transfers** (payments to people, such as pensions for the old).
- **Budget deficit:** spending more than tax revenue in a year, covered by borrowing. **Public debt** is the accumulated total owed.
- **Fiscal policy** uses taxes and spending to influence the economy. **Monetary policy** uses interest rates and money creation, run by central banks (5.2).

**How it works:**

*Scale.* Government spending (all levels) is roughly 40–57% of GDP in Western Europe, ~35–40% in the US and Japan, and ~10–25% in many lower-income countries, which often struggle to collect taxes from large informal economies. Rich countries collect about a third of GDP in taxes on average; low-income countries about 10–15%.

*Welfare states* are systems of government programs that insure people against old age, illness, unemployment, disability, and poverty. Rich countries spend roughly 20% of GDP on them on average.

| Model (after Gøsta Esping-Andersen) | Examples | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Social democratic | Sweden, Denmark, Norway | Universal benefits, high taxes, strong public services |
| Conservative/Continental | Germany, France | Social insurance tied to employment; family-based roles |
| Liberal | US, UK, Australia | Means-tested benefits (for the poor), larger private role |

*Regulation* sets rules for safety, the environment, finance, labor, and competition (antitrust law breaks up or blocks monopolies).

*Steering the economy.* In a slump, governments can cut taxes or raise spending to boost demand; central banks can cut rates. After 2008, and again in 2020, central banks also bought government bonds and other assets in large amounts (**quantitative easing**), totaling many trillions of dollars.

*Public debt.* Global public debt is roughly $100 trillion, near world GDP [Established, approximate]. Japan's is about 230–250% of its GDP, Italy's ~135%, the US's ~120%, Germany's ~60–65%. Countries borrowing in their own currency rarely default outright; those borrowing in foreign currency are more vulnerable (see 5.8).

**How it developed:** Early states taxed harvests and trade mainly to pay for armies and rulers. **Mercantilism** (1500s–1700s) used state power to maximize exports and gold. In rich countries around 1900, government spending was only ~10% of GDP. Germany introduced state social insurance in the 1880s; the US created Social Security in 1935; Britain's Beveridge Report (1942) designed a comprehensive welfare state. Keynesian demand management dominated from 1945 to the 1970s. The 1970s "stagflation" (high inflation plus unemployment) brought a turn toward markets, the **neoliberal** era: lower top taxes, privatization (selling state firms), and deregulation, led by Margaret Thatcher (UK, 1979) and Ronald Reagan (US, 1981). The 2008 crisis and COVID-19 brought very large interventions again; governments worldwide committed well over $10 trillion in pandemic support in 2020–21.

**Effects on human life and society:** Taxes and transfers substantially reduce poverty and inequality in rich countries; pensions largely ended mass poverty among the old there. Public schooling and health systems account for much of the rise in literacy and life expectancy (see 8.7, 12.7).

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Keynesian:** markets can get stuck in slumps with high unemployment; government should spend more in downturns.
- **Monetarist/New Classical:** fiscal fine-tuning is clumsy; stable rules and low inflation matter most.
- **Public choice** (James Buchanan): politicians and bureaucrats pursue their own interests, so government fails too.
- **Supply-side:** lower taxes boost work and investment; at very high rates, cutting taxes can even raise revenue (the Laffer curve). Most economists accept that such a point exists but think most rich countries are below it [Contested].
- **Developmental/entrepreneurial state:** governments can lead innovation and industry (Mariana Mazzucato; East Asia).

*Contested policy: austerity vs. stimulus.* After a crisis, should governments cut deficits (**austerity**) or spend more (**stimulus**)?
- *Case for austerity:* high debt raises interest costs, risks a loss of lenders' confidence, and may slow growth; credible cuts can calm markets. Alberto Alesina documented cases (e.g., Ireland and Denmark in the 1980s) where cuts accompanied growth.
- *Case for stimulus:* in deep slumps with interest rates near zero, each dollar of government spending raises output by more than a dollar, so cutting deepens recessions and can even raise debt relative to GDP.
- *Evidence:* an IMF study (Blanchard and Leigh, 2013) found that forecasters had underestimated how much austerity in Europe after 2010 would shrink economies [Refining]. "Expansionary austerity" appears rare and usually relied on currency devaluation or falling interest rates [Contested]. A widely cited claim that growth drops sharply once debt passes 90% of GDP (Reinhart and Rogoff, 2010) was weakened when a spreadsheet error was found in 2013; high debt does correlate with slower growth, but which causes which is unclear [Contested]. Large stimulus in 2020–21 sped recovery but contributed to the 2021–23 inflation [Refining as to size].

**Key works and figures:**
- John Maynard Keynes, *The General Theory* (1936).
- William Beveridge, *Social Insurance and Allied Services* (1942).
- Friedrich Hayek, *The Road to Serfdom* (1944): warned that economic planning leads toward tyranny.
- Paul Samuelson, "The Pure Theory of Public Expenditure" (1954): defined public goods.
- Milton Friedman, *Capitalism and Freedom* (1962).
- James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock, *The Calculus of Consent* (1962).
- Gøsta Esping-Andersen, *The Three Worlds of Welfare Capitalism* (1990).

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: all rich countries have large governments and welfare states; taxes and transfers reduce measured poverty; central banks can reduce inflation by raising rates, at the cost of slower growth.
- Debated: the right size of government; the size of fiscal multipliers; the safe level of public debt; how much taxes discourage work [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: whether taxation is legitimate collective provision or a restriction on liberty; what citizens owe each other [Belief/Value].

**Open questions:** How aging populations will pay for pensions and health care (see 16.6); how to tax mobile capital and digital firms (about 140 countries agreed in 2021 on a 15% global minimum corporate tax; implementation is incomplete); how much public debt is too much.

**See also:** 5.2 Money, Banking, and Finance; 5.5 Inequality; 6.1 The State; 9.2 Liberalism; 9.4 Socialism and Communism; 9.5 Capitalism and Free-Market Thought; 16.6 Demographic Change and Aging.

### 5.7 International Trade and the Global Economy

**In one sentence:** Humans trade across the borders of their states on a vast scale, which has made goods cheaper and helped poor countries industrialize, but has also produced concentrated losers and recurring political fights over tariffs and dependence.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Exports** are goods and services sold to other countries; **imports** are those bought from them.
- **Tariff:** a tax on imports. A **quota** limits the quantity imported. **Protectionism** is the use of such barriers to shield domestic producers.
- **Exchange rate:** the price of one currency in another. Some float freely; others are fixed ("pegged"). About 20 European countries share a single currency, the euro (launched 1999).
- **Comparative advantage** (the key idea): a country gains by specializing in what it produces at the *lowest relative cost* (what it gives up least to make), even if another country is better at making *everything*. David Ricardo's 1817 example: Portugal could make both wine and cloth more efficiently than England, yet both gained if Portugal specialized in wine and England in cloth, then traded. The analogy is a skilled surgeon who is also a fast typist: she still gains by hiring a typist and spending her time on surgery.

**How it works:**

*Scale.* Cross-border exports of goods and services total roughly $30–33 trillion a year, close to a third of world output. Around 80% of goods by volume travel by sea, mostly in standardized steel shipping containers (introduced 1956). Currency trading averages roughly $7–10 trillion *per day*.

*Supply chains.* Many products are made in stages across countries: a car or phone may contain parts that cross borders many times. About half of world trade is part of such global value chains [Refining]. This makes production cheaper but transmits shocks, as during COVID-19 shortages in 2020–22.

*The main institutions:*

| Institution | Founded | Members | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| World Trade Organization (WTO) | 1995 (successor to GATT, 1947) | ~166 | Sets trade rules, hosts negotiations, settles disputes. Its appeals body has been inoperative since 2019 because the US blocked appointments. |
| International Monetary Fund (IMF) | 1944 | ~191 | Monitors economies; lends to countries in currency or debt crises, usually with policy conditions. |
| World Bank | 1944 | ~189 | Long-term loans and grants for development. |
| Regional blocs | various | — | E.g., the European Union's single market (27 states), the African Continental Free Trade Area (2018), the Asia-Pacific RCEP (2020). |

**How it developed:**
- **Ancient–1500s:** long-distance trade in luxuries (silk, spices, precious metals) along the Silk Road, Indian Ocean, and trans-Saharan routes.
- **1500s–1700s: mercantilism and colonial trade**, including the Atlantic slave trade; states sought trade surpluses and gold, often by force.
- **1846:** Britain repealed its grain tariffs (the Corn Laws), a landmark of free trade.
- **~1870–1914: first globalization**, driven by steamships, telegraphs, and the gold standard.
- **1930s:** collapse. The US Smoot–Hawley tariff (1930) and retaliation contributed to world trade falling by roughly two-thirds in value between 1929 and 1933 (most of the fall reflected the Depression itself) [Refining].
- **1947–2008:** repeated rounds of tariff cuts. Average tariffs in rich countries fell from roughly 20–40% in the late 1940s to a few percent. China joined the WTO in 2001 and became the largest exporter of goods.
- **2008–today:** trade growth slowed relative to output ("slowbalization"). US–China tariff conflict began in 2018. In 2025 the US raised tariffs broadly, lifting its average tariff to the highest level in roughly 90 years (estimates in the mid-to-high teens of percent) [Refining; still changing].

**Effects on human life and society:** Export-led growth was central to the rise of Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and China, and therefore to the fall in global poverty (5.4). Consumers in importing countries pay less for many goods. Losses are concentrated: research on the "China shock" estimates that rising Chinese imports eliminated roughly 1–2 million US manufacturing jobs in the 2000s, with lasting damage to the affected towns [Refining]. Such losses fed political backlash against trade in several rich countries.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** *Contested policy: free trade vs. protection.*
- *Free-trade view:* comparative advantage means trade raises total income in all countries; tariffs are taxes paid mostly by domestic buyers and invite retaliation. Evidence: studies of the 2018–19 US tariffs found nearly all the cost was passed on to US buyers [Refining]. Most economists favor low tariffs, combined with help for displaced workers.
- *Infant-industry/developmentalist view:* young industries need temporary protection to grow. Alexander Hamilton (1791), Friedrich List (1841), and Ha-Joon Chang argue that Britain, the US, Germany, Japan, and South Korea all industrialized behind tariffs. Evidence: East Asia combined protection with strong pressure to export and succeeded; Latin American "import substitution" (1950s–1980s) built industry but often ended in inefficiency and debt crises [Contested which lessons generalize].
- *Security and resilience view:* dependence on rivals or single suppliers (e.g., advanced computer chips made mostly in Taiwan) is dangerous; some production should be kept at home. This view has spread across political camps since ~2020 [Contested how much it costs].
- *Labor and community view:* gains go to consumers and owners broadly, while losses fall on specific workers and places that are rarely compensated in practice.

**Key works and figures:**
- David Ricardo, *On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation* (1817): comparative advantage.
- Friedrich List, *The National System of Political Economy* (1841): case for protecting infant industries.
- Paul Krugman, "new trade theory" (1979 onward): trade driven by economies of scale (Nobel Prize 2008).
- Joseph Stiglitz, *Globalization and Its Discontents* (2002): critique of IMF policies.
- Ha-Joon Chang, *Kicking Away the Ladder* (2002).
- David Autor, David Dorn, and Gordon Hanson, "The China Shock" (2016).

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: comparative advantage is logically sound; trade expanded greatly after 1950; export-led growth lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty; trade's losses are concentrated in particular places.
- Debated: whether strategic protection helps developing or rich countries today; how much trade versus technology explains lost manufacturing jobs [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: how to weigh national self-sufficiency and particular communities against aggregate efficiency [Belief/Value].

**Open questions:** Whether the world is splitting into rival trade blocs; the long-run effects of the 2025 US tariffs; how climate policy (e.g., carbon border taxes) will reshape trade.

**See also:** 4.7 Globalization and the Present; 5.4 Growth, Development, and Poverty; 6.5 International Relations and Diplomacy; 12.4 Manufacturing and Materials; 12.5 Transportation.

### 5.8 Economic Crises

**In one sentence:** Human economies do not grow smoothly; they periodically lurch into slumps, panics, and collapses that can throw tens of millions out of work in months.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Business cycle:** the recurring pattern of expansion and contraction. A **recession** is a broad decline in economic activity (a common rule of thumb is two consecutive quarter-years of falling GDP). A **depression** is a very deep, long recession.
- **Unemployment:** people who want paid work but cannot find it, as a share of the workforce.
- **Bubble:** prices of an asset (houses, shares, tulip bulbs) rising far above what its underlying use would justify, sustained by expectations of further rises, until they collapse.
- **Leverage:** using borrowed money to invest. It magnifies gains and losses.
- **Default:** failing to repay a debt. **Hyperinflation:** extremely rapid price rises, conventionally over 50% per *month*.

**How it works:** Common mechanisms, often combined:
- **Credit booms and busts:** easy lending inflates asset prices; when prices fall, borrowers default, lenders cut back, and spending collapses.
- **Bank runs and panics:** fear spreads from one institution to others (*contagion*).
- **Debt deflation:** falling prices make existing debts heavier, forcing more selling and further price falls (Irving Fisher, 1933).
- **Sudden stops:** foreign lenders pull money out of a country, its currency crashes, and debts owed in foreign currency become unpayable.
- **Hyperinflation:** usually begins when a government finances large deficits by creating money amid a collapse in production or political order.
- **External shocks:** wars, pandemics, oil-price spikes.

Recessions are frequent: the US has had about a dozen since 1945. Major crises are rarer. Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff's survey of eight centuries found banking crises and government defaults recurring in almost every country.

| Crisis | Years | What happened | Scale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Great Depression | 1929–1939 | Stock crash, waves of US bank failures, collapse of trade and credit | US output fell ~25–30%, unemployment ~25%; worldwide |
| German hyperinflation | 1921–23 | Money printing to pay debts and wartime costs | Prices doubled every few days at the peak; savings wiped out |
| Latin American debt crisis | 1980s | Dollar debts became unpayable when US rates rose | A "lost decade" of growth across the region |
| Asian financial crisis | 1997–98 | Sudden stop of foreign capital | Indonesia's output fell ~13% in 1998 |
| Global financial crisis | 2007–09 | US housing bubble, risky mortgage debt, failure of Lehman Brothers (Sept 2008) | First fall in world output since World War II; US unemployment 10% |
| Eurozone debt crisis | 2010–12 | Government debt fears in countries sharing the euro | Greece lost about a quarter of its output; unemployment ~27% |
| Zimbabwe hyperinflation | 2007–09 | Money printing amid collapsing farm output | Monthly inflation in the billions of percent |
| COVID-19 recession | 2020 | Pandemic lockdowns | World output fell ~3%, the deepest drop since the 1930s; brief |

**How it developed:** Early speculative episodes include the Dutch "tulip mania" (1636–37; its severity is now disputed) and Britain's South Sea Bubble (1720). Panics recurred through the 1800s. The Great Depression transformed economics: it produced Keynesian theory, deposit insurance (US, 1933), and the postwar international institutions. A long period of relative stability in rich countries (~1985–2007, "the Great Moderation") ended with the 2008 crisis, after which banks faced stricter capital rules. Responses to 2020 were faster and larger.

**Effects on human life and society:** Crises cause job losses, home losses, business failures, and lasting income losses for those who graduate or lose jobs during them [Refining]. The US lost about 9 million jobs in 2008–10. Studies link economic crises to rises in suicide and to political upheaval; the Depression helped bring fascist movements to power in the 1930s, and the 2008 crisis is widely linked to later populist movements [Refining as to strength of the link].

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Keynesian:** slumps come from collapses in total spending; governments and central banks should replace lost demand (see 5.6).
- **Monetarist:** the Depression became severe because the US central bank let the money supply shrink by about a third in 1929–33 (Friedman and Schwartz). Now widely accepted as a major factor.
- **Financial instability (Hyman Minsky):** long calm encourages ever-riskier borrowing, so "stability breeds instability." Gained attention after 2008.
- **Austrian:** artificially cheap credit causes bad investments; busts are painful but necessary corrections, and bailouts store up problems [Contested].
- **Marxist:** crises are built into capitalism, through overproduction and falling profit rates [Contested].
- **Real business cycle:** fluctuations mainly reflect real shocks to productivity [Contested].

*Contested policy: bailouts.* Rescuing failing banks or countries limits panic, but may encourage reckless risk-taking (**moral hazard**) and is often seen as unfair to taxpayers. IMF rescue loans conditioned on spending cuts are defended as restoring stability and criticized for deepening recessions [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- Irving Fisher, "The Debt-Deflation Theory of Great Depressions" (1933).
- John Maynard Keynes, *The General Theory* (1936).
- Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz, *A Monetary History of the United States* (1963).
- Charles Kindleberger, *Manias, Panics, and Crashes* (1978).
- Hyman Minsky, *Stabilizing an Unstable Economy* (1986).
- Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff, *This Time Is Different* (2009): eight centuries of financial crises.
- Ben Bernanke, Douglas Diamond, and Philip Dybvig: research on banks and bank runs (Nobel Prize 2022).

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: crises recur across centuries and countries; credit booms often precede banking crises; central-bank and fiscal support can shorten slumps; hyperinflation follows from large-scale money creation to fund governments.
- Debated: how to predict crises; the long-term costs of bailouts; whether the post-2008 rules are adequate [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: who should bear crisis losses: shareholders, creditors, taxpayers, or workers [Belief/Value].

**Open questions:** Where the next crisis will come from (candidates include high public debt, lightly regulated "shadow banks," crypto, and climate-related losses); whether central banks can keep fighting inflation without causing crises; whether economics can forecast turning points at all.
