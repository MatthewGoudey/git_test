# Domain 5: Economics and Material Life

Economics is the study of how humans produce, divide, and use things that are in limited supply: food, shelter, energy, tools, time, and each other's labor. Almost every human spends most of their waking adult life working to obtain these things, and most political conflict is, at least in part, a dispute over who gets what. This domain explains the systems humans have built for this, starting from zero: what a trade is, what money is, why prices move, and why some groups of humans are hundreds of times richer than others. *Figures describe the mid-2020s unless otherwise stated and are given in US dollars (the currency of the United States, which humans use as the main international yardstick).*

---

### 5.1 Scarcity, Trade, and Markets

**In one sentence:** Humans want more than they can produce, so they must choose what to make and who gets it; today most of those choices are made through voluntary trade in "markets," where prices rise and fall.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Goods and services.** A *good* is a physical thing humans want (bread, a house, a phone). A *service* is useful work done for someone (a haircut, surgery, teaching).
- **Scarcity.** Land, materials, energy, and human time are limited. Human wants, in practice, are not. The gap between the two is the basic problem economics studies.
- **Opportunity cost.** Every choice gives up the next-best alternative. A field planted with wheat cannot also grow rice.
- **Trade (exchange).** Two parties voluntarily swap things. Each gives up something it values less for something it values more, so **both can end up better off**. This is the central fact of economic life: trade is not just dividing a fixed amount; it can create value for both sides.
- **Price.** How much of one thing (usually money; see 5.2) must be given up to get another.
- **Market.** Any setting where buyers and sellers meet: a village square, a stock exchange, a website.

**How it works:**

*Specialization.* A person who does one task repeatedly gets better at it, and tools can be built for that task. Specialization multiplies output, but it makes everyone dependent on trade. A typical modern human makes almost nothing they consume. A single phone involves materials and work from dozens of countries and many thousands of people, none of whom knows every step.

*Supply and demand.* The core model of a market:
- **Demand:** at lower prices, buyers generally want more of a thing.
- **Supply:** at higher prices, sellers generally offer more.
- The price tends to move toward the level where the amount wanted equals the amount offered (the "equilibrium"). In a shortage, sellers can raise prices; in a glut, they cut them.

*Prices as signals.* When frost destroys part of a coffee harvest, the price of coffee rises. With no central instruction, buyers use less and growers elsewhere plant more. The price carries compressed information about scarcity to millions of people who never learn the cause. This is the main reason most economists think markets coordinate complex economies better than central planners do [Established as a description of how prices work; how far the conclusion extends is Contested].

*Competition and monopoly.* When many sellers compete, each is pushed to cut prices and improve quality. When one seller dominates, it can charge more and produce less.

| Market structure | Number of sellers | Example | Power to set prices |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect competition (an idealized model) | Very many, identical products | Bulk wheat, roughly | None |
| Monopolistic competition | Many, slightly different products | Restaurants, clothing | Some |
| Oligopoly | A few large firms | Aircraft makers, mobile networks | Substantial |
| Monopoly | One | Local water utility; a patented drug | High (often regulated) |

*Market failures.* Economists agree that unregulated markets reliably go wrong in certain situations:
- **Externalities:** costs or benefits that fall on people outside the trade. Pollution is the standard case; carbon dioxide emissions (see 16.1) are the largest. The World Health Organization estimates air pollution causes roughly 7 million deaths a year [Refining].
- **Public goods:** things that are hard to charge for and that one person's use does not use up, such as national defense or basic research. Markets supply too little of them.
- **Information gaps:** one side knows much more than the other (a used-car seller, a person buying health insurance).
- **Market power:** monopolies and cartels (sellers who agree not to compete).
- **Common-pool resources:** fisheries and groundwater, overused when no one controls access (the "tragedy of the commons").

**How it developed:** Trade is older than writing. Stone and shells moved hundreds of kilometers between groups tens of thousands of years ago [Refining]. Organized markets existed in Mesopotamia, Egypt, India, China, and Mesoamerica, linked by long-distance routes such as the Silk Road and Indian Ocean sea lanes. Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) argued that self-interested trade, guided by competition, produces broad prosperity, as if by an "invisible hand." The supply-and-demand model was formalized in the 1870s–1890s. In the 20th century, roughly a third of humanity lived under **central planning**, where the government set production targets and prices instead of markets (the Soviet Union from ~1928 to 1991; China before reforms began in 1978). Nearly every country is now a **mixed economy**: mostly markets, with substantial government rules and spending.

**Effects on human life and society:** Specialization and trade underlie the rise in living standards described in 5.4. Central planning delivered rapid heavy industrialization in some cases but chronic shortages of consumer goods, and its worst failures contributed to famines: in the Soviet Union in 1932–33 and in China in 1959–61 (the latter killing roughly 15–45 million people; estimates vary widely) [Established that the famines occurred; their exact toll and the relative weight of policy versus other causes are Refining]. Market failures such as pollution and overfishing impose large, measurable costs.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Free-market view:** markets are usually self-correcting, and "government failure" (regulators who are captured, slow, or uninformed) is often worse than market failure. Associated with Friedrich Hayek and Milton Friedman.
- **Mainstream mixed-economy view:** markets are powerful but need rules: taxes on harmful externalities, competition (antitrust) law, and public provision of public goods. Most academic economists hold some version of this.
- **Socialist and Marxist critique:** markets produce exploitation, instability, and inequality; production should serve need rather than profit. Evidence: comprehensive central planning performed poorly on consumer welfare and innovation; worker cooperatives and partial public ownership function at smaller scale (see 9.4).
- **Commons governance:** Elinor Ostrom showed that many communities manage shared resources well through their own rules, without either the state or private ownership.

**Key works and figures:**
- Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776): specialization, trade, the "invisible hand."
- Alfred Marshall, *Principles of Economics* (1890): the standard supply-and-demand framework.
- Arthur Pigou, *The Economics of Welfare* (1920): taxing externalities.
- Friedrich Hayek, "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945): prices as information.
- George Akerlof, "The Market for 'Lemons'" (1970): how information gaps break markets.
- Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990): community management of shared resources.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: voluntary trade usually benefits both parties; prices respond to scarcity; externalities and monopolies cause markets to misfire.
- Debated: how often government intervention fixes market failures versus making things worse [Contested]; how to regulate digital platforms where one firm tends to dominate [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: whether market outcomes are *fair*; whether some things (organs, votes, sex, water) should ever be sold [Belief/Value].

**Open questions:** How to price externalities such as carbon in practice; whether data-driven platforms need new competition rules; how artificial intelligence will change market structure.

### 5.2 Money, Banking, and Finance

**In one sentence:** Money is a token that a whole society agrees to accept in exchange for almost anything, and banks and financial markets are the systems that store it, lend it, and move it between people and across time.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Barter's problem.** Without money, a fisher who wants shoes must find a shoemaker who wants fish at that moment. Money removes this need for a "double coincidence of wants."
- **Money's three jobs:** a *medium of exchange* (accepted in payment), a *unit of account* (prices are written in it), and a *store of value* (it can be saved).
- **Why money is valuable.** Modern money has almost no value as an object. A note or a number in a bank account is valuable because everyone expects everyone else to accept it, because the government declares it legal for paying debts, and because taxes must be paid in it. It is a clear example of a shared agreement with real effects (see the Preface). Such money is called **fiat money** ("by decree").
- **Currency.** Most states issue their own money (about 150–180 currencies exist). The US dollar dominates: it makes up roughly 55–60% of the money that governments hold in reserve and is on one side of nearly 90% of all currency trades [Established, figures approximate].
- **Debt, credit, and interest.** A *debt* is a promise to pay later. *Credit* is the ability to borrow. *Interest* is the price of borrowing, usually stated as a percentage of the amount per year.

**How it works:**

*Most money is not physical.* In rich economies, notes and coins are only around a tenth of the money supply. The rest is bank deposits: entries in banks' computer records.

*Banks create money when they lend.* When a bank grants a loan, it adds the amount to the borrower's account. That new deposit is new money. Most money in modern economies is created this way. Banks keep only a fraction of deposits as cash-like reserves, so if too many depositors ask for their money at once (a **bank run**), a healthy bank can fail. Governments respond with **deposit insurance**, capital rules (international standards known as the Basel Accords), and inspections.

*Central banks.* A **central bank** is a government-linked institution that issues the currency, acts as the bank for other banks, lends to them in a panic ("lender of last resort"), and sets short-term interest rates. Most aim to keep **inflation**, the general rise in prices, near 2% a year. Raising interest rates makes borrowing more expensive, slowing spending and inflation; cutting rates does the reverse. Important examples: the US Federal Reserve (1913), the European Central Bank (1998), the People's Bank of China, the Bank of Japan.

*Financial markets* channel savings into investment.

| Instrument | What it is | Rough global size (mid-2020s) |
|---|---|---|
| Bank loan | Money lent by a bank, repaid with interest | Largest source of credit in most countries |
| Bond | A tradable loan to a government or company paying fixed interest | ~$130–140 trillion outstanding |
| Stock (share) | Part-ownership of a company, with a claim on its profits | ~$100–120 trillion total market value |
| Derivative | A contract whose value depends on another asset (e.g., an option to buy oil at a set price) | Very large on paper; hard to summarize |
| Cryptocurrency | Digital tokens recorded on a shared public ledger ("blockchain") | Swung between ~$1 trillion and ~$4 trillion |

*Cryptocurrencies.* Bitcoin (launched 2009, designed by the pseudonymous "Satoshi Nakamoto") was the first. It runs without a central bank. Its uses are mainly speculation, with some use for cross-border payments, avoiding capital controls, and crime [Refining]. "Stablecoins," tokens tied to the dollar, grew to a few hundred billion dollars by the mid-2020s. Several central banks are testing their own digital currencies (China's e-CNY is the largest pilot).

**How it developed:**
- **~3000 BCE:** Mesopotamian temples and palaces recorded debts on clay tablets, measured in silver and barley. Credit records likely came before coins [Refining].
- Many societies used **commodity money**: cowrie shells, grain, salt, cloth, silver.
- **~600 BCE:** first coins in Lydia (in modern Turkey); coins emerged separately in China and India.
- **~1000s CE:** paper money in Song-dynasty China.
- **1300s–1600s:** Italian, then Dutch and English banking; double-entry bookkeeping. The Bank of England was founded in 1694.
- **~1870s–1914:** the **gold standard**: currencies exchangeable for fixed amounts of gold. Largely abandoned in the 1930s.
- **1944–1971:** the **Bretton Woods** system tied currencies to the dollar and the dollar to gold. The US ended gold convertibility in 1971; since then major currencies have been fiat and "float" against each other.
- **1990s onward:** most central banks become legally independent of politicians and adopt inflation targets (New Zealand first, 1990). Mobile money spreads (Kenya's M-Pesa, 2007).

**Effects on human life and society:** Credit lets humans buy homes (with loans called *mortgages*), start businesses, and smooth spending across life. About 1.3–1.4 billion adults have no bank or mobile-money account, mostly in poorer countries [Established, approximate]. Inflation surged in 2021–23 after the COVID-19 pandemic and the Russia–Ukraine war (peaks near 9% in the US and over 10% in the eurozone and UK), reducing real wages for millions. Financial crises can wipe out years of growth (see 5.8).

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Mainstream (New Keynesian) view:** central banks should adjust interest rates to stabilize inflation and employment. Dominant in central banks since the 1990s.
- **Monetarism** (Milton Friedman): inflation is "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon," caused by too much money. Partly absorbed into the mainstream; strict money-growth rules were dropped in the 1980s when the link proved unstable.
- **Austrian school and gold advocates:** central banks and fiat money cause booms, busts, and debasement; money should be tied to gold or left to private competition. A minority view among economists [Contested].
- **Modern Monetary Theory (MMT):** a government that issues its own currency cannot run out of it; the real limit is inflation. Most economists consider it misleading in practice [Contested].
- **Crypto advocates vs. skeptics:** supporters see a censorship-resistant alternative to state money; skeptics see a volatile speculative asset with few real uses [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- Walter Bagehot, *Lombard Street* (1873): the "lender of last resort" doctrine.
- John Maynard Keynes, *The General Theory* (1936): money, interest, and unemployment.
- Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz, *A Monetary History of the United States* (1963): blamed the Great Depression's depth on monetary collapse.
- Satoshi Nakamoto, "Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System" (2008).
- David Graeber, *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011): anthropological evidence that credit preceded coins.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: modern money is created mostly by bank lending; central banks influence inflation through interest rates; bank runs and deposit insurance work as described.
- Debated: how much central banks should respond to asset prices and financial risk; how large government debt can safely grow (see 5.6); crypto's long-run role [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: whether money should be controlled by states at all; whether charging interest is ethical (forbidden under Islamic law, and historically restricted by the Christian church) [Belief/Value].

**Open questions:** Whether digital central-bank currencies will replace cash; whether the dollar's dominance will last; how to prevent the next financial panic.

**See also:** 5.6 Government and the Economy; 5.8 Economic Crises; 6.1 The State; 7.4 Property, Contracts, and Commercial Law; 10.7 Religion's Effects on Society.

### 5.3 Labor, Capital, and Firms

**In one sentence:** Most humans get what they need by selling their time and effort (labor) to organizations (firms) that combine it with tools and money (capital) to make things others will buy.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Labor and wages.** *Labor* is human work used in production. A *wage* (or *salary*) is the money paid for it, usually per hour, month, or year. Human adults typically work 35–50 hours a week for several decades.
- **Capital** has three meanings: *physical capital* (machines, buildings, roads), *financial capital* (money available to invest), and *human capital* (skills and knowledge). **Investment** means spending now to build capital that produces more later. Worldwide, roughly a quarter of yearly output goes into investment.
- **Firm.** An organization that produces goods or services for sale. Its **profit** is revenue minus costs.
- **Corporation.** A firm that is a separate *legal person* (see 7.4): it can own property, sign contracts, and be sued. Its owners, the **shareholders**, have **limited liability**: if it fails, they lose only what they invested. Shareholders elect a board, which hires managers.
- **Entrepreneur.** Someone who starts a new firm, bearing the risk that it fails.

**How it works:**

*Where humans work.* The global workforce is about 3.6 billion people.

| Sector | Share of world workers (approx.) | Share in rich countries |
|---|---|---|
| Agriculture | ~25% | under 5% |
| Industry (manufacturing, mining, construction) | ~23% | ~20–25% |
| Services (trade, health, education, finance, etc.) | ~50% | ~70–80% |

Only about half of workers worldwide are wage employees; the rest are self-employed, small farmers, or unpaid family workers. About 60% work in the **informal economy**, without contracts, taxes, or legal protections. Roughly 140–160 million children do work classed as child labor, and an estimated ~28 million people are in forced labor (2021) [Established, approximate].

*How wages are set.* In the standard model, a wage tends toward the value of what one more worker adds to output (*marginal productivity*). Wages are also shaped by bargaining power, laws, discrimination, and how many employers compete for workers. When one or a few employers dominate a local job market (**monopsony**), they can pay less than workers produce.

*Unions* are organizations of workers that bargain collectively with employers over pay and conditions. Membership varies widely: roughly half or more of workers in Nordic countries, about 10% in the United States, and around 15% on average in rich countries, having declined since the 1970s.

*Firms* range from one-person shops to corporations with over a million employees and stock-market values of several trillion dollars. Other forms include partnerships, **cooperatives** (owned by workers or customers), and **state-owned enterprises** (owned by governments; large in China, energy, and transport).

*Why firms exist.* Ronald Coase (1937) asked why, if markets coordinate so well, so much activity happens inside organizations run by command. His answer: using markets has costs (searching, negotiating, enforcing contracts), and firms reduce those costs for many tasks.

**How it developed:** For most of history, most humans were farmers working family land, or were bound laborers: enslaved people, serfs, or indentured servants. Slavery existed in most ancient civilizations; the Atlantic slave trade forcibly moved about 12.5 million Africans between the 1500s and 1800s (see 4.5). The **joint-stock company**, selling shares to many investors, grew in the 1600s; the Dutch East India Company (1602) was among the first with publicly traded shares. The Industrial Revolution (from ~1760, Britain) moved workers into factories, initially with 60–80-hour weeks and child labor. Unions, labor laws, and the International Labour Organization (1919) followed. Hours in rich countries fell from ~3,000 per year in the late 1800s to roughly 1,400–1,800 today. Since the 1970s, manufacturing jobs in rich countries have shifted to services and to Asia.

**Effects on human life and society:** Work determines most humans' income, status, daily schedule, and often identity. Mechanization cut the US farm workforce from most workers in the early 1800s to under 2% today, while total employment grew. Women's participation in paid work rose sharply in the 20th century but remains lower than men's worldwide (roughly 50% vs. 70% of working-age adults).

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Marxist view:** workers produce value but owners of capital keep part of it as profit ("surplus value"), so capitalism is inherently exploitative and conflictual (see 9.4).
- **Neoclassical view:** in competitive markets, workers and capital are each paid roughly what they contribute; profit rewards risk and organization.
- **Institutional view:** pay depends heavily on laws, norms, and bargaining power, not only productivity.
- **Shareholder vs. stakeholder firms:** should corporations serve only owners' profits (Friedman, 1970) or also workers, customers, and communities? [Belief/Value, with Contested empirical claims]

*Contested policy: the minimum wage.* About 90% of countries set a legal minimum wage.
- *Case against high minimums:* the basic supply-and-demand model predicts that raising the price of labor above its market level reduces hiring, hurting the least-skilled workers most.
- *Case for:* where employers have wage-setting power (monopsony), a minimum can raise pay without cutting jobs, and higher pay can reduce turnover.
- *Evidence:* since Card and Krueger's 1994 study of fast-food jobs in New Jersey, hundreds of studies have found that moderate minimum wages (up to roughly half to 60% of a country's typical wage) have small or hard-to-detect effects on employment, while raising pay for low earners [Refining]. Some studies find reduced hours or hiring, especially for teenagers; the effects of very large increases are less known [Contested].

*Contested: automation and AI.* Historically, technology destroyed specific jobs while total employment grew. Whether AI will follow that pattern, and how fast, is unknown. Estimates of jobs "at risk" range from under 15% to nearly half, depending on method [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- Adam Smith (1776): the pin factory and the division of labor.
- Karl Marx, *Das Kapital* (1867): labor, capital, and exploitation.
- Ronald Coase, "The Nature of the Firm" (1937): why firms exist.
- Joseph Schumpeter, *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy* (1942): entrepreneurs and "creative destruction."
- Gary Becker, *Human Capital* (1964): education as investment.
- David Card and Alan Krueger, *Myth and Measurement* (1995): minimum-wage evidence.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: education raises average earnings; union membership has declined in most rich countries; mechanization shifted work from farms to factories to services.
- Debated: minimum-wage effects at high levels; why wages for less-educated workers stagnated in some rich countries; AI's effects on jobs [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: whether profit is fair reward or exploitation; what firms owe society [Belief/Value].

**Open questions:** Whether AI will raise or lower demand for human labor; why productivity growth in rich countries slowed after the mid-2000s; how to protect informal and gig workers.

**See also:** 5.5 Inequality; 7.4 Property, Contracts, and Commercial Law; 8.4 Social Class and Mobility; 9.4 Socialism and Communism; 12.6 Computing, the Internet, and Artificial Intelligence; 16.5 Artificial Intelligence.

### 5.4 Growth, Development, and Poverty

**In one sentence:** For most of history nearly all humans were poor; in the last ~200 years some societies became vastly richer, others are catching up, and explaining the difference is one of economics' central questions.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **GDP (Gross Domestic Product).** The total market value of all final goods and services produced in a territory in a year. World GDP is roughly $110–120 trillion. **GDP per capita** divides this by population: an average, not what a typical person earns.
- **PPP (purchasing power parity).** An adjustment for the fact that the same money buys more in poorer countries (a haircut costs far less in India than in Switzerland). PPP figures compare real living standards better.
- **Economic growth** is the yearly percentage rise in real (inflation-adjusted) GDP. Small rates compound: 2% a year doubles income in about 35 years; 7% a year doubles it in about 10.
- **Extreme poverty.** The World Bank's global line is about $3 a day per person (adjusted for prices, revised in 2025). Below it, people struggle to afford enough food.
- **What GDP misses:** unpaid household work, environmental damage, leisure, and how income is shared. The **Human Development Index** (1990) adds health and education.

**How it works:** Growth comes from more inputs (workers, capital) and, above all over the long run, from **productivity**: getting more output from the same inputs through better technology, skills, and organization. Robert Solow's 1956 model showed that piling up machines alone eventually runs into diminishing returns; sustained growth requires new ideas.

| Country income group (World Bank, approx.) | Income per person per year | Share of world population |
|---|---|---|
| High-income | above ~$14,000 | ~15% |
| Upper-middle-income | ~$4,500–14,000 | ~35% |
| Lower-middle-income | ~$1,100–4,500 | ~40% |
| Low-income | below ~$1,100 | ~9% |

**How it developed:**
- **Before ~1800:** most humans lived near subsistence everywhere. The richest regions were perhaps 2–3 times richer than the poorest [Refining].
- **The "Great Divergence" (~1800–1950):** industrialization in Britain, then Western Europe, North America, and Japan raised incomes there many times over, while much of Asia, Africa, and Latin America stagnated, often under colonial rule. World income per person has risen more than tenfold since 1820.
- **Catch-up (1950–today):** South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, and Hong Kong grew 6–10% a year for decades. South Korea went from roughly the income of poor African countries around 1960 to that of Western Europe. China, after market reforms from 1978, grew near 10% a year for three decades. India accelerated after reforms in 1991.

**Effects on human life and society:** In 1820 most humans lived in extreme poverty. In 1990, about 2 billion people (~38%) did. In the mid-2020s, roughly 800 million (~10%) do, about two-thirds of them in sub-Saharan Africa [Established, approximate; exact figures depend on the poverty line]. China alone accounts for about 800 million people leaving extreme poverty since 1980. Progress slowed after 2015 and briefly reversed during COVID-19 in 2020. Growth correlates strongly with longer lives, lower child mortality, and more schooling.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** Why are some countries rich? Every theory below has evidence; their relative weight is [Contested].
- **Institutions:** secure property rights, rule of law, and inclusive politics encourage investment (Douglass North; Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson, Nobel Prize 2024).
- **Geography:** climate, tropical disease, and access to the sea shape development (Jeffrey Sachs, Jared Diamond).
- **Culture and ideas:** attitudes toward work, trust, and innovation (Max Weber; Deirdre McCloskey).
- **Colonial extraction and dependency:** colonial powers extracted wealth and built institutions designed for extraction, leaving damaging legacies (Walter Rodney; dependency theorists). Kenneth Pomeranz argued Britain's lead owed much to coal and colonies.
- **The developmental state:** East Asia grew with heavy state guidance of investment and exports, not pure free markets (see 5.7).

*Contested policy: foreign aid.* Rich-country governments give about $200–225 billion a year in official aid (less after major donors made cuts in 2024–25). Migrants send home far more, roughly $650–700 billion a year in **remittances**.
- *Case for (Jeffrey Sachs):* targeted aid can break "poverty traps."
- *Case against (William Easterly, Dambisa Moyo):* top-down aid often fails, feeds corruption, and weakens local accountability.
- *Evidence:* health aid has strong documented successes (smallpox eradication, vaccination, malaria nets, HIV treatment saving many millions of lives) [Established]. The effect of aid on overall national growth is small or unclear [Contested]. Since the 2000s, randomized trials have tested specific programs; direct cash transfers to poor households often show lasting gains [Refining].

**Key works and figures:**
- Simon Kuznets: developed national income accounts (1930s), the basis of GDP.
- Robert Solow, "A Contribution to the Theory of Economic Growth" (1956).
- Amartya Sen, *Development as Freedom* (1999): development as expanding capabilities, not just income.
- Kenneth Pomeranz, *The Great Divergence* (2000).
- Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson, *Why Nations Fail* (2012).
- Abhijit Banerjee and Esther Duflo, *Poor Economics* (2011): experiments in development (Nobel Prize 2019, with Michael Kremer).
- Angus Maddison: long-run historical income estimates.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: sustained growth began ~200 years ago; extreme poverty has fallen sharply since 1990; East Asian economies converged rapidly.
- Debated: the main causes of the Great Divergence; whether poorest countries can repeat East Asia's export-led path; aid's growth effects [Contested].
- Matters of belief or value: whether GDP growth should be the goal at all, given environmental limits ("degrowth" advocates vs. "green growth") [Belief/Value, with Contested empirical claims].

**Open questions:** Why many African economies have grown slowly despite reforms; whether growth can continue while cutting emissions; why productivity growth slowed in rich countries.

**See also:** 4.5 Colonialism, Revolutions, and Industry; 5.5 Inequality; 5.7 International Trade and the Global Economy; 12.1 Tools and How Technology Changes; 16.1 Climate Change; 16.7 Resources: Food, Water, and Materials.
