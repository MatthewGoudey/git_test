# Domain 11: Philosophy and Ethics

Philosophy is the human practice of trying to answer the most basic questions (what exists, what can be known, how one should live) by careful reasoning rather than by experiment or by appeal to sacred authority. Most of its conclusions remain [Contested] or are matters of [Belief/Value], but the *history* of who argued what, and how those arguments shaped science, law, and politics, is [Established]. This domain explains the main questions, the main answers humans have given, and the traditions that produced them.

---

### 11.1 What Philosophy Is

**In one sentence:** Philosophy is the systematic use of argument to examine questions that cannot (yet) be settled by observation, such as what is real, what knowledge is, and what makes actions right or wrong.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- *Argument*: a set of statements (premises) offered as reasons for another statement (the conclusion). Philosophers judge arguments by whether the conclusion really follows and whether the premises are believable (see 3.2).
- *Thought experiment*: an imagined scenario used to test an idea, for example "If a perfect copy of you were made, would it be you?"
- *Concept*: a general idea, such as "justice" or "cause." Much philosophy is *conceptual analysis*: working out what such ideas mean and whether they hang together.
- "Philosophy" comes from Greek for "love of wisdom."

**How it works:** The main branches are:

| Branch | Central question |
|---|---|
| Metaphysics | What exists, and what is it like? |
| Epistemology | What is knowledge, and how do we get it? |
| Logic | What makes reasoning valid? |
| Ethics | What is good, and how should one act? |
| Political philosophy | What makes power legitimate, and what is a just society? |
| Aesthetics | What is beauty, and what is art? |
| Philosophy of mind, science, language, religion | Specialized questions about each |

Philosophy differs from **science** in that its questions usually cannot be decided by experiment, though philosophers increasingly use scientific findings. It differs from **religion** in that, in principle, it accepts no claim on authority or revelation; every premise is open to challenge. In practice the boundaries blur: much historical philosophy was also theology (reasoning about God).

Today philosophy is mostly an academic discipline, taught in universities worldwide. A 2020 survey of roughly 1,800 professional philosophers (mostly in English-speaking countries) found little agreement on core questions. For example, about two-thirds leaned toward atheism, about 60% toward moral realism (the view that some moral claims are objectively true), and about 60% toward compatibilism about free will (see 11.2) [Refining; figures approximate].

**How it developed:** Systematic philosophy appeared at roughly the same time in three regions: India (the Upanishads, ~800–500 BCE, and later Buddhist and Jain thought), China (Confucius, ~500 BCE, and rival schools), and Greece (Thales, ~600 BCE; Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, ~470–322 BCE). Some scholars call this the "Axial Age" [Contested as a single phenomenon]. Many sciences began as branches of philosophy and split off: physics (once "natural philosophy") in the 1600s–1700s, psychology in the late 1800s, and linguistics, economics, and logic-based computer science after that.

**Effects on human life and society:** Philosophical ideas underlie modern law (rights, due process), political systems (democracy, social contract), science (empiricism, the scientific method), and research ethics. Philosophy also shapes education: logic and ethics courses train argument.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** One view holds that philosophy makes real progress, slowly clarifying questions and sometimes handing them to science. Another holds that it rarely settles anything and mainly maps the possible answers. Both views have defenders [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- Socrates (~470–399 BCE): made questioning itself a method; executed by Athens.
- Plato's dialogues (~4th century BCE): the founding texts of Western philosophy.
- Confucius's *Analects*: foundational for Chinese ethics and politics.
- The Upanishads: early Indian inquiry into self and reality.
- *The Problems of Philosophy* (Bertrand Russell, 1912): a classic short introduction.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: the history of arguments and their influence; the branch structure.
- Debated: nearly every major philosophical conclusion.
- Matters of belief or value: answers to ethical and religious questions.

**Open questions:** Why is there something rather than nothing? Is morality objective? How does consciousness arise? Can philosophical questions ever be definitively answered?

**See also:** 3.2 Logic and Reasoning; 3.3 The Scientific Method; 10.1 What Religion Is and Why Humans Have It; 4.3 Classical Empires and Universal Religions; 11.5 World Philosophical Traditions.

### 11.2 Knowledge, Reality, and Mind

**In one sentence:** This field asks what humans can truly know, what the world is ultimately made of, and how minds (thoughts, feelings, awareness) relate to physical bodies and brains.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- *Epistemology*: the study of knowledge. *Metaphysics*: the study of what exists.
- *Consciousness*: the felt quality of experience: what it is like to see red or feel pain. Humans can describe its correlates in the brain but cannot yet explain why it exists at all (see 1.4).
- *Determinism*: the view that every event, including every human choice, is fully caused by prior events.

**How it works:**

*Knowledge.* A traditional definition (from Plato) is that knowledge is a belief that is true and justified (held for good reasons). In 1963 Edmund Gettier published short counterexamples, cases of justified true belief that seem to be lucky guesses rather than knowledge, and no replacement definition has won agreement since. *Skepticism* asks whether humans can know anything at all: René Descartes (1641) imagined a deceiving demon feeding him false experiences, and concluded that the one thing he could not doubt was that he was thinking ("I think, therefore I am"). Today's version: how would you know you are not a brain in a vat, or in a computer simulation?

*Sources of knowledge.* *Rationalists* (Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz) held that reason alone can reveal truths. *Empiricists* (Locke, Berkeley, Hume) held that all knowledge comes from sensory experience. Immanuel Kant (1781) argued that experience is always organized by built-in structures of the mind, such as space, time, and cause. David Hume's *problem of induction* (1748) remains unsolved: no non-circular argument shows that the future will resemble the past, yet all science assumes it. Indian Nyaya philosophers had earlier catalogued valid sources of knowledge (perception, inference, comparison, and testimony).

*Reality.* *Materialism* or *physicalism* says only physical things exist. *Idealism* says reality is fundamentally mental. *Dualism* says both exist. Other debates concern whether abstract things (numbers, properties) exist, and what makes a person the same person over time; the Buddhist view that there is no permanent self has modern defenders such as Derek Parfit.

*Mind and body.* Descartes argued that mind and body are different substances, but could not explain how they interact. Most philosophers and scientists today are physicalists [Refining as a majority view, not settled]. The **hard problem of consciousness** (named by David Chalmers, 1995) asks why physical brain processes are accompanied by experience at all. This question now bears on artificial intelligence: could a machine be conscious? [Contested].

*Free will.* If determinism is true, can humans choose freely and be morally responsible? *Compatibilists* (the most common view among philosophers) say yes, freedom means acting on one's own reasons without coercion. *Libertarians* (in this sense, unrelated to the political ideology) say real freedom requires that choices not be fully determined. *Hard determinists* say free will is an illusion. Neuroscience experiments since the 1980s showing brain activity before conscious decisions are [Contested] in what they imply.

**How it developed:** Greek and Indian thinkers debated these questions from ~500 BCE. The 17th–18th-century European debate between rationalism and empiricism shaped the Scientific Revolution. In the 20th century, the mind–body problem merged with neuroscience, psychology, and computer science.

**Effects on human life and society:** These debates shaped the scientific method, legal ideas of responsibility (whether someone "could have done otherwise"), and current arguments over the moral status of animals and AI systems.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** Rationalism vs. empiricism; physicalism vs. dualism vs. idealism; compatibilism vs. libertarianism vs. hard determinism (each described above). Neuroscience narrows these debates but has not settled them.

**Key works and figures:**
- *Meditations on First Philosophy* (Descartes, 1641): skepticism and mind–body dualism.
- *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (Hume, 1748): empiricism and the problem of induction.
- *Critique of Pure Reason* (Kant, 1781): how the mind structures experience.
- "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (Thomas Nagel, 1974): why consciousness resists physical explanation.
- *Reasons and Persons* (Derek Parfit, 1984): personal identity and ethics.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: the arguments and their history; that mental states correlate closely with brain states.
- Debated: the definition of knowledge, the nature of consciousness, free will.
- Matters of belief or value: whether a soul exists.

**Open questions:** Why is there subjective experience? Can a machine have it? Is free will compatible with a physical brain?

**See also:** 1.4 The Brain and Nervous System; 3.3 The Scientific Method; 12.6 Computing, the Internet, and Artificial Intelligence; 15.1 Cognition and Perception; 10.5 Spirituality, Ritual, and Religious Experience.

### 11.3 Ethics

**In one sentence:** Ethics is the study of right and wrong, good and bad: what humans owe each other, what makes a life go well, and whether moral claims can be true at all.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- *Morality*: the rules and ideals a group or person uses to judge conduct as right or wrong, praiseworthy or blameworthy.
- Ethics has three levels. *Normative ethics* asks which actions are right. *Metaethics* asks what moral claims are (facts? feelings? conventions?). *Applied ethics* tackles specific issues, such as abortion, euthanasia, animal welfare, or war.
- *Descriptive* claims about morality (e.g., "most societies forbid murder") are testable. *Normative* claims ("murder is wrong") are [Belief/Value] in this guide's labeling, even when nearly all humans share them.

**How it works:** Three frameworks dominate Western academic ethics, each with parallels elsewhere:

| Framework | Core idea | Classic test case answer | Main criticism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Consequentialism** (e.g., utilitarianism) | The right act is the one with the best outcomes; for utilitarians, the greatest total well-being | Divert a runaway trolley to kill one person instead of five | Seems to permit terrible acts (punishing an innocent person) if outcomes are better |
| **Deontology** (duty-based ethics) | Some acts are required or forbidden regardless of outcomes; people have rights that cannot be traded away | Do not push a person off a bridge to stop the trolley, even to save five | Rigid; unclear how to resolve conflicting duties |
| **Virtue ethics** | Focus on character: be honest, brave, just, and wise, and a good life (*eudaimonia*, "flourishing") follows | Ask what a person of good character would do | Gives vague guidance in hard cases |

The "trolley problem" (introduced by Philippa Foot, 1967) is a famous thought experiment for comparing these frameworks; surveys in dozens of countries find that far more people approve of diverting the trolley than of pushing a person, a pattern psychologists still study [Established as a finding; its interpretation is Contested].

Other views include *contractualism* (right acts are those no one could reasonably reject), *care ethics* (morality grounded in relationships and responsibility, developed largely by feminist philosophers from the 1980s), and *divine command theory* (right is what God commands). Plato's *Euthyphro* poses the classic problem for the last: is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good?

**Metaethics.** *Moral realists* hold that some moral claims are objectively true. *Anti-realists* hold that moral claims express attitudes or social agreements. *Descriptive relativism* (moral codes differ across cultures) is [Established]. *Normative relativism* (no culture's morality is better than another's) is [Belief/Value]. Research also finds near-universal themes: one study of 60 societies found that helping kin, helping one's group, reciprocating, bravery, respecting superiors, fair division, and respecting property were treated as morally good wherever they were documented [Refining].

**How it developed:** Aristotle (~350 BCE) developed virtue ethics; Confucius and Mencius developed a parallel ethics of cultivated character. Jeremy Bentham (1789) and John Stuart Mill (1863) founded utilitarianism. Immanuel Kant (1785) founded modern deontology with the *categorical imperative*: act only on principles you could will everyone to follow, and never treat people merely as means. Virtue ethics revived in Western academia after 1958. Applied ethics grew rapidly from the 1970s.

**Effects on human life and society:** Ethical theory shaped human rights law, medical ethics (the Nuremberg Code of 1947 after Nazi medical experiments; informed consent), animal welfare laws, and public-health trade-offs. Peter Singer's utilitarian arguments (1972) helped inspire the "effective altruism" movement in charitable giving.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** See the table. Many philosophers combine frameworks; in the 2020 survey, support was split roughly evenly among the three main ones [Refining].

**Key works and figures:**
- *Nicomachean Ethics* (Aristotle, ~340 BCE): founding text of virtue ethics.
- *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (Kant, 1785): duty and human dignity.
- *Utilitarianism* (Mill, 1863): the greatest happiness principle.
- "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (Peter Singer, 1972): argued that the rich are obliged to help distant strangers.
- *After Virtue* (Alasdair MacIntyre, 1981): revived virtue ethics.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: what each theory says; that moral codes vary across cultures yet share common themes.
- Debated: which framework (if any) is correct; whether morality is objective.
- Matters of belief or value: every first-order moral judgment.

**Open questions:** Is there moral truth independent of human minds? How should moral concern extend to animals, future generations, and possibly AI systems?

**See also:** 7.5 International Law and Human Rights; 9.1 What an Ideology Is; 10.7 Religion's Effects on Society; 15.6 Cooperation, Conflict, and Group Behavior; 11.4 Political Philosophy.

### 11.4 Political Philosophy

**In one sentence:** Political philosophy asks what makes some people's power over others legitimate, what a just society looks like, and how to balance values such as liberty and equality.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- *Legitimacy*: the quality of power being rightful, not merely effective. A robber and a tax collector both take money; most humans regard only the second as legitimate.
- *Justice*: giving people what they are owed, in punishment (retributive justice) or in the division of goods (distributive justice).
- *Liberty* and *equality* each have several meanings. Isaiah Berlin (1958) distinguished *negative liberty* (freedom from interference) from *positive liberty* (the real capacity to act and govern oneself).
- This field differs from **Domain 9 (Ideologies)**: political philosophy examines the arguments; ideologies are the mass movements built on them.

**How it works:** The central debates:
- *Who should rule?* Plato (~375 BCE) argued for rule by trained philosopher-guardians; Aristotle compared monarchy, aristocracy, and constitutional government; Confucian thinkers argued that rulers hold the "Mandate of Heaven" only while they govern virtuously.
- *Why obey the state at all?* **Social contract** theorists imagined people agreeing to government to escape a lawless "state of nature." Thomas Hobbes (1651) thought that state would be "a war of all against all," so almost any strong ruler is better. John Locke (1689) argued that people have natural rights to life, liberty, and property, and may overthrow governments that violate them. Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1762) grounded legitimacy in the "general will" of the people.
- *What may the state forbid?* John Stuart Mill's *harm principle* (1859): power may be used against an individual only to prevent harm to others.
- *How should goods be divided?* John Rawls (1971) asked what principles people would choose behind a "veil of ignorance," not knowing their own place in society; he argued they would protect basic liberties and allow inequalities only if they benefit the worst-off. Robert Nozick (1974) replied that any distribution arising from fair exchanges is just, so redistribution by taxation violates rights. Karl Marx argued that capitalism exploits workers and that justice requires ending class society.
- *Individual or community?* Communitarians (from the 1980s) argued that liberal theories ignore how people are shaped by communities and traditions.

**How it developed:** Classical Greek, Chinese, and Indian thinkers (including Kautilya's *Arthashastra* on statecraft) → medieval theories of divine authority → Machiavelli's realism (1532) → social contract theory (1600s–1700s) → liberalism, socialism, and conservatism (1700s–1800s) → after 1971, a revival of systematic theories of justice, plus feminist, global-justice, and post-colonial critiques.

**Effects on human life and society:** Locke's ideas are echoed almost word for word in the US Declaration of Independence (1776); Rousseau influenced the French Revolution (1789); Marx's ideas inspired states that at their peak governed about a third of humanity. Rawls's theory is widely cited in debates over welfare states. See 9.2–9.4 for outcomes.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** Liberalism (priority of individual rights), libertarianism (minimal state), egalitarianism (reduce inequality), republicanism (freedom as not being under anyone's arbitrary power), communitarianism, Marxism, and anarchism (no state is legitimate). The arguments' core premises are [Belief/Value]; their factual predictions (e.g., about how markets or planned economies perform) can be tested.

**Key works and figures:**
- *The Republic* (Plato, ~375 BCE): justice and the ideal state.
- *Leviathan* (Hobbes, 1651): the case for strong sovereign power.
- *Two Treatises of Government* (Locke, 1689): natural rights and consent.
- *On Liberty* (Mill, 1859): the harm principle.
- *A Theory of Justice* (Rawls, 1971): the most influential 20th-century theory of justice.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: the history of these arguments and their influence on constitutions and revolutions.
- Debated: empirical claims inside them (e.g., what life without states is like).
- Matters of belief or value: what justice requires; how to weigh liberty against equality.

**Open questions:** Can there be justice between nations, or between present and future generations? What legitimates global institutions, or the power of technology companies?

**See also:** 6.1 The State; 7.2 Constitutions and Rights; 9.2 Liberalism; 9.4 Socialism and Communism; 9.5 Capitalism and Free-Market Thought.

### 11.5 World Philosophical Traditions

**In one sentence:** Humans developed several largely independent traditions of philosophy, notably in Greece and Europe, China, India, and the Islamic world, along with others in Africa and elsewhere, each with its own questions, methods, and classic texts.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- A *tradition* here is a line of thinkers who read, answer, and build on each other over centuries.
- *Orthodox* and *heterodox*: in Indian philosophy, schools that accept the authority of the Vedas (Hindu scriptures) are called *astika* (orthodox); those that reject it, such as Buddhism and Jainism, *nastika* (heterodox).
- Traditions did not develop in isolation. Greek philosophy reached Europe largely through Arabic translations; Buddhism carried Indian thought to China and Japan.

**How it works:**

| Tradition | Period of origin | Central concerns | Representative figures |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Greek and Western** | ~600 BCE | Nature of reality, knowledge, virtue, logic, the state | Socrates, Plato, Aristotle; Stoics and Epicureans; Augustine, Aquinas; Descartes, Hume, Kant |
| **Chinese** | ~500 BCE | Ethics, social harmony, good government, the *Dao* | Confucius, Mencius, Xunzi (Confucian); Laozi, Zhuangzi (Daoist); Mozi; Han Feizi (Legalist); Zhu Xi, Wang Yangming (Neo-Confucian) |
| **Indian** | ~800 BCE | Self, liberation, sources of knowledge, logic | Six orthodox schools (Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Mimamsa, Vedanta); Buddhist (Nagarjuna), Jain, and materialist Charvaka thinkers; Shankara |
| **Islamic** | ~800 CE | Reason and revelation, God, metaphysics, logic | al-Kindi, al-Farabi, Ibn Sina (Avicenna), al-Ghazali, Ibn Rushd (Averroes), Ibn Khaldun |
| **African** | Oral traditions (ancient); written works from 1600s | Personhood, community, ethics | Zera Yacob (Ethiopia, 1667); Kwame Gyekye, Kwasi Wiredu, Paulin Hountondji (20th century) |
| **Modern analytic** | ~1900 | Logic, language, science, precise argument | Frege, Russell, Wittgenstein, Quine |
| **Modern continental** | ~1900 | Experience, existence, history, power | Husserl, Heidegger, Sartre, de Beauvoir, Foucault |

**Greek and Western.** Plato and Aristotle defined many of the questions later Western thought pursued. Hellenistic schools (Stoicism, Epicureanism, Skepticism) treated philosophy as a way of life. Medieval Christian, Jewish (Maimonides), and Muslim thinkers fused Greek philosophy with scripture. Early modern Europe produced the debates over knowledge and the social contract described in 11.2 and 11.4.

**Chinese.** Most Chinese schools focused on practical ethics and government. Confucians stressed moral cultivation, ritual, and family duty; Daoists, harmony with nature and suspicion of rigid rules; Mohists, impartial care for all; Legalists, strict laws and punishments. The Qin state used Legalist methods to unify China in 221 BCE; later dynasties adopted Confucianism as official doctrine (see 10.3).

**Indian.** Indian philosophy combined spiritual goals (liberation from rebirth) with highly developed logic and theory of knowledge. Debates included whether a permanent self exists, whether the world is real or illusory (Shankara's Advaita Vedanta, ~8th century, held that only the absolute, Brahman, is fully real), and what counts as valid evidence. Charvaka materialists rejected gods and rebirth entirely.

**Islamic.** From the 800s, scholars in Baghdad and elsewhere translated Greek works into Arabic and extended them. Ibn Sina's medical and philosophical works were used in European universities for centuries; Ibn Rushd's commentaries on Aristotle shaped medieval European thought. Al-Ghazali (~1095) argued that philosophers overstepped reason's limits.

**African.** Much African philosophy was oral and is preserved in proverbs and communal ethics, such as southern African *ubuntu* ("a person is a person through other persons"). Whether these collective worldviews count as philosophy, or only individual critical texts do, was a major 20th-century debate (the "ethnophilosophy" debate).

**Modern analytic and continental.** In the 20th century, Western philosophy split. *Analytic* philosophy, dominant in English-speaking universities, emphasizes logic, clarity, and links to science. *Continental* philosophy, centered in France and Germany, includes *phenomenology* (study of the structures of experience), *existentialism* (humans create their own meaning in an indifferent universe), and *critical theory* (analysis of power in society). The divide has softened since the late 20th century.

**How it developed:** See the table. Cross-tradition contact accelerated after ~1800, and the comparative study of traditions grew from the mid-20th century.

**Effects on human life and society:** Confucianism shaped East Asian government and family life for two millennia; Greek and Enlightenment philosophy shaped Western science and law; Indian philosophy shaped Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain practice; existentialism influenced 20th-century literature and art.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:** Some scholars argue "philosophy" proper is a Greek invention; others argue that this view reflects Western bias and that comparable critical inquiry arose independently in several places [Contested]. University curricula worldwide still focus mainly on the Western tradition.

**Key works and figures:**
- *Analects* (Confucius): foundation of East Asian ethics.
- *Tao Te Ching* (attributed to Laozi): foundational Daoist text.
- *Nicomachean Ethics* and *Metaphysics* (Aristotle): shaped Western, Jewish, and Islamic thought for 2,000 years.
- *The Book of Healing* (Ibn Sina, ~1027): major synthesis of Greek and Islamic philosophy.
- *Mulamadhyamakakarika* (Nagarjuna, ~2nd century CE): key Buddhist text on emptiness.
- *Being and Time* (Heidegger, 1927) and *The Second Sex* (de Beauvoir, 1949): central continental works.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: the existence, chronology, and influence of these traditions.
- Debated: how independent they were; whether "philosophy" is one human activity or several.
- Matters of belief or value: which tradition's answers are right.

**Open questions:** How much ancient thought, especially oral thought, has been lost? Can a global philosophy integrate these traditions, or are their starting points incompatible?
