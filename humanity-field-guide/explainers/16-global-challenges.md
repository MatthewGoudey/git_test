# Domain 16: Global Challenges and the Future

This domain covers the problems that humans themselves describe as shaping their medium-term future: changes to the planet's climate and living systems, disease, weapons able to end civilization, a new kind of technology that imitates human thinking, shifts in the size and age of the population, and the supply of food, water, and materials. These problems have two things in common. They cross the borders of the ~195 states into which humans divide themselves, so no single government can solve them alone. And they combine physical facts, which can often be measured, with choices about costs and priorities, which are [Belief/Value] questions that evidence alone cannot settle.

### 16.1 Climate Change

**In one sentence:** Humans are warming their planet, mainly by burning coal, oil, and gas, and this is changing weather, ice, oceans, and living things in ways that will last for centuries.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Weather** is the state of the air on a given day; **climate** is the average weather of a place over decades.
- The **greenhouse effect**: certain gases in the air (chiefly carbon dioxide, CO2; methane; water vapor) let sunlight in but absorb some of the heat the surface gives off, keeping the planet warmer than it would otherwise be. Without any greenhouse effect, Earth's average surface would be roughly −18 °C instead of ~+15 °C.
- **Fossil fuels** are the buried remains of ancient plants and microbes. Burning them releases carbon that had been out of the air for millions of years.
- **ppm** (parts per million) measures how much of a gas is in the air. **°C** is a temperature scale on which water freezes at 0 and boils at 100.

**How it works:** [Established] Before industrialization, CO2 was ~280 ppm. By the mid-2020s it was ~420–425 ppm, higher than at any point in at least 800,000 years (known from air trapped in ancient ice). Humans emit roughly 55–60 billion tonnes of greenhouse gases per year (counted as CO2-equivalent), about two-thirds of it from fossil fuels. Oceans and plants absorb about half of emitted CO2; the rest accumulates. Global average surface temperature has risen ~1.3 °C since the late 1800s (individual years, such as 2024, have briefly exceeded 1.5 °C). Observed consequences include sea level rise of ~20–25 cm since 1900, shrinking glaciers and Arctic sea ice, more intense heat waves, heavier downpours in many regions, and more acidic oceans.

[Refining] **Climate sensitivity**, the eventual warming from doubling CO2, is likely 2.5–4 °C, with ~3 °C the central estimate. How warming changes rainfall, droughts, and storms in particular regions is still being narrowed. [Contested] **Tipping points** are thresholds beyond which a system changes in a self-reinforcing way, such as the loss of the Greenland or West Antarctic ice sheets, dieback of the Amazon rainforest, or collapse of a major Atlantic ocean current (the AMOC). Most scientists consider these real possibilities, but the temperatures and timescales at which they would occur are disputed.

**How it developed:** Joseph Fourier described the heat-trapping effect of air in the 1820s; Eunice Foote (1856) and John Tyndall (1859) showed experimentally that CO2 absorbs heat. Svante Arrhenius estimated in 1896 that doubling CO2 would warm the planet several degrees. Continuous CO2 measurement began in 1958. The UN's Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) was founded in 1988; states signed a climate treaty in 1992, the Kyoto Protocol in 1997, and the Paris Agreement in 2015, which aims to hold warming "well below 2 °C" and pursue 1.5 °C. Since 2010, the cost of solar panels has fallen by roughly 90%, and wind and battery costs have also dropped sharply.

**Effects on human life and society:** Effects are already measurable: heat-related deaths, crop stress, coastal flooding, and wildfire. [Refining] With current policies, warming by 2100 is projected at roughly 2.5–3 °C. Poorer, hotter countries emitted least but are generally most exposed. China now emits ~30% of annual CO2; the US ~13%; the EU and India ~7% each. Counted over all history, the US and Europe have emitted the most.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Mitigation** means cutting emissions (renewables, nuclear power, electric vehicles, efficiency); **adaptation** means coping (sea walls, heat-tolerant crops). Most experts say both are needed.
- **Net zero**: emitting no more than is removed. Many states pledge it for 2050–2070.
- Economists debate the right **carbon price** and how much to value future generations' welfare (the **discount rate**), a debate with a large [Belief/Value] component.
- **Climate justice** holds that rich, high-emitting states owe poorer ones money and faster cuts; others stress that most future emissions growth is in developing economies.
- Some humans and political movements deny that warming is human-caused or serious; the scientific evidence does not support this.

**Key works and figures:**
- Svante Arrhenius (1896): first quantitative estimate of CO2 warming.
- Charles David Keeling's CO2 record (1958–): the "Keeling Curve."
- Syukuro Manabe (1967 model; Nobel Prize 2021): early physically based climate model.
- IPCC Assessment Reports (1990–2023; AR6 the latest full set): the consensus summary.
- *The Stern Review* (2006) and William Nordhaus (Nobel 2018): competing economic analyses of how fast to cut emissions.
- The Paris Agreement (2015): the current global framework.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: warming is happening and is mainly human-caused; CO2 levels; sea level rise; warming continues as long as net emissions continue.
- Debated: exact sensitivity; regional impacts; tipping-point thresholds; transition costs.
- Matters of belief or value: how to weigh present costs against future harm; who should pay; how much risk is acceptable.

**Open questions:** How sensitive is the climate, exactly? Will key tipping points be crossed, and when? Can emissions fall fast enough without large economic costs? Will deliberate cooling (e.g., reflecting sunlight with particles high in the atmosphere) be tried, and by whom?

**See also:** 2.5 Climate and Atmosphere; 12.3 Energy; 5.7 International Trade and the Global Economy; 6.5 International Relations and Diplomacy; 16.2 Biodiversity and Environmental Degradation; 16.7 Resources: Food, Water, and Materials.

### 16.2 Biodiversity and Environmental Degradation

**In one sentence:** Human land use, harvesting, pollution, and introduced species are shrinking the populations and ranges of many other living things, and degrading the air, water, and soil that humans also depend on.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- A **species** is a group of organisms that can interbreed. **Biodiversity** is the variety of life: species, the genetic variation within them, and the ecosystems they form.
- An **ecosystem** is a community of organisms and their physical surroundings, linked by flows of energy and nutrients.
- **Extinction** is the death of the last member of a species. Some extinction is natural; the long-run **background rate** is low. Earth's fossil record shows five **mass extinctions**, in which roughly three-quarters or more of species vanished in a geologically short time.
- **Ecosystem services** is the human term for what living systems provide to humans: pollination, clean water, fish, fertile soil, flood control.

**How it works:** [Refining] Scientists have described ~2.1 million species; estimates of the true total range from several million to far more (insects and microbes are poorly counted). The main drivers of loss, roughly in order of impact, are [Established in direction, Refining in ranking]: (1) **land and sea use change**, especially clearing forests and grasslands for farms (agriculture uses about half of the planet's habitable land); (2) **direct exploitation** (hunting, logging, fishing); (3) climate change; (4) pollution; (5) **invasive species** carried by humans to new places.

Some measures of scale: humans and their livestock make up ~95% of the mass of all mammals on Earth; wild mammals ~5%. Forests are lost at roughly 10 million hectares per year (net loss is smaller, as some forests regrow). About a third of assessed fish stocks are fished beyond sustainable levels. Humans make ~400 million tonnes of plastic per year, and less than 10% is recycled. Air pollution, indoor and outdoor, is linked to roughly 7 million early deaths per year [Refining].

**How it developed:** Humans have driven extinctions for tens of thousands of years; the disappearance of many large mammals as humans spread across continents is [Refining in its causes]. Industrialization from ~1800 multiplied pollution. Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* (1962) triggered modern environmental regulation. Wins followed: the US Clean Air Act (1970); bans on the pesticide DDT; and the **Montreal Protocol** (1987), which phased out chemicals destroying the ozone layer (a layer of the upper atmosphere that blocks harmful ultraviolet light). The ozone layer is now slowly recovering, often cited as humanity's most successful environmental treaty. The Convention on Biological Diversity was signed in 1992; in 2022 states agreed to protect 30% of land and sea by 2030 ("30x30"). Currently ~17% of land and ~8% of the ocean is protected on paper.

**Effects on human life and society:** About 1 million species are at risk of extinction, according to the 2019 IPBES global assessment [Refining]. Recorded extinctions since 1500 number roughly 900 species, likely a large undercount. Declines threaten fisheries that feed billions, pollinators that support about a third of crop production by volume, and forest peoples' livelihoods. Local pollution falls heavily on poorer communities.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Conservation** (protecting places and species) vs. **sustainable use** (managing harvest so it can continue). Indigenous groups often argue they manage land better than parks that exclude them; evidence partly supports this.
- **"Sixth mass extinction"** [Contested]: some biologists say one is underway; others say current losses, though far above background, are not yet near mass-extinction scale.
- The **Living Planet Index** reports an average 73% decline in monitored wildlife populations since 1970. Critics note this is an average of relative changes, heavily influenced by some steeply declining populations, and does not mean 73% of animals have vanished [Contested interpretation].
- Debates over whether nature has **intrinsic value** or matters mainly for its use to humans are [Belief/Value].

**Key works and figures:**
- Rachel Carson, *Silent Spring* (1962): launched modern environmentalism.
- E.O. Wilson (1929–2021): popularized "biodiversity"; *Half-Earth* (2016).
- The Montreal Protocol (1987): model of a successful global agreement.
- IPBES Global Assessment (2019): the main scientific summary.
- Rockström et al., "Planetary boundaries" (2009): framework of safe limits [Contested].
- Elizabeth Kolbert, *The Sixth Extinction* (2014): widely read synthesis.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: human activity is the dominant driver of current biodiversity loss; many populations are declining; pollution kills millions.
- Debated: total species count; true extinction rate; whether a mass extinction is underway; how far ecosystems can degrade before collapsing.
- Matters of belief or value: whether species matter for their own sake; trade-offs between development and conservation.

**Open questions:** How many species exist? Which ecosystems are near collapse? Can farming feed more people on less land? Will "30x30" be enforced in practice?

**See also:** 2.6 Life on Earth: Biology and Ecology; 12.2 Agriculture and Food Systems; 16.1 Climate Change; 16.7 Resources: Food, Water, and Materials; 9.8 Other Movements.

### 16.3 Pandemics and Global Health

**In one sentence:** Infectious diseases can spread across the whole species in months, and the ability to prevent, detect, and treat them is very unevenly shared among humans.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- A **pathogen** is a microbe (virus, bacterium, parasite, or fungus) that causes disease. An **epidemic** is an unusual surge of a disease in a region; a **pandemic** is one that spreads across many countries and continents.
- A **zoonosis** is a disease that jumps from other animals to humans. Most newly emerging human infections are zoonotic [Established].
- A **vaccine** trains the immune system to recognize a pathogen before infection. **Antibiotics** are drugs that kill bacteria; **antimicrobial resistance (AMR)** is the evolution of microbes that survive these drugs.
- **Excess deaths** are deaths above what would normally be expected in a period. They capture deaths missed by official counts.

**How it works:** A pathogen spreads if each infected person infects, on average, more than one other (this number is called **R**). Dense cities, air travel (several billion passenger trips per year), and close contact with farmed or wild animals raise the chance of spread. Defenses include surveillance (detecting outbreaks early), testing, isolation, masks and ventilation, treatments, and vaccines. AMR arises through natural selection: each use of an antimicrobial kills susceptible microbes and leaves resistant ones [Established]. Overuse in medicine and livestock speeds this.

**How it developed:** Pandemics have repeatedly reshaped human history: the Black Death (1340s) killed perhaps a third to half of Europe's population; diseases brought by Europeans killed a large majority of Indigenous Americans after 1492 [Refining in exact share]; the 1918 influenza killed roughly 50 million (estimates range ~17–100 million). Edward Jenner's smallpox vaccine (1796), germ theory (1860s–1880s), and antibiotics (penicillin discovered 1928, mass-produced 1940s) changed the balance. Smallpox was declared eradicated in 1980, the only human disease eliminated worldwide. HIV/AIDS, recognized in 1981, has killed ~40 million people. **COVID-19**, caused by a coronavirus first identified in Wuhan, China, in late 2019, was declared a pandemic in March 2020.

**Effects on human life and society:** COVID-19 officially recorded ~7 million deaths, but excess-death estimates for 2020–2023 range from roughly 15 to 30 million [Refining]. Governments closed schools and businesses, the world economy shrank ~3% in 2020, and debates over lockdowns and mandates deepened political divisions. Vaccines were developed in under a year (including the first widely used **mRNA** vaccines), and more than 13 billion doses were given, though poorer countries received them much later.

Ongoing burdens are large: tuberculosis kills ~1.2 million people a year, malaria ~600,000 (mostly young African children), HIV ~600,000. AMR directly caused an estimated ~1.2 million deaths in 2019 and contributed to ~5 million [Refining]. Deaths of children under five have fallen from ~12.8 million (1990) to ~5 million per year, but a child born in the poorest countries is still roughly 10–20 times more likely to die before age five than one born in the richest.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Global health security** stresses early detection and rapid response; **health systems strengthening** stresses basic clinics, staff, and supply chains. Funding is split between them.
- **COVID-19 response**: the balance between lockdowns (reducing spread) and their economic, educational, and liberty costs is [Contested], and partly [Belief/Value].
- **Origin of COVID-19** [Contested]: many virologists favor natural spillover from animals at a Wuhan market; some scientists and several intelligence agencies consider a laboratory-related accident plausible. No decisive evidence has been made public.
- **"One Health"** treats human, animal, and environmental health as linked.
- **Gain-of-function research** (making pathogens more transmissible to study them) is defended as useful for preparedness and criticized as dangerous.

**Key works and figures:**
- Edward Jenner (1796): first vaccine.
- John Snow (1854): traced cholera to a water pump, a founding act of epidemiology.
- Alexander Fleming (1928): discovered penicillin and warned of resistance (1945).
- World Health Organization (founded 1948): coordinates global health; led smallpox eradication.
- International Health Regulations (2005) and the WHO Pandemic Agreement (adopted 2025): legal rules for outbreak response.
- Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman (Nobel 2023): groundwork for mRNA vaccines.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: germ theory; vaccines' large effect on mortality; AMR's mechanism; that major pandemics recur.
- Debated: COVID-19's origin; effectiveness of specific non-drug measures; true death tolls; the likelihood of the next pandemic in a given decade.
- Matters of belief or value: individual liberty vs. collective protection; how vaccines and drugs should be shared between rich and poor countries.

**Open questions:** Where will the next pandemic come from (avian influenza such as H5N1 is one concern)? Can new antibiotics be developed fast enough? Will states comply with international health rules? Can surveillance be both effective and respectful of privacy?

**See also:** 1.6 Health, Disease, and Medicine; 12.7 Public Health and Medical Technology; 4.7 Globalization and the Present; 5.5 Inequality; 16.4 Nuclear Weapons and Catastrophic Risks.

### 16.4 Nuclear Weapons and Catastrophic Risks

**In one sentence:** Humans have built weapons, and could build or trigger other dangers, capable of killing a large share of the species in hours to years, and they manage this risk mainly through mutual threat and a patchwork of treaties.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Nuclear weapons** release energy by splitting heavy atoms (**fission**) or by fusing light ones (**fusion**, in "thermonuclear" or "hydrogen" bombs). One modern warhead can be tens to hundreds of times more powerful than the bomb that destroyed the city of Hiroshima.
- **Deterrence** is the strategy of preventing attack by threatening unacceptable retaliation. **Mutually assured destruction** describes a situation where each side could destroy the other even after being attacked first.
- **Arms control** means agreements to limit, verify, or reduce weapons. **Proliferation** is the spread of weapons to more states or groups.
- A **catastrophic risk** threatens a large fraction of humanity; an **existential risk** threatens extinction or a permanent collapse of human potential.

**How it works:** In the mid-2020s there are ~12,000 nuclear warheads, held by nine states: Russia and the United States (together ~88%), China (~600, growing fastest), France, the United Kingdom, India, Pakistan, Israel (which has never officially confirmed its arsenal), and North Korea. About 3,500–4,000 are deployed on missiles and bombers, and some ~2,000 are kept ready to launch within minutes.

[Contested] **Nuclear winter**: models suggest that smoke from burning cities could block sunlight, cool the planet by several degrees, and collapse harvests worldwide. One 2022 study estimated a large US–Russia war could cause famine killing billions; critics argue such models overstate smoke and cooling. Direct deaths alone would be in the tens to hundreds of millions [Refining].

Other catastrophic risks include **engineered pandemics** (biotechnology makes modifying pathogens easier), **asteroid impacts** (very rare; most large near-Earth objects are mapped), and **supervolcanic eruptions**.

**How it developed:** The US built the first bombs during World War II (the Manhattan Project) and used them on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, Japan, in August 1945, killing roughly 110,000–210,000 people by the end of that year. The Soviet Union tested its first in 1949. Global stockpiles peaked at ~70,000 in 1986. Near-misses include the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962) and a 1983 Soviet false alarm that officer Stanislav Petrov correctly judged to be an error. Key agreements: the Partial Test Ban Treaty (1963); the **Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT, 1968)**, joined by ~190 states; US–Soviet/Russian reduction treaties from 1972 onward; the Biological Weapons Convention (1972), which has no verification system; and the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (2017), which no nuclear-armed state has joined. Arms control has weakened since the 2000s: the INF Treaty collapsed in 2019, and New START, the last treaty limiting US and Russian deployed arsenals, reached its expiry in February 2026.

**Effects on human life and society:** No nuclear weapon has been used in war since 1945, about 80 years. Nuclear tests (over 2,000 since 1945) exposed populations to radiation. Nuclear powers spend roughly US$100 billion per year on these weapons. [Contested] Some scholars credit deterrence with preventing war between great powers since 1945; others call this luck or attribute peace to other causes.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Deterrence theory**: nuclear weapons stabilize relations by making major war suicidal.
- **Abolitionism**: weapons should be eliminated, since deterrence must work forever and has nearly failed.
- **Arms control realism**: reduce numbers and accident risk without full abolition.
- **Existential risk studies**: a newer field ranking threats to humanity's long-term future. Philosopher Toby Ord estimated a roughly 1-in-6 chance of existential catastrophe this century; this is a subjective judgment, not a measurement [Contested].
- Whether future generations' welfare should weigh heavily in present decisions (**longtermism**) is [Belief/Value].

**Key works and figures:**
- Einstein–Szilárd letter (1939): prompted the US bomb program.
- J. Robert Oppenheimer (1904–1967): led bomb design at Los Alamos.
- John Hersey, *Hiroshima* (1946): first widely read account of the human effects.
- Thomas Schelling, *The Strategy of Conflict* (1960): founding text of deterrence theory.
- Turco et al. (1983) and Alan Robock: nuclear winter research.
- Toby Ord, *The Precipice* (2020): survey of existential risks.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: arsenal sizes (roughly); the destructive power of nuclear weapons; the near-misses.
- Debated: the severity of nuclear winter; whether deterrence causes peace; the probability of nuclear war or engineered pandemics in a given period.
- Matters of belief or value: whether possessing weapons of mass destruction can be justified; how much present cost is worth paying to reduce small risks of extinction.

**Open questions:** Will a new three-way arms race (US, Russia, China) develop? Will more states seek weapons? How will AI and cyberweapons affect early-warning systems? Can biological threats be controlled as biotechnology becomes cheaper?

**See also:** 12.8 Weapons and Military Technology; 6.6 War and Organized Violence; 6.5 International Relations and Diplomacy; 4.6 The World Wars and the Cold War; 7.5 International Law and Human Rights; 16.3 Pandemics and Global Health; 16.5 Artificial Intelligence.

### 16.5 Artificial Intelligence

**In one sentence:** Humans have built computer systems that learn from data to perform tasks once thought to require human minds, such as writing, translating, recognizing images, and writing software, and they are rapidly expanding these systems' power while disagreeing sharply about the risks.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- A **computer** follows instructions (**programs**) to process information. An **algorithm** is a step-by-step procedure.
- **Artificial intelligence (AI)** is the broad project of making machines do tasks that need intelligence when humans do them.
- **Machine learning** means a system improves at a task by adjusting itself to examples rather than following hand-written rules. A **neural network** is a machine-learning system loosely inspired by brain cells: many simple numerical units connected in layers, whose connection strengths are tuned during **training**.
- A **large language model (LLM)** is a neural network trained on very large amounts of text to predict the next word; from this, it learns to answer questions, summarize, and converse.
- **Compute** is the amount of calculation used, run on specialized chips in buildings called **data centers**.

**How it works:** [Established] Modern AI performance improves predictably as developers increase data, model size, and compute (so-called **scaling laws**, though how long they continue is [Contested]). The compute used to train leading models has grown roughly 4–5 times per year in the 2020s. Companies and governments spend hundreds of billions of US dollars per year on chips and data centers. Data centers used ~1.5% of world electricity in 2024, and rising.

Mid-2020s systems write fluent text and working code, pass many professional exams, generate realistic images and video, and act as **agents** that take multi-step actions on computers. They also make confident errors ("hallucinations"), can be manipulated, and are only partly understood even by their makers [Established]. Hundreds of millions of people use AI chatbots weekly.

**How it developed:** Alan Turing asked in 1950 whether machines could think. The term "artificial intelligence" was coined for a 1956 workshop at Dartmouth College. Progress came in waves, with slumps called "AI winters" (1970s, late 1980s). Neural networks revived after 2012, when one far outperformed rivals at image recognition. In 2016 AlphaGo beat a world champion at the board game Go. The **transformer** design (2017) enabled LLMs; OpenAI's ChatGPT (November 2022) reached ~100 million users in about two months. In 2024, Nobel Prizes went to neural-network pioneers (Physics) and to AlphaFold, an AI that predicts protein shapes (Chemistry).

**Effects on human life and society:** [Contested] Economic effects are uncertain. Studies show productivity gains on specific tasks such as coding; estimates of overall impact range from modest to transformative. Some jobs will likely be automated and others created; how fast, and who gains, is disputed. Documented harms include fake images and audio ("deepfakes") used for fraud and harassment, and biased decisions when training data reflect past discrimination. Leading development is concentrated in a few firms, mainly in the US and China, and depends on advanced chips made largely in Taiwan, making AI a matter of great-power competition.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **AI safety / alignment**: the problem of making AI systems reliably pursue the goals their designers intend. Some researchers argue that systems smarter than humans could escape control and cause catastrophe. A 2023 survey of ~2,700 AI researchers found a median estimate of ~5% for outcomes as bad as human extinction; responses varied enormously [Contested].
- **Near-term harms focus**: others argue attention belongs on present problems (bias, surveillance, misinformation, labor) and that extinction scenarios are speculative.
- **Techno-optimism**: AI will greatly increase wealth, science, and health; heavy regulation would forgo benefits or cede ground to rivals.
- **Timelines** [Contested]: forecasts of when AI might match humans at most cognitive work range from a few years to many decades, or never.
- How much risk to accept for potential benefit, and who decides, is [Belief/Value].

**Key works and figures:**
- Alan Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950): posed the question of machine thought.
- Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio, Yann LeCun (Turing Award 2018): developed deep learning; Hinton and Bengio later warned of serious risks.
- Vaswani et al., "Attention Is All You Need" (2017): the transformer.
- Nick Bostrom, *Superintelligence* (2014), and Stuart Russell, *Human Compatible* (2019): influential cases for taking control risks seriously.
- The EU AI Act (2024): first broad AI law; plus international AI safety summits from 2023.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: rapid capability growth; current systems' strengths and typical failures; concentration of resources.
- Debated: whether scaling will continue; economic effects; timelines to human-level AI; probability of loss of control.
- Matters of belief or value: how to balance innovation against risk; whether AI systems could ever merit moral consideration; who should govern AI.

**Open questions:** Can humans verify what large models are doing internally? Will AI replace or complement human workers? Can rival states agree on rules? Could AI systems have any form of experience (unknown)?

*Note on perspective:* AI systems are now often used to help draft documents such as this one. Readers should treat this as a possible source of bias on this particular topic.

### 16.6 Demographic Change and Aging

**In one sentence:** Humans are having far fewer children than in the past, so their population is growing more slowly and aging fast, while most remaining growth is concentrated in Africa.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- The **total fertility rate (TFR)** is the average number of children a woman would have over her lifetime at current rates. (Human females carry pregnancies; humans usually bear one child at a time.) About **2.1** children per woman is the **replacement level**, which keeps a population stable over the long run without migration.
- **Life expectancy** is the average number of years a newborn would live at current death rates.
- The **demographic transition** is the pattern seen in most countries: death rates fall first (better food, sanitation, medicine), population grows fast, then birth rates fall too.
- The **old-age dependency ratio** compares people aged 65+ (mostly no longer in paid work) to people aged 15–64 (working age).

**How it works:** [Established] Global TFR has fallen from ~5 in 1950 to ~2.2–2.3 today. Roughly two-thirds of humans now live in countries below replacement. Examples (approximate, early-to-mid 2020s): South Korea ~0.7–0.8 (the lowest in the world), China ~1.0, Japan and Italy ~1.2, the US ~1.6, India ~2.0, Nigeria ~4.5, Niger ~6. Causes of decline are [Refining]: falling child deaths, urbanization, women's education and paid work, access to contraception, the cost of raising children, and changing norms about family.

Because past high birth rates built up large young generations, populations keep growing for decades after fertility falls ("population momentum"). [Refining] The UN projects world population peaking at ~10.3 billion in the mid-2080s; some other groups project an earlier, lower peak of ~9.5–10 billion around 2060–2070. Projections beyond a few decades depend heavily on assumptions about future fertility.

**How it developed:** World population was ~1 billion around 1800, 2.5 billion in 1950, and passed 8 billion in 2022. Its growth rate peaked at ~2.1% per year in the late 1960s and is now under 1%. Fertility fell first in Europe (1800s), then East Asia (rapidly after 1960; China also enforced a "one-child policy" 1980–2015), then Latin America and South Asia. Japan's population began shrinking around 2010, China's in 2022. Several governments now pay cash incentives for births, with modest measured effects so far [Refining].

**Effects on human life and society:**
- **Aging**: people 65+ are ~10% of humanity today and projected at ~16% by 2050. In Japan the share is ~29%. Fewer workers per retiree strains **pensions** (regular payments to retired people) and health systems, which in many countries are funded by taxes on current workers.
- **Africa's share**: sub-Saharan Africa has ~1.2 billion people and is projected to reach ~2 billion by 2050, about half of all global population growth to then. Whether this becomes an economic boost (a large working-age population, the "demographic dividend") depends on jobs and education [Contested].
- **Migration**: ~280–300 million people (~3.5%) live outside their country of birth; ~120 million are forcibly displaced by war or persecution. Aging rich countries need workers, while migration is among their most divisive political issues.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Overpopulation concern** (from Malthus to Paul Ehrlich's *The Population Bomb*, 1968): more people strain resources. Ehrlich's predictions of mass famine in the 1970s–80s did not occur, largely due to farm productivity gains.
- **Depopulation concern**: low fertility will shrink economies, innovation, and welfare states.
- **Adaptation view**: automation, later retirement, and migration can manage aging.
- Whether governments should try to influence how many children people have, and in which direction, is [Belief/Value]; coercive policies in the past (forced sterilizations in India in the 1970s, China's birth quotas) are documented.

**Key works and figures:**
- Thomas Malthus, *An Essay on the Principle of Population* (1798): population tends to outrun food.
- Warren Thompson (1929) and Frank Notestein (1945): demographic transition theory.
- Paul Ehrlich, *The Population Bomb* (1968): influential, largely failed predictions.
- UN *World Population Prospects* (regular editions; latest 2024): standard projections.
- Hans Rosling (1948–2017): popularized data on falling fertility and health gains.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: fertility decline; population aging; growth concentrated in Africa.
- Debated: the timing and size of the peak; causes of ultra-low fertility; economic effects of aging; whether pro-birth policies work.
- Matters of belief or value: ideal population size; immigration levels; reproductive rights and the role of the state.

**Open questions:** Will fertility in low-fertility countries recover, stabilize, or keep falling? Will African fertility fall as fast as it did in Asia? Can welfare states be redesigned for older populations?

### 16.7 Resources: Food, Water, and Materials

**In one sentence:** Humans draw ever larger quantities of food, fresh water, energy, and raw materials from a finite planet, and they disagree about whether ingenuity can keep supply ahead of demand indefinitely.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- A **resource** is anything humans use. **Renewable** resources (fish, forests, rivers) refill if not overused; **non-renewable** ones (oil, metal ores) exist in fixed amounts, though how much is extractable depends on price and technology.
- **Food security** means reliable access to enough safe, nutritious food. **Undernourishment** is eating too few calories over a long period.
- **Water stress** is when demand approaches or exceeds available fresh water. Only ~2.5% of Earth's water is fresh, and most of that is frozen or underground.
- **Groundwater** is water stored underground in rock layers (**aquifers**); some refill only over thousands of years.
- **Critical minerals** are materials vital to modern technology with concentrated or risky supply, such as lithium, cobalt, nickel, copper, and rare earth elements (used in batteries, magnets, and electronics).

**How it works:**
- **Food**: World farms produce more than enough calories to feed everyone; hunger is mainly a problem of poverty, conflict, and distribution [Established]. Still, ~670–730 million people are chronically undernourished, mostly in Africa and South Asia, and roughly one-third of food is lost or wasted. Synthetic nitrogen fertilizer (the Haber–Bosch process, 1909–13) is estimated to support food for roughly half of humanity [Refining].
- **Water**: Agriculture takes ~70% of human freshwater withdrawals. About 2 billion people lack safely managed drinking water, and ~3.5 billion lack safely managed sanitation. Roughly 4 billion experience severe water scarcity at least one month per year [Refining]. Major aquifers are falling in northern India, northern China, the Middle East, and the US High Plains.
- **Soil**: About a third of the world's soils are moderately to highly degraded by erosion, salt buildup, or nutrient loss (FAO, 2015) [Refining].
- **Materials**: Humans extract roughly 100 billion tonnes of materials per year (sand, gravel, ores, fossil fuels, biomass), more than triple the 1970 amount. The shift to low-carbon energy raises demand for minerals: China refines most of the world's rare earths, lithium, and cobalt, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo mines ~70% of cobalt. Supply has become a geopolitical issue.

**How it developed:** Thomas Malthus argued in 1798 that population would outrun food. Instead, food output per person rose, thanks to new land, fertilizer, machinery, and the **Green Revolution** (high-yield crops of the 1950s–70s, credited with averting famine in South Asia). The report *The Limits to Growth* (1972) used computer models to warn that growth could hit physical limits in the 21st century. Recurring "peak oil" forecasts were repeatedly overtaken by new extraction methods such as **fracking** (cracking rock to release oil and gas, widespread in the US since ~2010). In 1980, biologist Paul Ehrlich and economist Julian Simon bet on whether five metal prices would rise by 1990; they fell and Simon won, though other decades would have given different results.

**Effects on human life and society:** Famine deaths have fallen dramatically since the mid-1900s; recent famines are mostly linked to war and political crisis (e.g., in Yemen, Sudan, and Gaza in the 2020s [Refining in scale]). Food price spikes (2007–08; 2022, after Russia invaded Ukraine, a major grain exporter) pushed tens of millions into hunger. Water disputes affect shared rivers such as the Nile, Indus, and Mekong. Mining brings revenue but also pollution and, in some regions, documented child labor.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Limits / "Malthusian" view**: physical limits are real; endless growth in material use is impossible on a finite planet. Includes **degrowth** advocates.
- **Cornucopian / innovation view** (Julian Simon): human ingenuity, prices, and substitution overcome scarcity; past doom forecasts mostly failed.
- **Green growth / decoupling**: economies can keep growing while resource use and emissions fall. [Contested] Several rich countries have cut emissions while growing, but global material use has not yet decoupled.
- **Planetary boundaries** (2009, updated since) proposes nine Earth-system limits and argues several have been exceeded; critics say the thresholds are partly arbitrary [Contested].
- How to share limited resources between rich and poor, and present and future, is [Belief/Value].

**Key works and figures:**
- Thomas Malthus, *An Essay on the Principle of Population* (1798).
- Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch: industrial nitrogen fertilizer.
- Norman Borlaug (Nobel Peace Prize 1970): leader of the Green Revolution.
- Meadows et al., *The Limits to Growth* (1972) [Contested].
- Julian Simon, *The Ultimate Resource* (1981).
- Kate Raworth, *Doughnut Economics* (2017): growth within social and ecological limits.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: hunger is driven mainly by poverty and conflict; aquifer depletion; rising material use; concentrated mineral supply.
- Debated: whether absolute decoupling is achievable globally; how close limits are; future crop yields under climate change.
- Matters of belief or value: whether growth itself should continue; how resources should be distributed.

**Open questions:** Can yields keep rising in a hotter climate? Can mineral supply expand fast and cleanly enough for the energy transition? Will new crops or alternative proteins shrink farming's land use? Will water disputes between states escalate?

## Closing: The State of the Species

Measured by the things humans themselves track, ordinary human life has changed more in the last two centuries than in the previous several thousand years. The figures below are rounded; earlier ones are reconstructions with wide error margins [Refining].

| Measure | ~1800 | ~1950 | mid-2020s |
|---|---|---|---|
| Population | ~1 billion | ~2.5 billion | ~8.2 billion |
| Life expectancy at birth | ~30 years | ~46 years | ~73 years |
| Children dying before age 5 | ~40% | ~20–25% | ~4% |
| Living in extreme poverty | ~75–85% | ~50–60% | ~10% |
| Adults who can read | ~10–15% | ~50% | ~87% |

These gains came mainly from farming productivity, sanitation, vaccines and medicine, fossil-fuel energy, economic growth, and mass schooling [Established in broad outline; Refining in the relative weight of each cause]. They are unevenly shared: in the poorest countries, life expectancy is still ~20–30 years shorter than in the richest.

The same capacities produce the main risks covered in this domain. Burning fossil fuels has warmed the planet ~1.3 °C. Land use and harvesting are depleting wild species and ecosystems. ~12,000 nuclear warheads remain, with arms-control treaties weakening. Global travel and biotechnology raise pandemic risk, as COVID-19 showed. Artificial intelligence is advancing faster than human understanding of how to govern it. Populations are aging, and pressure on water, soil, and minerals is rising.

[Contested] Humans disagree about how these trends balance. Some argue that past progress shows problems will be solved as they have been before; others argue that today's risks are different in kind because they are global and potentially irreversible. Both camps generally agree that outcomes depend on choices not yet made, and that most of these problems require cooperation among the ~195 states that, so far, humans have found difficult to achieve.
