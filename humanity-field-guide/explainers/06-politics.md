# Domain 6: Politics and Power

Politics is the activity through which humans decide who gets to make binding decisions for a group, what those decisions are, and how they are enforced. Its central institution is the **state**, and its central currency is **power**: the ability to make others act as they otherwise would not. Politics decides whether humans go to war, how much of their income is taken in taxes, which rights they hold, and, in the worst cases, whether they live or die. This domain describes how human political systems actually work. The *ideas* about how they should work are covered mainly in Domain 9 (Ideologies) and 11.4 (Political Philosophy).

---

### 6.1 The State

**In one sentence:** A state is an organization that claims the sole right to make and enforce binding rules, using force if necessary, over everyone inside a fixed piece of territory.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Legitimacy** is the belief, among the people being ruled, that a ruler's power is rightful. Accepted power is called **authority**. Legitimacy is cheaper than force: a state most people obey willingly needs far fewer police.
- **Borders** are lines, agreed on or disputed by neighboring states, marking the edge of a state's **territory**. They are mostly invisible on the ground and enforced at crossing points.
- **Sovereignty** is a state's claim to be the highest authority inside its territory and to be free from outside control.
- A **nation** is a group of people who see themselves as sharing a culture, language, history, or destiny. A **state** is a governing organization. They often do not line up. The Kurds (roughly 30–40 million people) see themselves as a nation but have no state of their own. India is one state containing hundreds of language and ethnic groups.
- A **government** is the set of people currently running a state. Governments come and go; the state persists.
- **Citizenship** is legal membership in a state, bringing rights (to live there, often to vote) and duties (taxes, sometimes military service).

**How it works:**
The sociologist Max Weber (1919) gave the most-quoted definition: a state is a community that successfully claims a **monopoly on the legitimate use of physical force** within a territory. Other groups may use force, but only the state's use is officially allowed.

States do a common core of jobs: defense, policing, courts, taxes, money, and records of who lives where. Most also build infrastructure, run schools, and pay pensions. To pay for this, governments in rich countries collect roughly 30–45% of their country's total income (GDP) in taxes; many low-income states collect only 10–15%. The work is done by a **bureaucracy**, a hierarchy of salaried officials following written rules. In many rich countries, about one worker in five is employed by the government.

There are about **195 sovereign states**; 193 belong to the United Nations, and the Vatican and Palestine are UN observers. They range from India and China (~1.4 billion people each) to Tuvalu (~11,000) and Vatican City (~800). Statehood partly depends on other states agreeing that you are one. Some entities govern themselves but lack wide recognition. **Taiwan** (~23 million people) governs itself, but the People's Republic of China claims it, and only about a dozen states formally recognize it. **Kosovo** and **Palestine** are recognized by some states and not others.

Citizenship is usually acquired at birth: by being born on the territory (most of the Americas) or through a parent (most of Europe, Asia, and Africa). Several million people are **stateless**, recognized by no state; the Rohingya of Myanmar are the largest such group.

**State capacity** is a state's practical ability to carry out its decisions: to collect taxes, enforce laws, and deliver services everywhere it claims to rule. Where capacity collapses, observers speak of **fragile** or **failed states**. Somalia had no effective central government for most of the 1990s and 2000s. Libya has had rival governments since 2014. In Haiti, armed gangs controlled most of the capital by 2024–25. There, militias, clans, and gangs take over the state's jobs, including taxing and policing.

**How it developed:**
For most of the ~300,000 years of their existence, humans lived in bands of a few dozen people without any state. The first states appeared about **5,000–5,500 years ago** in Mesopotamia (modern Iraq) and Egypt, and later, independently, in the Indus Valley, China, Mesoamerica, and the Andes. They grew out of farming, which produced storable surpluses (especially grain) that rulers could count and tax. Early writing was largely used for this accounting.

The modern system of sovereign, bordered states is usually traced to Europe's **Peace of Westphalia (1648)** [Refining: historians call this origin story simplified]. Charles Tilly summarized the European pattern as "war made the state, and the state made war": rulers built tax systems and bureaucracies to pay for armies. Colonial empires spread this model worldwide and then broke up: the UN had 51 founding members in 1945 and has 193 today (the newest, South Sudan, joined in 2011). Government spending in rich countries rose from about 10% of GDP around 1900 to about 40% today.

**Effects on human life and society:**
- Nearly every human lives under a state, and **which state** matters hugely: a worker moving from a poor country to a rich one can often earn several times more for the same work.
- States with high capacity tend to have longer life expectancy, higher literacy, and less violent crime [Established as correlation; Refining on causes].
- States have also been among the largest killers of humans. Twentieth-century governments killed tens of millions of civilians, many of them their own citizens, through massacres, man-made famines, and camps [Established; totals Contested].

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Social contract theory** holds that states are justified because people would agree to them. Thomas Hobbes (1651) argued that without a state, life would be "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." John Locke (1689) argued a state exists to protect rights and loses legitimacy if it violates them. [Belief/Value]
- **Marxism** sees the state as a tool by which an economic class rules others, and predicted it would "wither away" under communism. No communist state has withered away.
- **Anarchism** holds that states are unnecessary and harmful. [Belief/Value]
- **Chinese tradition** linked legitimacy to the **Mandate of Heaven**: a ruler governs rightly only while governing well [Belief/Value].

**Key works and figures:**
- *Arthashastra* (attributed to Kautilya, India, ~300 BCE–300 CE): a detailed manual on running a state, taxing, and spying.
- *Muqaddimah* (Ibn Khaldun, 1377): a theory of dynasties rising and falling as group solidarity strengthens and decays.
- *Leviathan* (Thomas Hobbes, 1651): the classic case for a powerful state as the cure for chaos.
- "Politics as a Vocation" (Max Weber, 1919): the monopoly-on-force definition.
- *Coercion, Capital, and European States* (Charles Tilly, 1990): how war built states.
- *Seeing Like a State* (James C. Scott, 1998): how states make people "legible" (countable, taxable) and how grand state plans can fail.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: States emerged after farming; they now cover almost all inhabited land; state capacity varies enormously.
- Debated: Whether states reduced violence overall compared with stateless societies [Contested]; why some states build strong capacity and others do not; whether outsiders can build states (the US-led effort in Afghanistan, 2001–2021, failed within weeks of US withdrawal).
- Matters of belief or value: How much a state *should* do; whether state power is ever fully legitimate.

**Open questions:** Why did states arise when and where they did? Can state capacity be built quickly? Will power shift upward to bodies like the European Union, or toward large technology companies, which now control infrastructure that states depend on?

**See also:** 4.2 Farming and the First Civilizations; 5.6 Government and the Economy; 7.2 Constitutions and Rights; 9.6 Nationalism; 11.4 Political Philosophy.

### 6.2 Forms of Government

**In one sentence:** Forms of government are the different rule-sets humans use to decide who holds state power, how they get it, and what limits it.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- A **monarchy** is rule by one person, a **monarch** (king, queen, emperor, sultan, emir), who usually inherits the position from a parent.
- A **republic** is any state without a hereditary monarch as head of state. A republic is not automatically a democracy; North Korea calls itself a republic.
- **Democracy** means "rule by the people." In **direct democracy**, citizens vote on decisions themselves. In **representative democracy**, they elect people to decide for them.
- An **aristocracy** or **oligarchy** is rule by a small group, often a hereditary or wealthy elite.
- **Theocracy** is rule by religious authorities, claiming to govern according to divine law.
- The **head of state** is the symbolic top figure; the **head of government** runs day-to-day affairs. Sometimes one person holds both roles.
- The **executive** carries out laws; the **legislature** (parliament, congress) makes them; the **judiciary** (courts) interprets them.

**How it works:**
The Greek philosopher Aristotle (~350 BCE) sorted governments by whether one, a few, or many rule. That scheme still helps, but modern classification looks at two questions: **how is power gained**, and **how is it checked**?

| Form | How power is gained | How power is checked | Examples (mid-2020s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Absolute monarchy | Inheritance, often chosen within a royal family | Royal family, religious leaders, risk of revolt; few formal checks | Saudi Arabia, Oman, Brunei, Eswatini, Vatican City (elected monarch) |
| Constitutional monarchy | Monarch inherits; real power held by elected government | Elections, courts, parliament; monarch is largely symbolic | United Kingdom, Japan, Sweden, Spain, Thailand |
| Presidential democracy | President and legislature elected separately | Separation of powers, courts, fixed terms, elections | United States, Brazil, Mexico, Indonesia, Nigeria |
| Parliamentary democracy | Voters elect parliament; parliament's majority picks the prime minister | Parliament can remove the government; courts; elections | India, Germany, Japan, Canada, Australia |
| Semi-presidential | Elected president shares power with a prime minister answerable to parliament | Mixed | France, Portugal, Ukraine, (formally) Russia |
| One-party state | Rise through the ruling party | Party discipline and factions; no legal opposition | China, Vietnam, Laos, Cuba |
| Military rule | Seizure of power by armed forces (a **coup**) | Rival officers; mass protest; foreign pressure | Myanmar, Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger |
| Theocracy | Selection by religious bodies | Religious hierarchy; limited elections | Iran (a senior cleric serves as Supreme Leader above an elected president), Afghanistan under the Taliban (since 2021) |

About 43 states have a monarch as head of state, but 15 of these share one: the British monarch is also king of Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Jamaica, and others.

A second dimension is how power is divided **geographically**. In a **unitary state** (France, Japan, China), the central government holds ultimate authority and local governments act by its permission. In a **federal state**, power is constitutionally split between a central government and regional units (states, provinces) that cannot be abolished at will. About 25 countries are federal, including the US, India, Brazil, Germany, Nigeria, Mexico, Russia, Canada, and Australia; together they hold roughly 40% of humanity.

Formal labels can mislead. Many states hold elections that the incumbents cannot lose, or have constitutions that are ignored. Political scientists therefore classify regimes by **how they actually operate**, often using expert-coded datasets (see 6.3).

**How it developed:**
Monarchy was the most common form of large-scale government for about 5,000 years, often backed by claims of divine approval. Early republics and limited democracies appeared in Athens (from ~508 BCE, with only adult male citizens voting, perhaps 10–20% of residents) and Rome (509–27 BCE). Most later states were monarchies or empires until the late 1700s, when revolutions in America (1776) and France (1789) spread the idea that authority comes from the people. In 1900, most humans lived under monarchs or colonial empires. By the 2000s, absolute monarchy survived mainly in the Persian Gulf region, and most states called themselves republics.

**Effects on human life and society:**
Form of government shapes taxes, rights, and the risk of violence. Democracies have rarely, if ever, fought full wars against each other (the **democratic peace**) [Established as pattern; reasons Contested]. Federalism can let diverse regions govern themselves (Switzerland, India) but can also let regions block national policy.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Presidential vs. parliamentary:** political scientist Juan Linz (1990) argued presidential systems are more prone to breakdown because president and legislature can deadlock with no way out. Others argue the pattern reflects where presidential systems were adopted (often Latin America) rather than the system itself [Contested].
- **Majoritarian vs. consensus democracy:** Arend Lijphart (1999) contrasted systems that concentrate power in the winning majority (UK-style) with those that spread it among many parties and groups (Swiss- or Dutch-style), and found consensus systems did at least as well on most measures [Contested].
- **Mixed government:** from Polybius (~150 BCE) to the US founders, many thinkers held that combining one, few, and many produces stability [Belief/Value, with some empirical support].

**Key works and figures:**
- *Politics* (Aristotle, ~350 BCE): the first comparative study of constitutions (he collected 158).
- *The Spirit of the Laws* (Montesquieu, 1748): the case for separating legislative, executive, and judicial power.
- *The Federalist Papers* (Hamilton, Madison, Jay, 1787–88): the design logic of the US federal system.
- "The Perils of Presidentialism" (Juan Linz, 1990).
- *Patterns of Democracy* (Arend Lijphart, 1999).

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Most states today are formally republics; real practice often differs from formal labels; federal and unitary states both exist at every income level.
- Debated: Which democratic design works best; whether federalism reduces or inflames ethnic conflict.
- Matters of belief or value: Whether monarchy or theocracy can be legitimate; how much regions should govern themselves.

**Open questions:** Why do some constitutional designs last for centuries while others collapse within years? Can institutions designed for one society be transplanted to another?

**See also:** 6.3 Democracy and Elections; 6.4 Authoritarianism; 7.2 Constitutions and Rights; 10.6 Secularism, Atheism, and Religious Change; 11.4 Political Philosophy.

### 6.3 Democracy and Elections

**In one sentence:** Democracy is a system in which the people being governed choose, and can peacefully remove, those who govern them, usually through regular competitive elections.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- An **election** is a scheduled event in which eligible adults (**voters**) choose officials, usually by secret ballot. The right to vote is called **suffrage**.
- A **political party** is an organized group that runs candidates under a shared name and program, hoping to win office.
- **Separation of powers** divides government among branches (executive, legislature, courts) so each can block the others. **Checks and balances** are the specific tools for doing so: vetoes, court rulings, impeachment.
- **Civil society** means organized groups outside government: unions, charities, religious bodies, clubs, advocacy groups.
- A **free press** is news media that can criticize government without being shut down or punished.
- Scholars distinguish **electoral democracy** (free, fair, competitive elections) from **liberal democracy** (elections *plus* protected rights, independent courts, and limits on the majority).

**How it works:**
Every few years, voters choose representatives. The rules that turn votes into seats strongly shape politics:

| Voting system | How it works | Typical effect | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| First-past-the-post | Country divided into districts; the candidate with the most votes in each wins | Tends toward two large parties; winners often hold a majority of seats with a minority of votes | United States, United Kingdom, India, Canada |
| Two-round | If no one wins a majority, top candidates face a runoff | Many parties compete, then consolidate | France, many presidential elections (Brazil) |
| Proportional representation (PR) | Parties win seats in proportion to their vote share | Many parties; coalition governments common | Netherlands, Israel, South Africa, most of Europe |
| Mixed | Some seats by district, some by PR | Blends both | Germany, Japan, New Zealand |
| Ranked / preferential | Voters rank candidates; lowest are eliminated and votes transferred | Rewards broad appeal | Australia (lower house) |

The observed tendency of district-based plurality systems to produce two main parties is called **Duverger's law** [Refining: a strong tendency, with many exceptions such as India and Canada].

The scale is large. **2024** was the biggest election year in human history: national elections in roughly 70 countries home to about half of humanity. India's 2024 general election had about 970 million eligible voters and about 640 million votes cast, spread over six weeks. Indonesia held its presidential and legislative elections for about 200 million voters in a single day.

How many humans live in democracies depends on the measure. The three most-cited rating systems (V-Dem, Freedom House, and the Economist Intelligence Unit) broadly agree on the extremes but differ in the middle. By V-Dem's count, roughly 70% of humans lived in autocracies in the mid-2020s, and autocracies slightly outnumbered democracies for the first time in about two decades [Refining]. A large reason is that India, with about 18% of humanity, has been classed by V-Dem since 2018 as an "electoral autocracy," a rating the Indian government rejects [Contested].

**How it developed:**
Athens practiced direct democracy from about 508 BCE, but only for free adult males. Councils and assemblies existed in many societies, from Icelandic and Germanic assemblies to the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy in North America. The English Parliament gradually gained power over the monarch, especially after 1689. The American (1776) and French (1789) revolutions spread the principle of popular sovereignty. Suffrage then widened step by step: property requirements were dropped; New Zealand was the first self-governing country to let women vote in national elections (1893); Switzerland did so only in 1971. Racial exclusions ended in the US South after 1965 and in South Africa in 1994.

Political scientist Samuel Huntington described three **waves of democratization**: about 1828–1926, 1943–1962, and from 1974 (Portugal) through the collapse of communism in Eastern Europe (1989–91). Since about 2010, many researchers describe a **wave of autocratization** [Refining].

**Effects on human life and society:**
- Economist Amartya Sen observed that no major famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press, because governments that can be voted out respond to food crises [Established as pattern; Refining on exceptions].
- Democracies rarely fight wars against each other (see 6.2).
- One major study (Acemoglu and others, 2019) found countries that democratize end up about 20% richer after 25 years [Contested]. Some autocracies (South Korea and Taiwan before the late 1980s, China since 1980) also grew very fast.
- Democracies usually protect speech, religion, and the right to organize more reliably than autocracies [Established].

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Minimalist view:** Joseph Schumpeter (1942) defined democracy simply as competition among elites for votes. Its virtue is peaceful removal of rulers.
- **Liberal view:** elections are not enough; rights, courts, and a free press are essential to prevent "tyranny of the majority" (Tocqueville, J.S. Mill).
- **Participatory/deliberative view:** citizens should take part directly, through referendums (as in Switzerland, which holds several national votes a year) or randomly selected **citizens' assemblies** (used in Ireland on abortion and same-sex marriage).
- **Critiques:** Plato argued ordinary citizens lack the knowledge to rule. Modern critics point to low voter knowledge; defenders reply that groups can decide well even when individuals are poorly informed [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- Pericles' Funeral Oration (recorded by Thucydides, ~431 BCE): an early statement of democratic ideals.
- *Democracy in America* (Alexis de Tocqueville, 1835/1840): how civil society sustains democracy.
- *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy* (Joseph Schumpeter, 1942).
- *The Third Wave* (Samuel Huntington, 1991).
- *How Democracies Die* (Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt, 2018): how elected leaders erode democracy from within.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: The share of humans with voting rights grew enormously after 1900; voting rules shape party systems; democracies rarely fight one another.
- Debated: Whether democracy is in long-term decline or a temporary dip [Contested]; how to measure it; whether democracy causes growth.
- Matters of belief or value: Whether democracy is the best form of government; who should be allowed to vote (citizens only? at what age?).

**Open questions:** **Democratic backsliding** is the gradual weakening of democracy by elected leaders: packing courts, pressuring media, changing election rules. Researchers point to cases such as Hungary after 2010, Turkey, Venezuela, and Nicaragua, and debate whether others, including the United States, are on a similar path; views on this differ sharply along partisan lines [Contested]. Other open questions: How do social media and polarization (growing hostility between political camps) affect democracy? Why has trust in elected governments fallen in many countries?

**See also:** 6.2 Forms of Government; 6.4 Authoritarianism; 9.2 Liberalism; 13.3 Media and Mass Communication; 13.4 The Internet and Social Media.

### 6.4 Authoritarianism

**In one sentence:** Authoritarianism is government in which rulers hold power without being genuinely accountable to the governed, typically by restricting political competition, free speech, and independent courts.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- An **autocracy** is any non-democratic government. "Authoritarian," "autocratic," and "dictatorship" are used almost interchangeably.
- **Totalitarianism** is an extreme form in which the state tries to control not just politics but all of social, economic, and private life, guided by an official ideology (Nazi Germany, the USSR under Stalin, North Korea).
- **Censorship** is the suppression of speech or information. **Surveillance** is systematic watching of people, now often by digital means.
- **Repression** means using force or threats (arrest, torture, killing) against opponents.
- **Co-optation** means buying loyalty by sharing power, jobs, or money.
- An **elite** is the small group with outsized power: senior officials, military officers, wealthy business owners.

**How it works:**
No ruler can govern alone. Every autocrat depends on a coalition, usually the military, security services, party officials, and business elites. Political scientists find autocracies survive through a mix of three tools:

1. **Repression:** secret police, political prisoners, exile, and, at the extreme, mass killing.
2. **Co-optation:** sharing wealth (often from oil or state companies) and positions with key supporters.
3. **Legitimation:** persuading the public that the regime deserves to rule, through economic performance, nationalism, ideology, religion, or claims of protecting order.

| Type | Who rules | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| One-party | A ruling party; leaders rise through it | China (Communist Party, ~100 million members), Vietnam, Cuba, Laos |
| Military | Officers, after a coup | Myanmar (since 2021); Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger (2020–2023 coups) |
| Personalist | One leader dominates all institutions, often with family | North Korea (Kim family since 1948), Belarus (same president since 1994), Turkmenistan, Nicaragua |
| Monarchic | Royal family | Saudi Arabia, United Arab Emirates, Qatar |
| Theocratic | Religious authorities | Iran, Afghanistan under the Taliban |
| Electoral ("competitive") authoritarian | Leaders win elections held on an uneven field: opponents jailed or barred, media controlled | Rating agencies place Russia, Venezuela, Azerbaijan and others here; governments concerned reject the label |

Most modern autocracies hold elections. These serve to display strength, test officials' loyalty, and gather information. Lopsided results are common: Russia's 2024 presidential election was officially won with about 87% of the vote; anti-war candidates were barred from running, and the best-known opposition leader, Alexei Navalny, had died in prison a month earlier.

Modern technology has changed the tools. Some states use facial recognition, monitoring of messaging apps, and large-scale online censorship; China's internet filtering system (the "Great Firewall") blocks most major foreign websites. Governments, democratic as well as autocratic, ordered roughly 300 internet shutdowns in 2024 by one civil-society count. Political scientists Sergei Guriev and Daniel Treisman argue many newer autocrats are "spin dictators," relying on information manipulation more than open terror [Refining].

**How it developed:**
For most of recorded history, nearly all large states were autocracies of some kind. Totalitarianism is a 20th-century creation, made possible by mass media, modern bureaucracy, and ideology. The worst human-caused death tolls of that century came under totalitarian regimes. Stalin's USSR killed millions through executions, labor camps, and famine (including the Holodomor in Ukraine, 1932–33). Mao's Great Leap Forward caused a famine (1959–61) that killed roughly 15–45 million people [range reflects Contested estimates]. The Khmer Rouge in Cambodia (1975–79) killed about 1.5–2 million, around a fifth of the population. In the present, a 2022 UN human-rights report found China's detention of large numbers of Uyghurs and other Muslims in Xinjiang may amount to crimes against humanity; China describes the facilities as vocational training centers [Contested].

**Effects on human life and society:**
Outcomes under autocracy vary more widely than under democracy [Refining]. Some autocracies achieved rapid growth: China lifted roughly 800 million people out of extreme poverty after 1980 by World Bank measures, and Singapore became very rich under one dominant party. Others produced economic collapse, famine, or mass killing. Autocracies more often restrict speech, religion, and movement, and hold more political prisoners.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Selectorate theory** (Bueno de Mesquita and others): leaders who depend on a small coalition reward it with private goods (cash, contracts); leaders who need many supporters provide public goods (schools, roads).
- **Performance legitimacy:** some argue autocracies can be justified if they deliver order and growth. Critics reply that they cannot be removed when they fail [Belief/Value].
- Defenders of particular systems, such as China's government, argue their model reflects national conditions and delivers stability; critics emphasize rights restrictions [Belief/Value].

**Key works and figures:**
- *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (Hannah Arendt, 1951): how totalitarian movements arose from mass society.
- *Totalitarian and Authoritarian Regimes* (Juan Linz, 1975/2000): the standard distinction between the two.
- *Competitive Authoritarianism* (Levitsky and Way, 2010): regimes that hold real but unfair elections.
- *The Dictator's Handbook* (Bueno de Mesquita and Smith, 2011): selectorate theory for general readers.
- *Spin Dictators* (Guriev and Treisman, 2022).

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Autocracies depend on elite coalitions; most now hold elections; the 20th century's largest state killings occurred under totalitarian regimes.
- Debated: Whether autocracies are better at long-term planning [Contested]; whether digital surveillance makes them more durable.
- Matters of belief or value: Whether order or prosperity can justify limits on freedom.

**Open questions:** Historically, most autocrats lost power through coups by insiders. Since the Cold War, more have fallen through mass protest or lost elections. Examples include Bangladesh (2024, when protests forced out a long-serving prime minister) and Syria (December 2024, when the Assad family's 54-year rule ended after 13 years of civil war). Whether AI-assisted surveillance will reverse this trend is unknown.

**See also:** 6.3 Democracy and Elections; 6.7 Revolutions, Protest, and Political Change; 9.7 Fascism and Totalitarianism; 13.5 Propaganda, Persuasion, and Misinformation; 4.6 The World Wars and the Cold War.

### 6.5 International Relations and Diplomacy

**In one sentence:** International relations is how states (and some other groups) deal with each other in a world that has no government above them, and diplomacy is the practice of managing those dealings by negotiation.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Anarchy** here does not mean chaos. It means there is **no world government**: no authority above states that can make binding laws and enforce them. Each state must, in the end, look after itself.
- A **great power** is a state strong enough to shape the whole system. In the mid-2020s, the United States and China are the two largest; Russia is a major military (especially nuclear) power with a much smaller economy.
- An **alliance** is a promise between states to help each other, often militarily.
- A **treaty** is a written agreement between states, treated as binding.
- **Sanctions** are penalties, usually economic (bans on trade, frozen bank accounts), imposed to change another state's behavior.
- **Soft power** (a term coined by Joseph Nye, 1990) is influence through attraction (culture, values, prestige) rather than force or payment.
- **Diplomats** are officials who represent their state abroad, usually from an **embassy** in another state's capital. By long custom, codified in a 1961 treaty, they are immune from local arrest.

**How it works:**
States pursue security, wealth, and status using a toolkit: negotiation, trade, aid, alliances, sanctions, propaganda, and the threat or use of military force. World military spending was about **US$2.7 trillion in 2024**, roughly 2.5% of world GDP. The United States accounted for about 37% of it; China, the second-largest spender, an estimated 12%.

States also cooperate through **international organizations**:

| Organization | Founded | Members | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| United Nations (UN) | 1945 | 193 | Forum for all states; peacekeeping; humanitarian and development agencies. Its **Security Council** can authorize force and binding sanctions, but any of its five permanent members (US, UK, France, Russia, China) can veto a decision. |
| NATO | 1949 | 32 | Military alliance of North American and European states; an attack on one is treated as an attack on all. Finland (2023) and Sweden (2024) joined after Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine. |
| European Union (EU) | 1993 (roots 1951) | 27 | Unusually deep union: a shared market, shared laws, and for 20 members a shared currency (the euro). |
| African Union | 2002 | 55 | Continental body for Africa; suspends members after coups. |
| ASEAN | 1967 | 11 | Southeast Asian cooperation; Timor-Leste joined in 2025. |
| G20 / BRICS | 1999 / 2009 | ~20 / ~10 | Forums of large economies; BRICS (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa, plus newer members) presents itself as a voice for non-Western states. |

Sanctions are now a primary tool. After 2022, Western states froze roughly US$300 billion of Russia's central-bank reserves. Iran, North Korea, Venezuela, and others are also under heavy sanctions. Studies disagree on how often sanctions achieve their stated goals; one well-known review estimated about a third of cases succeeded at least partly [Contested]. Sanctions also impose costs on ordinary people in the targeted country.

Major present-day tensions include rivalry between the US and China over trade, technology, and military power in Asia; the confrontation between Russia and NATO states over Ukraine; and the status of Taiwan, which the People's Republic of China says is part of its territory and which governs itself, while the US keeps its position on defending Taiwan deliberately vague. Many states in Africa, Asia, and Latin America (often called the **Global South**) avoid picking sides.

**How it developed:**
Diplomacy is ancient: one of the oldest surviving peace treaties, between Egypt and the Hittite empire, dates to about 1259 BCE. Permanent embassies spread from Renaissance Italy in the 1400s. The Congress of Vienna (1815) created a system of regular consultation among European great powers. After World War I, the **League of Nations** (1920) tried to make war illegal and failed to prevent World War II. The UN (1945) and financial institutions such as the IMF and World Bank (1944) followed. The Cold War (about 1947–1991) divided the world between US- and Soviet-led blocs. The US was the single dominant power after 1991; since the 2010s, many analysts describe a shift toward a more **multipolar** world [Refining].

**Effects on human life and society:**
International politics decides whether wars start, whether trade flows, and whether global problems such as climate change and pandemics get coordinated responses. UN agencies feed and vaccinate hundreds of millions of people. The UN has deployed peacekeepers in over 70 missions since 1948. Security Council vetoes have also blocked action in many crises, including Syria and Ukraine.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**

| Theory | Core claim | Strongest evidence | Main criticism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Realism** | States seek power and security in an anarchic world; morality and institutions matter little | Recurrent great-power rivalry; alliances shift with power balances | Underrates cooperation and domestic politics |
| **Liberalism** (in IR, not the same as the ideology in 9.2) | Trade, institutions, and democracy can make cooperation durable | Long peace in Western Europe; democratic peace | Institutions fail when great powers defect |
| **Constructivism** | States' interests come from identities and shared ideas, which can change | Changes like European integration; norms against chemical weapons | Hard to test or predict with |
| **Marxist / dependency** | International order reflects economic exploitation of poorer by richer states | Persistent global inequality | Rapid growth of some poor countries (East Asia) |

Most specialists treat these as lenses, each useful for some questions [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- *History of the Peloponnesian War* (Thucydides, ~400 BCE): "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must."
- *Perpetual Peace* (Immanuel Kant, 1795): the idea that republics and trade could end war.
- *Politics Among Nations* (Hans Morgenthau, 1948): founding text of modern realism.
- *Theory of International Politics* (Kenneth Waltz, 1979).
- *Power and Interdependence* (Robert Keohane and Joseph Nye, 1977).
- "Anarchy Is What States Make of It" (Alexander Wendt, 1992): the constructivist challenge.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: There is no world government; great-power vetoes limit the UN; economic interdependence is very high.
- Debated: Whether trade reduces war [Contested]; whether the world is becoming multipolar; whether sanctions work.
- Matters of belief or value: Whether states owe duties to foreigners; when outside intervention is justified.

**Open questions:** Can a rising power and an established one avoid war (sometimes called the "Thucydides Trap") [Contested]? Can humans govern global problems without a global government?

**See also:** 5.7 International Trade and the Global Economy; 6.6 War and Organized Violence; 7.5 International Law and Human Rights; 16.4 Nuclear Weapons and Catastrophic Risks.

### 6.6 War and Organized Violence

**In one sentence:** War is sustained, organized armed fighting between groups, usually states or groups seeking to control a state, and it remains one of the largest causes of deliberate human death.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- An **army** (and navy, air force) is a state's organization of trained, armed people, called **soldiers**. Most are young men.
- A **civil war** is a war inside one state, between its government and armed groups, or between rival groups. An **interstate war** is between states.
- **Civilians** are people who are not fighting. Laws of war (see 7.5) require that they not be deliberately targeted; this is frequently violated.
- **Terrorism** is violence against civilians by non-state groups intended to frighten a wider audience for political ends. There is no universally agreed definition, because governments often label their opponents "terrorists."
- **Genocide** is the attempt to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group (legal definition, 1948).
- **Deterrence** is preventing an attack by threatening unacceptable retaliation.

**How it works:**
Researchers at Uppsala University, who maintain the most widely used dataset, count an **armed conflict** when a government is involved and at least 25 people die in battle in a year, and a **war** when 1,000 or more die. By their count, there were about 60 active state-based conflicts in 2023–2024, the highest number since records began in 1946. Most are civil wars, many with foreign states involved. Major ongoing wars in the mid-2020s include Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine (since February 2022); the civil war in Sudan (since April 2023), which has displaced over 10 million people; the war between Israel and Hamas in Gaza (since October 2023); and conflicts in Myanmar, the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Africa's Sahel region.

Deaths are hard to count. Battle deaths worldwide ranged from tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand per year over the past three decades; 2022, with wars in Ethiopia and Ukraine, was among the deadliest years since the early 1990s [Refining]. War also kills indirectly, through hunger, disease, and destroyed health systems, often in larger numbers than combat. At least 120 million people were **forcibly displaced** from their homes by conflict and persecution in 2024, about double the number a decade earlier.

**How it developed:**
Archaeological evidence of group violence goes back at least 10,000 years, and war became more organized with states. Gunpowder (invented in China, ~9th century) transformed it over centuries. Industrialization made war vastly deadlier: **World War I** (1914–18) killed roughly 15–20 million people; **World War II** (1939–45) killed roughly 70–85 million, about 3% of all humans alive, including some 6 million Jews murdered in the Holocaust. Nuclear weapons were used twice, on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, Japan, in 1945, killing perhaps 110,000–210,000 people by the end of that year. Since 1945 there has been no direct war between great powers, a period some call the **Long Peace**. Wars since have mostly been civil wars or wars involving smaller states, and drones and cyberattacks have added new forms of fighting. In Ukraine, small cheap drones have become one of the main causes of battlefield casualties [Refining].

Other forms of mass violence: the Rwandan genocide (1994) killed roughly 500,000–800,000 people, mostly Tutsi, in about 100 days. Terrorism killed roughly 8,000 people in 2024, down from a peak above 30,000 around 2014–15 when the group known as ISIS was strongest; the deadliest region is now Africa's Sahel. The attacks on the United States on 11 September 2001 killed nearly 3,000 people and led to wars in Afghanistan and Iraq in which several hundred thousand people died.

**Effects on human life and society:**
War destroys lives, cities, and economies; a civil war is estimated to set a country's development back by decades [Refining]. It has also driven state formation, taxation, technology (radar, jet engines, computers, and the internet all had military origins), and the redrawing of borders. Survivors often carry long-term psychological trauma.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Rational bargaining theory** (James Fearon, 1995): because war is costly, both sides should prefer a deal. Wars happen when sides misjudge each other's strength, cannot trust each other to keep promises, or see the prize as indivisible (holy sites, for example).
- **Greed vs. grievance:** do civil wars arise from opportunity (lootable resources such as diamonds, poor states) or from injustice (ethnic exclusion)? Evidence suggests both, with poverty and weak states among the strongest predictors [Refining].
- **Just war tradition:** war can be justified only for a just cause, as a last resort, with proportionate means [Belief/Value]. **Pacifism** holds war is never justified [Belief/Value].
- **Is war declining?** Steven Pinker argues violence, including war, has fallen over centuries. Others (Bear Braumoeller, Pasquale Cirillo and Nassim Taleb) argue large wars are too rare to show a reliable trend, and the next could be catastrophic [Contested].

**Key works and figures:**
- *The Art of War* (attributed to Sun Tzu, ~5th century BCE): strategy, deception, winning without fighting.
- *On War* (Carl von Clausewitz, 1832): war as "the continuation of politics by other means."
- "Rationalist Explanations for War" (James Fearon, 1995).
- *The Better Angels of Our Nature* (Steven Pinker, 2011) [Contested in parts].
- *Why We Fight* (Christopher Blattman, 2022): why most rivalries do *not* become wars.

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: World War II was the deadliest war in history; civil wars are now the most common form; nuclear weapons have not been used in war since 1945.
- Debated: Whether war is in long-term decline; the deterrent role of nuclear weapons [Contested]; casualty totals in many recent wars.
- Matters of belief or value: When, if ever, war is justified; how to weigh civilian deaths against military aims.

**Open questions:** Will nuclear deterrence hold indefinitely? How will AI-guided weapons change war? Why do some ethnically divided societies fight and others not?

**See also:** 4.6 The World Wars and the Cold War; 7.5 International Law and Human Rights; 12.8 Weapons and Military Technology; 15.6 Cooperation, Conflict, and Group Behavior; 16.4 Nuclear Weapons and Catastrophic Risks.

### 6.7 Revolutions, Protest, and Political Change

**In one sentence:** Political systems change through gradual reform, through mass movements and protest, through sudden seizures of power by insiders (coups), and occasionally through revolutions that overturn the whole social order.

**What an alien needs to know first:**
- **Reform** is change made through existing institutions: passing laws, amending constitutions, winning elections.
- A **revolution** is a rapid, fundamental change in who holds power and often in how society is organized, usually driven by mass mobilization and frequently violent.
- A **coup d'état** ("coup") is the seizure of government by a small group from inside the state, usually military officers.
- A **social movement** is a sustained, organized effort by many people to change society, such as movements for workers', women's, or civil rights.
- **Nonviolent resistance** (or civil resistance) uses strikes, boycotts, mass demonstrations, and refusal to cooperate instead of weapons.

**How it works:**
Rulers depend on cooperation from soldiers, officials, workers, and taxpayers. Movements succeed when they cause that cooperation to break down, especially when security forces refuse to shoot protesters. **Defections** by elites or the military are the most common turning point.

Coups are common. Researchers count roughly 490 coup attempts worldwide since 1950, about half successful, with Africa experiencing the most. A cluster of successful coups hit West and Central Africa between 2020 and 2023 (Mali, Guinea, Sudan, Burkina Faso, Niger, Gabon).

Political scientists Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan studied over 300 major resistance campaigns from 1900 to 2006. They found **nonviolent campaigns succeeded about 53% of the time, versus about 26% for violent ones**, likely because nonviolent movements attract far more participants and more easily win over defectors [Refining]. A widely repeated claim that no campaign failed once 3.5% of the population actively joined is [Contested], with later counterexamples. Success rates for nonviolent campaigns fell sharply after 2010 [Refining].

**How it developed:**

| Revolution | Dates | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| American | 1775–1783 | Independence from Britain; a constitutional republic that kept slavery |
| French | 1789–1799 | End of absolute monarchy; a period of mass executions (the Terror); then Napoleon's rule |
| Haitian | 1791–1804 | Enslaved people overthrew French colonial rule; first state founded after a successful slave revolt |
| Mexican | 1910–1920 | Land reform; one party dominated until 2000 |
| Russian | 1917 | Communist one-party state (the USSR) |
| Chinese | 1949 | Communist one-party state that still rules |
| Iranian | 1979 | Monarchy replaced by an Islamic republic |
| Eastern Europe | 1989–1991 | Largely peaceful end of communist rule; most states became democracies |

Nonviolent movements reshaped many countries: India's independence movement led by Mohandas Gandhi (independence 1947); the US civil rights movement (1950s–60s); the "People Power" revolt in the Philippines (1986); and the campaign against **apartheid** (South Africa's legal system of racial segregation), which combined protest, armed struggle, international sanctions, and negotiation before ending with elections in 1994. The **Arab Spring** (2010–2012) toppled rulers in Tunisia, Egypt, Libya, and Yemen; Tunisia became a democracy (later reversed after 2021), Egypt returned to military-backed rule, and Libya, Syria, and Yemen fell into civil war. Youth-led protest waves forced out governments in Sri Lanka (2022), Bangladesh (2024), and Nepal (2025).

**Effects on human life and society:**
Revolutions have produced both lasting democracies and some of the most durable dictatorships. Research by Levitsky and Way (2022) finds that regimes born of violent revolution are unusually long-lived and resistant to coups. Revolutions are often followed by civil war, terror, or foreign intervention; the Russian civil war (1917–22) killed millions. Coups usually produce military governments that promise elections and frequently delay them.

**Major ideas, schools, or ideologies:**
- **Marxist theory:** revolutions arise from class conflict when economic change outgrows the political order [Belief/Value as program; Contested as prediction].
- **Structural theory** (Theda Skocpol, 1979): revolutions happen when states are weakened, often by war or fiscal crisis, at the same time as peasants or workers revolt.
- **Rising expectations:** Tocqueville noted that revolts often come when conditions are *improving* but not fast enough.
- **Demographic-structural theory** (Jack Goldstone, Peter Turchin): rapid population growth, large youth populations, and too many would-be elites competing for too few positions raise instability [Contested].
- **Reform vs. revolution:** conservatives (Edmund Burke) favor gradual change; revolutionaries argue some systems cannot be reformed from within [Belief/Value].

**Key works and figures:**
- *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (Edmund Burke, 1790): the case against sudden upheaval.
- *The Old Regime and the Revolution* (Alexis de Tocqueville, 1856).
- *States and Social Revolutions* (Theda Skocpol, 1979).
- *From Dictatorship to Democracy* (Gene Sharp, 1993): a manual of nonviolent tactics used by movements worldwide.
- *Why Civil Resistance Works* (Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan, 2011).

**What is established vs. debated:**
- Established: Military and elite defections are central to regime collapse; coups are concentrated in poorer states; nonviolent campaigns historically succeeded more often than violent ones in the studied period.
- Debated: Why nonviolent success rates have fallen [Contested]; whether social media helps or hurts movements; whether revolutions can be predicted.
- Matters of belief or value: When rebellion against a government is justified; whether violence is ever a legitimate political tool.

**Open questions:** Can revolutions be predicted? Humans have repeatedly failed to anticipate them (the fall of the USSR surprised most experts). Do digital surveillance and online organizing shift the balance toward rulers or toward protesters?
