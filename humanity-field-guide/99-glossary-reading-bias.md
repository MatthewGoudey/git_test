# Glossary, Reading Path, and a Note on Bias

---

## Glossary

Short definitions of human-specific terms used throughout this guide. Numbers in brackets point to the field where the term is explained in depth.

### A–C
- **Agnosticism**: The position that whether gods exist is unknown or unknowable. [10.6]
- **Agriculture**: Deliberately growing plants and raising animals for food and materials. It began ~12,000 years ago. [4.2, 12.2]
- **Algorithm**: A precise, step-by-step procedure for solving a problem, especially one a computer follows. [12.6]
- **Antibiotic**: A drug that kills bacteria or stops their growth. Overuse breeds resistant bacteria. [1.6, 12.7]
- **Atheism**: The absence of belief in gods. [10.6]
- **Authoritarianism**: Rule in which power is concentrated in a leader or small group that is not accountable to the public through free elections. [6.4]
- **Bank**: A business that holds people's money and lends it to others, earning interest. [5.2]
- **Bias (cognitive)**: A systematic, predictable error in human thinking. [15.3]
- **Bureaucracy**: A system of salaried officials who carry out a government's or organization's work according to rules. [6.1]
- **Capital**: Things produced in order to produce other things (machines, buildings, tools), or money used to buy them. [5.3]
- **Capitalism**: An economic system in which most productive property is privately owned and goods are mostly allocated through markets. [9.5]
- **Caste**: A hereditary social rank, fixed at birth, that traditionally limits occupation and marriage. Most associated with South Asia. [8.4]
- **Central bank**: A government-linked institution that issues a country's money and sets interest rates. [5.2]
- **Citizen**: A person legally recognized as a member of a state, with its rights and duties. [6.1]
- **City**: A large, dense permanent settlement where most people do work other than farming. [8.6]
- **Civil war**: War between organized groups within one state. [6.6]
- **Class (social)**: A group of people who share a similar economic position (wealth, income, occupation). [8.4]
- **Climate**: The long-term average pattern of weather in a place, usually measured over 30 years or more. [2.5, 16.1]
- **Colonialism**: One society taking political control of another territory and its people, usually to extract resources or settle. [4.5]
- **Communism**: (1) The theoretical goal, in Marxism, of a classless, stateless society with common ownership. (2) In practice, one-party states ruled by communist parties (e.g., the USSR, China). [9.4]
- **Comparative advantage**: The principle that two parties can both gain from trade if each specializes in what it gives up least to produce. [5.7]
- **Consciousness**: Subjective experience, i.e., what it is like to be something. It is not well explained by science. [1.4, 11.2]
- **Conservatism**: A political outlook that values tradition, stability, and gradual change. [9.3]
- **Constitution**: The basic law that sets up a government and limits its powers. [7.2]
- **Contract**: A legally enforceable agreement. [7.4]
- **Corporation**: A business that the law treats as a "person" separate from its owners. It can own property, sue, and be sued. Owners' losses are usually limited to what they invested. [5.3, 7.4]
- **Coup**: The sudden, illegal seizure of government power, usually by the military. [6.7]
- **Culture**: The learned, shared habits, beliefs, and practices of a group. [14.1]

### D–G
- **Democracy**: Government in which citizens hold ultimate power, usually by electing representatives in free, competitive elections. [6.3]
- **Demographic transition**: The shift societies go through as they develop, from high birth and death rates to low ones. [8.5]
- **Deterrence**: Preventing an attack by threatening a costly response. [6.6, 16.4]
- **DNA**: The molecule that carries the hereditary instructions of living things. [1.2]
- **Ecosystem**: A community of living things and their physical environment, interacting as a system. [2.6]
- **Election**: A formal process in which people vote to choose officials or decide questions. [6.3]
- **Empire**: A large state that rules over many peoples and territories, usually acquired by conquest. [4.3]
- **Ethics**: The study of right and wrong, and of how to live. [11.3]
- **Ethnicity**: Group identity based on shared ancestry, language, culture, or history (real or perceived). [8.3]
- **Evolution**: Change in the inherited traits of populations over generations. Natural selection is its main driver of adaptation. [1.1]
- **Externality**: A cost or benefit of an activity that falls on people not involved in it, e.g., pollution. [5.1]
- **Fascism**: A far-right, authoritarian nationalist ideology that glorifies the nation and the leader, suppresses opposition, and celebrates violence. [9.7]
- **Feudalism**: A system in which land was granted in exchange for loyalty and service, creating a hierarchy of lords and dependent peasants. [4.4]
- **Fossil fuel**: Coal, oil, or natural gas, made from ancient organisms. Burning them releases energy and carbon dioxide. [12.3]
- **GDP (Gross Domestic Product)**: The total market value of all final goods and services produced in a country in a year. It is the standard, though imperfect, measure of economic size. [5.4]
- **Gene**: A segment of DNA that carries instructions for a trait or function. [1.2]
- **Genocide**: Acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group. [6.6, 7.5]
- **Gender**: The social roles, behaviors, and identities a society associates with being male, female, or otherwise. It is related to biological sex but not identical to it. [8.2]
- **Globalization**: The increasing connection of the world's economies, cultures, and peoples through trade, travel, and communication. [4.7, 5.7]
- **God / deity**: In religious belief, a supernatural being with power over nature or human fate. [10.1]
- **Greenhouse effect**: The warming of a planet's surface by gases (such as CO₂) that let sunlight in but trap outgoing heat. [2.5]

### H–M
- **Human rights**: Rights claimed for every human simply because they are human. They are written into international law since 1948. [7.5]
- **Hypothesis**: A testable proposed explanation. [3.3]
- **Ideology**: A connected set of beliefs about how society works and should work. [9.1]
- **Inequality**: Unequal distribution of income, wealth, or opportunity. [5.5]
- **Inflation**: A general rise in prices, which means money buys less. [5.2]
- **Interest**: The price paid for borrowing money, usually a yearly percentage. [5.2]
- **Law**: Rules made and enforced by a governing authority. [7.1]
- **Legitimacy**: The general acceptance that a ruler or institution has the right to rule. [6.1]
- **Liberalism**: A political tradition centered on individual rights, limited government, and the rule of law. (In US speech, "liberal" usually means center-left.) [9.2]
- **Market**: Any arrangement in which buyers and sellers exchange goods, with prices set by their interaction. [5.1]
- **Marriage**: A socially or legally recognized union, typically between two people, that sets rights and duties toward each other and any children. [8.1]
- **Migration**: People moving from one place to live in another. [8.5]
- **Money**: Anything widely accepted in exchange for goods, used to measure value and to store it. Modern money is mostly government-issued currency and bank records. [5.2]
- **Monotheism / Polytheism**: Belief in one god / belief in many gods. [10.1]
- **Myth**: A traditional story, often sacred, that explains origins or conveys values. [14.2]

### N–R
- **Nation**: A large group of people who see themselves as sharing a common identity, often including language, history, or ancestry. It is distinct from a *state*. [9.6]
- **Nation-state**: A state whose population largely shares one national identity. [6.1]
- **Natural selection**: The process by which inherited traits that improve survival and reproduction become more common. [1.1]
- **Norm**: An unwritten rule of expected behavior within a group. [14.1]
- **Nuclear weapon**: A bomb that releases energy by splitting (fission) or fusing (fusion) atomic nuclei. One can destroy a city. [12.8, 16.4]
- **Pandemic**: An outbreak of infectious disease spreading across many countries. [16.3]
- **Peer review**: Experts checking a scientific study before it is published. [3.3]
- **Political party**: An organization that competes to win government power. [6.3]
- **Populism**: Politics that frames society as a "pure people" against a "corrupt elite." [9.8]
- **Poverty, extreme**: Living below an international threshold, currently about US$3 per person per day (adjusted for local prices). [5.4]
- **Price**: The amount of money for which something is exchanged. [5.1]
- **Productivity**: Output per unit of input, e.g., goods produced per hour of work. It is the main long-run driver of living standards. [5.4]
- **Propaganda**: Information, often biased or misleading, spread to promote a political cause. [13.5]
- **Property**: Legally recognized rights to use, control, and transfer things. [7.4]
- **Race**: A social category grouping people by perceived physical traits and ancestry. It corresponds only loosely to biological genetic variation. [8.3]
- **Recession**: A significant, widespread decline in economic activity, often defined as two consecutive quarters of falling GDP. [5.8]
- **Refugee**: A person forced to flee their country because of persecution, war, or violence. [8.5]
- **Religion**: A system of beliefs and practices concerning the sacred or supernatural, usually shared by a community. [10.1]
- **Revolution**: A rapid, fundamental change in political power or social organization, often by force. [6.7]
- **Rule of law**: The principle that everyone, including rulers, is bound by publicly known laws applied equally. [7.2]

### S–Z
- **Science**: A systematic method of learning about the world by testing ideas against observation and experiment. [3.3]
- **Scripture**: Texts a religion treats as sacred or authoritative. [10.1]
- **Secularism**: Separating religion from government, or the decline of religion's role in society. [10.6]
- **Slavery**: A system in which people are owned as property and forced to work. It is now illegal everywhere, but forced labor persists. [4.5, 8.3]
- **Socialism**: A family of ideologies favoring collective or public ownership or control of major means of production, or strong redistribution. [9.4]
- **Sovereignty**: Supreme authority over a territory, free from outside control. [6.1]
- **Species**: A group of organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring. [2.6]
- **State**: An organization that claims the sole right to use legitimate force within a territory. [6.1]
- **Statistics**: Methods for collecting and analyzing data, and for measuring uncertainty. [3.4]
- **Stock (share)**: A unit of ownership in a corporation. [5.2]
- **Supply and demand**: The interaction between how much of something sellers offer and how much buyers want, which sets prices in markets. [5.1]
- **Tariff**: A tax on imported goods. [5.7]
- **Tax**: Money a government requires people and businesses to pay. [5.6]
- **Terrorism**: Violence against civilians to achieve political goals through fear. [6.6]
- **Theocracy**: Government by religious leaders or according to religious law. [6.2]
- **Theory (scientific)**: A well-tested, broadly supported explanation of some aspect of nature. It is *not* a guess. [3.3]
- **Totalitarianism**: A form of rule in which the state seeks to control nearly every part of public and private life. [9.7]
- **Treaty**: A formal written agreement between states. [7.5]
- **Union (labor)**: An organization of workers that bargains with employers as a group. [5.3]
- **Urbanization**: The shift of population from rural areas to cities. [8.6]
- **Vaccine**: A preparation that trains the immune system to resist a disease. [1.6]
- **Welfare state**: A government that provides broad social insurance and services, such as pensions, healthcare, and unemployment benefits. [5.6]

## Recommended Reading Path

An ordered route through humanity's own writing, from foundations to depth. Religious and ideological texts are listed **as cultural artifacts**: they are essential for understanding what humans believe, not endorsements of what they claim.

### Stage 1: Foundations: What humans are and how they know things
1. *A Short History of Nearly Everything* (Bill Bryson, 2003). A readable tour of science's main discoveries.
2. *On the Origin of Species* (Charles Darwin, 1859). The founding text of evolutionary biology.
3. *The Selfish Gene* (Richard Dawkins, 1976). Evolution explained at the level of genes. [Its framing is contested by some biologists.]
4. *The Demon-Haunted World* (Carl Sagan, 1995). How science works and how humans fool themselves.
5. *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (Daniel Kahneman, 2011). How human minds actually make decisions.

### Stage 2: The human story
6. *Sapiens* (Yuval Noah Harari, 2011). A broad, accessible overview. [Popular; some claims are contested by specialists.]
7. *Guns, Germs, and Steel* (Jared Diamond, 1997). Why civilizations developed unequally. [Influential, partly contested.]
8. *The Dawn of Everything* (David Graeber & David Wengrow, 2021). A challenge to standard accounts of early societies. [Contested.]
9. *The Silk Roads* (Peter Frankopan, 2015). World history centered on Asia rather than Europe.
10. *Postwar* (Tony Judt, 2005) or *The Age of Extremes* (Eric Hobsbawm, 1994). The 20th century.

### Stage 3: Material life and power
11. *The Wealth of Nations* (Adam Smith, 1776). Markets, specialization, and trade.
12. *The Communist Manifesto* (Karl Marx & Friedrich Engels, 1848). The most influential critique of capitalism.
13. *The Road to Serfdom* (Friedrich Hayek, 1944). The most influential case against central planning.
14. *Why Nations Fail* (Daron Acemoglu & James Robinson, 2012). Institutions as the cause of wealth and poverty.
15. *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (Thomas Piketty, 2013). Long-run inequality. [Conclusions debated.]
16. *Development as Freedom* (Amartya Sen, 1999). What development means for human lives.
17. *The Prince* (Niccolò Machiavelli, 1532) and *Leviathan* (Thomas Hobbes, 1651). Power and why humans accept states.
18. *The Federalist Papers* (Hamilton, Madison, Jay, 1787–88). The design of a constitutional democracy.
19. *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (Hannah Arendt, 1951). How modern tyranny arose.
20. *The Universal Declaration of Human Rights* (1948). Humanity's most widely endorsed statement of shared values. It is short.

### Stage 4: Belief, meaning, and morality (as human phenomena)
21. *The Hebrew Bible* (selected: Genesis, Exodus) and *The New Testament* (the Gospels). Sacred to Jews and Christians.
22. *The Quran*. Sacred to Muslims. Read with a historical introduction.
23. *The Bhagavad Gita*. A central Hindu text.
24. *The Dhammapada*. Core early Buddhist teachings.
25. *The Analects* (Confucius) and *Tao Te Ching* (attributed to Laozi). Foundations of Chinese thought.
26. *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (William James, 1902). A psychological study of religious experience.
27. *Religion Explained* (Pascal Boyer, 2001). A cognitive-science account of why humans believe. [Refining/Contested.]
28. *Nicomachean Ethics* (Aristotle) and *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (Immanuel Kant). Two foundations of moral philosophy.
29. *On Liberty* and *Utilitarianism* (John Stuart Mill, 1859, 1863). Liberty, and the greatest good.
30. *A Theory of Justice* (John Rawls, 1971). The most influential modern theory of fairness.

### Stage 5: Culture and the present
31. *The Epic of Gilgamesh*. Among the oldest surviving stories.
32. *The Iliad* (Homer) and *The Ramayana*. Founding epics of two major traditions.
33. *Things Fall Apart* (Chinua Achebe, 1958). Colonialism seen from inside an African society.
34. *Nineteen Eighty-Four* (George Orwell, 1949). Fiction that shaped how humans think about surveillance and propaganda.
35. *Silent Spring* (Rachel Carson, 1962). The start of modern environmentalism.
36. *Factfulness* (Hans Rosling, 2018). How much the world has improved, and how humans misjudge it.
37. The *IPCC Sixth Assessment Report: Summary for Policymakers* (2021–2023). The scientific consensus on climate change.
38. *The Precipice* (Toby Ord, 2020). Risks to humanity's long-term survival. [Probability estimates contested.]
39. *Energy and Civilization* (Vaclav Smil, 2017). How energy shapes every society.
40. *The Better Angels of Our Nature* (Steven Pinker, 2011). The argument that violence has declined. [Influential and contested.]

## A Note on Bias

No account of humanity is neutral, including this one. A careful reader should know where the distortions are likely to be.

1. **The written record is lopsided.** Most of human history went unrecorded. Of what was recorded, most was written by elites (rulers, priests, and later the educated and wealthy), and mostly by men. The lives of peasants, enslaved people, women, children, and societies without writing are known mostly indirectly, through archaeology, oral tradition, or the writings of outsiders who were often hostile.

2. **Victors and empires wrote more.** Conquerors and colonial powers produced and preserved more documents than the peoples they conquered. Many indigenous archives were destroyed (for example, most Maya books were burned by Spanish missionaries in the 1500s).

3. **Modern scholarship is concentrated.** Most academic research, especially in economics, psychology, and political science, comes from universities in North America and Europe. Psychology in particular has relied heavily on study participants from what researchers call "WEIRD" societies (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic). These may not represent humans in general.

4. **The data are uneven.** Statistics on population, income, health, and crime are much more reliable for rich countries than for poor ones or for countries whose governments restrict information. Global figures in this guide are estimates, and some (such as religious affiliation, extreme poverty, or war deaths) have wide error margins.

5. **Language and framing carry assumptions.** This guide is written in English. That language, and the scholarly traditions behind it, shape what counts as a "field," what counts as "religion" versus "philosophy" versus "culture," and where the "left–right" line falls. Other traditions draw these boundaries differently. For example, many languages have no word that maps neatly onto "religion."

6. **Selection is judgment.** Deciding which events, books, and people to include is itself a value-laden choice. Giving more space to economics and politics, as this guide does on purpose, reflects a judgment about what shapes human lives most. It is a defensible judgment, but still a judgment.

7. **The present is hardest to see.** Events of the last few decades have not yet been fully studied, and their importance is hard to judge. Claims about current trends, such as the effects of social media, the direction of democracy, or the impact of artificial intelligence, are the most likely to be revised.

8. **"Neutral" is a stance too.** Treating every view evenly can falsely suggest that a fringe view is as well supported as a consensus. This guide tries to avoid that by labeling claims according to the evidence. Readers should still keep in mind that deciding what counts as "evidence," and whose expertise to trust, are themselves positions humans argue about.

The best defense against these biases is the one humans have slowly developed: **compare sources, check claims against evidence, and stay open to being wrong.**
