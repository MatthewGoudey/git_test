# A Field Guide to Humanity

### A briefing on the human species and what it knows, written for a reader from elsewhere

*Figures describe the mid-2020s unless otherwise stated. They are rounded, because exact values change every year and because rounding is more honest about how well they are known.*

---

## Preface: To the Reader

You are about to read a description of **humans**, the dominant species on a small rocky planet they call **Earth**, the third planet orbiting an ordinary star they call **the Sun**.

Humans are large-brained, two-legged, social animals. There are about 8.2 billion of them alive today. They are not the strongest or fastest animals on their planet. Their advantage is that they **cooperate in very large groups** and **pass knowledge between generations** through language and, for the last ~5,000 years, through writing. Because of this, knowledge piles up. One human can use ideas that millions of earlier humans worked out. That process has given humans control of fire, farming, cities, machines, electricity, medicine, computers, and weapons able to destroy their own civilization.

Humans also organize their lives around things that exist only because they **collectively agree that they exist**: money, nations, laws, companies, borders. These are not illusions. They have very real effects on who eats, who fights, and who lives. But they are human-made, and they can change. Understanding this is the single most useful key to understanding human society.

Finally, humans hold many **beliefs** that cannot be tested: beliefs about gods, souls, the afterlife, and the meaning of existence. These beliefs are among the most powerful forces in human history, and this guide treats them seriously **as human phenomena**. It does not present them as facts about the universe.

### How to read the claim labels

Throughout this guide, important claims are marked or phrased by how well-supported they are:

| Label | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **[Established]** | Strong evidence and broad expert agreement | Earth orbits the Sun. Smallpox was eradicated in 1980. |
| **[Refining]** | Well supported, but details are still being studied | Exactly how most cancers begin and spread. |
| **[Contested]** | Experts disagree, or evidence is weak or mixed | The long-run effects of a particular minimum wage level. |
| **[Belief/Value]** | A claim about the supernatural or about what *ought* to be. It cannot be settled by evidence | "God exists." "Equality matters more than liberty." |

Where no label appears, the statement should be read as [Established].

3. **The Explainers**: a short briefing on each field (in the `explainers/` folder).

## Humans at a Glance

| Category | Fact (mid-2020s, approximate) |
|---|---|
| **Species** | *Homo sapiens*, a primate. Anatomically modern humans appeared in Africa roughly 300,000 years ago. All other human species (e.g., Neanderthals) are extinct. |
| **Population** | About 8.2–8.3 billion. It was ~1 billion in 1800 and ~2.5 billion in 1950. Growth is slowing; the UN projects a peak around 10.3 billion in the 2080s [Refining]. |
| **Lifespan** | Average life expectancy at birth is ~73 years worldwide. It ranges from ~55 in some countries to ~85 in others. Around 1800 it was ~30, mostly because so many children died. |
| **Where they live** | On every continent except Antarctica (which has only research stations). ~58% live in cities. About 60% live in Asia, ~18% in Africa, ~9% in Europe, ~8% in Latin America, ~5% in North America. |
| **How they are governed** | Divided into ~195 sovereign states (193 are members of the United Nations). Most of the population lives under governments that do not hold fully free and fair elections [Refining; depends on how "democracy" is measured]. |
| **How they get food** | Almost entirely through farming and herding (agriculture), plus fishing. Roughly a quarter of human workers are farmers. Roughly 670–730 million people are chronically undernourished. |
| **How they get energy** | ~80% from burning fossil fuels (oil, coal, natural gas). The rest comes from hydropower, nuclear, biomass, wind, and solar. Solar and wind are the fastest-growing sources. |
| **Economy** | Total yearly output (world GDP) is roughly US$110–120 trillion. Very unequal: average income per person in the richest countries is over 50 times that in the poorest. About 1 in 10 humans lives in extreme poverty (under ~$3/day). |
| **Languages** | About 7,000. The most widely spoken (including second-language speakers) are English, Mandarin Chinese, Hindi, Spanish, Arabic, and French. |
| **Literacy and information** | ~87% of adults can read. About 6 billion (~70%) use the internet. |
| **Religion** | Roughly 2.3 billion Christians, 2 billion Muslims, 1.9 billion religiously unaffiliated, 1.2 billion Hindus, 0.3–0.5 billion Buddhists, plus many smaller groups (Pew Research estimates for 2020). |
| **Technology** | Spacefaring for ~70 years; first artificial satellite 1957, first human on the Moon 1969. They have built AI systems that can produce human-like language. |
| **Weapons** | About 12,000 nuclear warheads, held by 9 states. Enough to kill a large fraction of the species. |
| **State of the planet** | Human activity has warmed Earth by roughly 1.3–1.5 °C since the late 1800s, mainly by releasing carbon dioxide [Established]. Wild animal populations and many species are in steep decline. |
| **Timeline** | Earth formed ~4.5 billion years ago. Life appeared ~3.5–4 billion years ago. *Homo sapiens* ~300,000 years ago. Farming ~12,000 years ago. Writing ~5,200 years ago. The industrial era ~250 years ago. The internet ~35 years ago (in wide public use). |

### Domain 1: The Human Organism

**Why it matters:** Humans are animals. Their needs (food, water, warmth, sleep, safety, sex, social contact) and their limits (disease, aging, death) set the frame for everything else they do.

| Field | Key topics |
|---|---|
| **1.1 Evolution and Human Origins** | Natural selection; primate relatives; hominin species; out-of-Africa migration; interbreeding with Neanderthals and Denisovans |
| **1.2 Genetics and Heredity** | DNA, genes, the genome; inheritance; mutation; human genetic diversity; genetic engineering |
| **1.3 The Body and Its Needs** | Organ systems; metabolism and nutrition; sleep; the senses; physical limits |
| **1.4 The Brain and Nervous System** | Neurons; brain regions; neuroplasticity; consciousness (largely unexplained); drugs and the brain |
| **1.5 Sex, Reproduction, and the Life Cycle** | Two sexes and intersex variation; pregnancy and birth; childhood dependency; puberty; aging; death |
| **1.6 Health, Disease, and Medicine** | Germ theory; infectious vs. chronic disease; vaccines; antibiotics; leading causes of death; modern and traditional medicine |

- **Key concepts:** natural selection, gene, DNA, mutation, cell, organ, metabolism, immune system, neuron, pathogen, vaccine, antibiotic resistance, life expectancy, infant mortality.
- **Landmark works:** *On the Origin of Species* (Charles Darwin, 1859); *Experiments on Plant Hybridization* (Gregor Mendel, 1866); the DNA double-helix paper (Watson & Crick, using Rosalind Franklin's data, 1953); the Human Genome Project (completed 2003); *Gray's Anatomy* (1858).
- **Major figures:** Charles Darwin, Gregor Mendel, Louis Pasteur, Robert Koch, Edward Jenner, Alexander Fleming, Rosalind Franklin.
- **Connections:** Health ↔ Economics (healthy workers produce more; healthcare is ~10% of world GDP). Reproduction ↔ Society (family structures, gender roles). Evolution ↔ Religion (conflict with literal creation accounts).

### Domain 12: Technology and Infrastructure

**Why it matters:** Technology is how humans turn knowledge into power over their environment. It explains why today's humans live roughly twice as long, and are many times richer, than their ancestors of 1800. It is also what gives them the power to cause global harm.

| Field | Key topics |
|---|---|
| **12.1 Tools and How Technology Changes** | Stone tools to machines; inventions vs. diffusion; general-purpose technologies; why innovation sped up |
| **12.2 Agriculture and Food Systems** | Domestication of plants and animals; the Green Revolution; fertilizer; modern farming; food supply chains; famine |
| **12.3 Energy** | Muscle, wood, and water power; coal and the steam engine; oil and gas; electricity; nuclear; renewables; the energy transition |
| **12.4 Manufacturing and Materials** | Metals; mass production; factories and assembly lines; plastics; semiconductors; global supply chains |
| **12.5 Transportation** | Wheels, ships, railways, cars, aircraft; shipping containers; spaceflight |
| **12.6 Computing, the Internet, and Artificial Intelligence** | Computers; transistors and chips; the internet and the World Wide Web; smartphones; data; machine learning and AI |
| **12.7 Public Health and Medical Technology** | Sanitation and clean water; vaccines; antibiotics; hospitals and health systems; imaging; pharmaceuticals |
| **12.8 Weapons and Military Technology** | From spears to gunpowder to nuclear weapons; industrial warfare; drones; cyberweapons |

- **Key concepts:** invention, innovation, diffusion, productivity, energy density, fossil fuel, electricity grid, renewable energy, mass production, semiconductor, algorithm, internet, machine learning, sanitation, nuclear fission.
- **Landmark works and documents:** *The Rise and Fall of American Growth* (Robert Gordon, 2016); "On Computable Numbers" (Alan Turing, 1936); "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" (Claude Shannon, 1948); *Energy and Civilization* (Vaclav Smil, 2017); *The Box* (Marc Levinson, 2006); "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017, the basis of current AI language models).
- **Major figures:** James Watt, Thomas Edison, Nikola Tesla, the Wright brothers, Fritz Haber, Norman Borlaug, Alan Turing, John von Neumann, Tim Berners-Lee.
- **Connections:** Technology ↔ Economics (growth). Technology ↔ Politics (military power, surveillance). Technology ↔ Climate (emissions). Technology ↔ Information (the internet).

### Domain 13: Language, Communication, and Information

**Why it matters:** Language is the human species' defining tool. It lets humans share knowledge, coordinate, persuade, and deceive. Whoever controls information has a great deal of power.

| Field | Key topics |
|---|---|
| **13.1 Language** | What language is; grammar; language acquisition in children; language families; dying languages |
| **13.2 Writing and Literacy** | Invention of writing; alphabets, syllabaries, logographs; printing; the spread of literacy |
| **13.3 Media and Mass Communication** | Newspapers, radio, television; journalism; media ownership; press freedom |
| **13.4 The Internet and Social Media** | Search engines; social platforms; algorithms and attention; effects on politics and mental health [Contested] |
| **13.5 Propaganda, Persuasion, and Misinformation** | Advertising; state propaganda; disinformation campaigns; censorship; fact-checking |

- **Key concepts:** grammar, phoneme, dialect, language family, script, literacy, printing press, mass media, journalism, censorship, propaganda, disinformation, algorithm, virality, echo chamber.
- **Landmark works:** *Course in General Linguistics* (Ferdinand de Saussure, 1916); *Syntactic Structures* (Noam Chomsky, 1957); *The Gutenberg Galaxy* (Marshall McLuhan, 1962); *Public Opinion* (Walter Lippmann, 1922); *Propaganda* (Edward Bernays, 1928); *Manufacturing Consent* (Herman & Chomsky, 1988); *Nineteen Eighty-Four* (George Orwell, 1949, fiction).
- **Connections:** Information ↔ Politics (elections, propaganda). Information ↔ Economics (advertising and the attention economy). Information ↔ Methods of Knowing (misinformation).

### Domain 14: Culture and the Arts

**Why it matters:** Culture is the shared set of habits, stories, tastes, and meanings that make one human group different from another. The arts are where humans express emotion, identity, and ideas. They are also large industries.

| Field | Key topics |
|---|---|
| **14.1 What Culture Is** | Norms, customs, and values; cultural transmission; cultural variation; globalization of culture |
| **14.2 Storytelling and Literature** | Myth and oral tradition; epic poetry; the novel; major literary traditions |
| **14.3 Music** | Universality of music; scales and rhythm; traditions; recorded music; the music industry |
| **14.4 Visual Arts and Architecture** | Cave paintings to digital art; major styles; monumental architecture; art markets |
| **14.5 Performance, Film, and Games** | Theater; dance; cinema; television; video games |
| **14.6 Sport and Play** | Organized sports; the Olympics; sport and national identity; sport as an industry |
| **14.7 Food and Everyday Customs** | Cuisines; taboos; dress; greetings; holidays; time-keeping and calendars |

- **Key concepts:** culture, norm, custom, tradition, myth, genre, aesthetics, canon, popular culture, cultural appropriation, soft power, taboo.
- **Landmark works:** *Epic of Gilgamesh* (~2100–1200 BCE); *Iliad* and *Odyssey* (attributed to Homer, ~8th c. BCE); *Mahabharata* and *Ramayana*; *The Tale of Genji* (Murasaki Shikibu, ~1010); *One Thousand and One Nights*; *Divine Comedy* (Dante, ~1320); Shakespeare's plays (~1590–1613); *Don Quixote* (Cervantes, 1605/1615); *Dream of the Red Chamber* (Cao Xueqin, 18th c.); *Things Fall Apart* (Chinua Achebe, 1958); *One Hundred Years of Solitude* (Gabriel García Márquez, 1967). In anthropology: *Patterns of Culture* (Ruth Benedict, 1934); *The Interpretation of Cultures* (Clifford Geertz, 1973).
- **Connections:** Culture ↔ Religion (sacred art, festivals). Culture ↔ Economics (entertainment industries). Culture ↔ Politics (national identity, censorship).

### Domain 15: Mind, Psychology, and Behavior

**Why it matters:** Human societies are the sum of individual human minds, and those minds have systematic quirks. Knowing how humans actually think explains much of their politics, economics, and conflict.

| Field | Key topics |
|---|---|
| **15.1 Cognition and Perception** | Memory; attention; perception and its illusions; intelligence; learning |
| **15.2 Emotion and Motivation** | Basic emotions; stress; reward and pleasure; needs and goals |
| **15.3 Cognitive Biases and Decision-Making** | Heuristics; confirmation bias; loss aversion; overconfidence; behavioral economics |
| **15.4 Personality and Development** | Child development; attachment; the "Big Five" personality traits; nature vs. nurture |
| **15.5 Mental Health and Illness** | Depression, anxiety, schizophrenia, addiction; prevalence; treatments; stigma |
| **15.6 Cooperation, Conflict, and Group Behavior** | Altruism; reciprocity; in-groups and out-groups; conformity; obedience to authority; prejudice |

- **Key concepts:** cognition, memory, heuristic, bias, emotion, motivation, reinforcement, personality, attachment, intelligence, mental disorder, conformity, obedience, in-group, empathy.
- **Landmark works:** *The Principles of Psychology* (William James, 1890); *The Interpretation of Dreams* (Sigmund Freud, 1899, historically influential, largely unsupported) [Contested]; Pavlov's conditioning studies (1890s–1900s); Milgram's obedience experiments (1963); Asch's conformity experiments (1951); *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (Kahneman, 2011); *The Better Angels of Our Nature* (Steven Pinker, 2011) [Contested in parts]; the *DSM* (diagnostic manual, current edition DSM-5-TR, 2022).
- **Connections:** Mind ↔ Economics (behavioral economics). Mind ↔ Politics (polarization, persuasion). Mind ↔ Religion (religious experience, belief formation).

**Why it matters:** These are the problems on which humanity's medium-term future depends. Most are global and cannot be solved by any one country. They test whether humans can cooperate at the scale of the whole species.

| Field | Key topics |
|---|---|

| **16.2 Biodiversity and Environmental Degradation** | Habitat loss; extinctions; pollution; oceans; deforestation; conservation |
| **16.3 Pandemics and Global Health** | COVID-19; emerging diseases; antimicrobial resistance; global health inequality |
| **16.4 Nuclear Weapons and Catastrophic Risks** | Arsenals; arms control; proliferation; nuclear winter; biological and other catastrophic risks |
| **16.5 Artificial Intelligence** | Capabilities; economic effects; risks and safety; governance |
| **16.6 Demographic Change and Aging** | Falling birth rates; aging populations; population growth in Africa; migration pressures |
| **16.7 Resources: Food, Water, and Materials** | Food security; water stress; critical minerals; soil; the limits debate |

- **Key concepts:** emissions, net zero, tipping point, adaptation, mitigation, extinction rate, pandemic preparedness, antimicrobial resistance, deterrence, arms control, existential risk, AI alignment, dependency ratio, sustainability.

- **Connections:** Global challenges ↔ every domain, but especially Economics, Politics, Technology, and Science.
